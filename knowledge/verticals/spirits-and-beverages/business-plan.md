# Spirits and beverages from farm-grown grain

Three routes are compared for turning this farm's wheat, grain sorghum (milo) and corn —
and possibly rye or a heritage wheat — into alcoholic beverages:

| Route | What the farm does | License needed | Capital | Time to first revenue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Contract-grow** specialty grain for Kansas craft distilleries, brewers and maltsters | grows, cleans, stores and delivers identity-preserved grain to spec | none (grain sale) | ~$0 (cleaning/storage already owned or custom) | next harvest |
| **B. Contract distilling (co-pack) under a farm brand** | supplies grain; a licensed Kansas DSP mashes, distills, ages and bottles under the farm's label; a Kansas distributor sells it | brand-owner LLC only; the DSP holds TTB and Kansas licenses | ~$25-55k | 6-9 months for unaged vodka/white whiskey; 2-4 years for straight whiskey |
| **C. Own farm distillery** (Kansas microdistillery license + federal DSP), tasting room | everything, on or near the farm | TTB DSP permit, Kansas microdistillery license, drinking-establishment status for a tasting room, county conditional use | ~$380k-1.0M | 9-15 months to first unaged sales after permits; 3-5 years to a mature aged portfolio |

**Recommendation (summary):** pursue Route A now at zero capital; run Route B as the brand
test only if a Kansas DSP will co-pack a small run on acceptable terms and the trustee
signs off on the LLC structure; treat Route C as a 2029+ decision gated on Route B
selling at least 6,000 bottles a year at a net $20 or better for two consecutive years,
on a confirmed non-trust or trustee-approved site, with a named head distiller. The
national craft-spirits market has contracted three years running and aged-whiskey
inventory is in glut (ACSA 2026; MGP and Beam pauses), which is exactly the wrong time
to borrow against a still. Beer/malt is a side door: sell wheat to the two Kansas
maltsters and to Wichita brewers rather than building a malthouse.

## 1. Concept
Kansas is the top hard red winter wheat state and Sedgwick County sits in the middle of
a 650,000-person metro with one working distillery (Wheat State Distilling) and a dozen
breweries. Spirits turn a $6.45 bushel of wheat into roughly 16 bottles of 80-proof
spirit whose wholesale value is $20 or more each — the largest value multiplier of any
vertical this farm has examined (grain value about $0.40 per bottle). The multiplier is
real; the question is who captures it. Distilling is capital-heavy, three-tier
distribution takes half the retail price, excise and state taxes take another $1 a
bottle, aged whiskey locks cash up for two to four years, and the market is shrinking.
The farm's genuine advantages are (1) provenance — a single-estate, traceable Kansas
grain story that Boot Hill (Dodge City) has already shown sells, (2) sorghum, which few
distillers use and which gives a gluten-free-marketable "Kansas milo whiskey" or
sorghum vodka that no Wichita brand owns, and (3) owned grain, cleaning and storage, so
the grain itself costs the farm only its elevator price.

The farm has no dedicated labor today and the land is in a family trust. Both facts
push the plan toward Routes A and B, which need no building, no still, no employee and
no alcohol-manufacturing activity on trust land.

## 3. Market
**Size within reach (estimate, verify):** US spirits sales are roughly 300 million 9-liter
cases a year; craft producers sold 11.7 million cases in 2025 (ACSA 2026), about 4% of
volume. Scaled by population, the Wichita metro (650k) drinks roughly 7 million 750 ml
bottles of spirits a year, of which ~275,000 are craft; all of Kansas (2.9M people)
about 1.2 million craft bottles. A new Kansas farm brand that reaches 1% of the metro
craft pool sells ~2,800 bottles; 2% of the state craft pool is ~24,000 bottles. The
Route B base case sells 1,400 bottles in year 1 and 7,200 in year 5; Route C base sells
4,900 and 22,500. The Route C figure is ambitious for a new brand and is the reason that
route's favorable case must not size debt.

**Trend:** three straight years of decline. Active craft distillers 2,131 in August 2026,
down 6.6%; closures exceeded openings for the first time since the data project began
(2016); craft volume -8.0% and value -3.7% in 2025; distributors "narrowed their
portfolios to work through excess inventory" (ACSA/Park Street 2026). Aged-whiskey
oversupply: MGP idled two Kentucky distilleries for at least 12 months from May 2026 and
took a $153M inventory impairment; Jim Beam paused its main Clermont distillery from
January 2026. Bulk aged bourbon and new-make are cheap and plentiful, which (a) caps
what sourced-whiskey brands can charge and (b) makes a contract distiller with spare
capacity easier to find and cheaper to hire — good for Route B, bad for Route C.

**Pricing benchmarks (Kansas craft, 750 ml, retail, accessed 2026-11-16):** $37-50 for
a standard craft bourbon or wheat whiskey; $55-85 for reserve releases; white whiskey
and vodka in the high $20s to mid $30s. Three-tier arithmetic used in the model: retail
$40 → retailer margin ~30% → distributor sells at ~$28-31 → distributor margin ~25-30%
→ supplier (farm brand) nets ~$20-23 FOB the distiller. Kansas distributors hold
exclusive-territory franchise agreements and require 45 days' notice of price changes
(K.S.A. 41-410 series; verify). Named Kansas spirits wholesalers to approach: Standard
Beverage, LDF Sales & Distributing, Southern Glazer's of Kansas (verify current
portfolios and whether they take sub-1,000-case brands; many now do not).

**Channel strategy:** Route A — direct relationships with 3-6 distilleries and
maltsters; sample lots with lab sheets; letters of intent before planting (see
`knowledge/markets/specialty-buyer-network.md`). Route B — one Kansas distributor, Wichita
retail liquor stores (Kansas allows tastings at retailers with ABC notice — verify) and
Wichita restaurants/bars; no direct-to-consumer (Kansas prohibits spirits DTC
shipping and a brand owner without a DSP cannot sell to consumers). Route C — adds the
tasting room (highest margin channel, ~30-40% of volume at farm-distillery peers) and
on-premise cocktails under a drinking-establishment license.

**Beer and malt route:** Wichita has 10+ breweries (Wichita Brewing Co., Hopping Gnome
— explicitly buy-local, Aero Plains — red-wheat Weizenbock, Central Standard, River
City, Third Place, Nortons). Brewers buy malt, not raw wheat; the farm sells raw wheat
either to a maltster (Great Plains Custom Grain, Ottawa, which has sold malted wheat to
Blind Tiger, Cinder Block and Double Shift but reports most of its malt comes from
Canada; Kansas Malt Company, Wilsey) or pays for toll-malting. Craft malt sells to
brewers at ~$0.99/lb vs ~$0.70 commodity (2022); maltsters pay farmers ~$0.25-0.30/lb
for malting barley (~$12-14/bu equivalent) and less for wheat. A farm micro-malthouse
(1-2 t batch, ~$150-400k, estimate) is not justified by a 1,000-acre wheat farm with no
labor; selling malting-grade wheat or growing a small barley trial for an existing
maltster is.

**How first accounts are won:** Route A — visit Boot Hill, Union Horse, Wheat State,
Bluff Creek, Smoky Valley and the El Dorado distilleries with 5-lb samples, a lab sheet
(protein, moisture, test weight, falling number, DON/aflatoxin) and a one-page offer;
ask what they pay now and what spec they would pay a premium for. Route B — a sorghum
or heritage-wheat story that a distributor's craft rep can sell to Wichita's 60-80
retail liquor stores; two anchor restaurants.

Specs distillers ask for (collected from buyer contracts and references; confirm with
each buyer): moisture <= 13.5% wheat, <= 15.5% corn (cannot mill above 18.5%); test
weight No. 1 or No. 2 grade; sound, no musty or sour odor; aflatoxin < 20 ppb and DON
< 1-2 ppm (distillers are strict because mycotoxins concentrate in spent grain fed to
cattle and some carry into distillate); low protein / high starch wheat preferred for
yield (hard wheat ferments viscous and foamy); non-GMO if the brand claims it; lot
traceability (field, variety, harvest date, bin); cleaned to remove screenings and
weed seed; sometimes malted or a specific variety (e.g. Turkey Red) for the story.
Identity preservation: one dedicated bin per lot; the farm has no bin inventory on
record (farm-profile.md) so Route A may need one 3,000-5,000 bu hopper bin (~$15-22k,
see wheat-flour model) or a buyer who takes grain at harvest. Off-spec grain goes to
the elevator at commodity price — the fallback is costless, which is the main virtue of
Route A.

Yield math used in the models: 1 proof gallon (PG) = 1 US gallon at 100 proof. Corn at
60% starch yields 5.0-5.2 PG/bu in industrial practice (EPA AP-42); wheat about 5.9
PG/bu theoretical (Reich 1933). Small pot-still craft plants realise roughly half to
two-thirds of theoretical after fermentation efficiency, heads/tails cuts and losses.
Base case 2.5 PG/bu bottled after ~8% aging loss; a 750 ml bottle at 80 proof is
0.1585 PG, so 1 PG = 6.31 bottles, 2.5 PG/bu = 15.8 bottles per bushel = **26 bottles per
100 lb** of wheat (adverse 21, favorable 32). One bushel of wheat ($6.45) becomes
~16 bottles with a wholesale value of ~$320.

## 5. Process and equipment
**Route A:** combine, clean (custom or owned air-screen cleaner), dedicated bin, grain
testing, deliver by gravity wagon or in 2,000-lb totes. Labor ~1 hour per 100 bushels
beyond normal harvest.

**Route B:** farm delivers grain to the DSP; the DSP mills, mashes, ferments, distills,
barrels (farm-owned barrels stored in the DSP's bonded warehouse), proofs, bottles,
labels and ships to the distributor; the farm owns the brand, labels (COLA filed by the
DSP), barrels and inventory. Candidate co-packers: any Kansas or near-Kansas DSP with
spare capacity — in 2026 many have it. MGP is the national bulk option (minimum one tote
or four barrels) but uses its own grain; the farm's grain requires a craft DSP willing to
run a custom mash bill, typically at $1,200-2,000 per still day (Woodworks fee
schedule, 2025) or $15-30 per proof gallon (estimate, verify). Timber Creek-type
private-label finished whiskey at $40/bottle shows the ceiling when the distiller
supplies everything.

Utilities: a 300-gal mash needs ~1,000 lb grain, 250 gal water, ~0.5-1 MMBTU steam,
and 6-8 hours of still time per stripping run; one mash a week = ~900 bu/yr = 20 acres
of wheat. Labor: a full-time head distiller plus ~6 bottles per labor hour across
mashing, barrel work and bottling. Automation (PLC mash control, semi-auto bottling) is
what moves variable labor from $3 toward $2.20 a bottle. A DSP cannot be in a dwelling
or on premises where other businesses operate without TTB approval (27 CFR 19.52;
verify), and must be a single described location under the Kansas license.

## 6. Regulatory and food safety
**Kansas (Kansas Department of Revenue, Alcoholic Beverage Control):**
- Microdistillery license, K.S.A. 41-354: manufacture and storage of not more than
  50,000 gallons of spirits per year; sale to licensed spirits distributors; sale on the
  licensed premises in original unopened containers to consumers for off-premises
  consumption (hours 6 a.m. to midnight in the amended text — verify); free samples on
  premises and at ABC-monitored special events; by-the-drink service when the premises
  also holds drinking-establishment authority (10% liquor drink tax applies); a
  separate packaging and warehousing facility license ($200) for off-site storage; one
  location per license. Fees: $500 per two-year license term, $30 new application, $20
  modernization fee, $10 renewal application (KDOR pricing table). Kansas has **no**
  microdistillery self-distribution to retailers — unlike microbreweries, which since
  HB 2124 (July 2024) may self-distribute up to 1,000 barrels. No bill granting
  distillers the same was found for 2025-26; re-check each session. No
  direct-to-consumer shipping of spirits. Kansas also has no "farm distillery" license
  class; the farm winery license (K.S.A. 41-316) is the nearest agricultural analog and
  does not cover spirits.
- Taxes: gallonage tax $2.50 per wine gallon of spirits (K.S.A. 41-501; ~$0.50 per 750 ml),
  remitted by the distributor on wholesale sales and by the microdistillery on its own
  retail sales (ABC-1041 monthly return; verify remitter); liquor enforcement tax 8% on
  off-premise sales to consumers (K.S.A. 79-4101); liquor drink tax 10% on on-premise
  drinks; monthly ABC-1041 production return even in zero months. Brand registration /
  price posting rules for suppliers: Park Street says no price posting but 45-day
  notice to distributors of price changes; Sovos says posting is required — verify with
  ABC licensing (kdor_abc.licensing@ks.gov).
- Local: Sedgwick County zoning conditional-use permit for manufacturing and a
  tasting room on agricultural land; state fire marshal plan review; boiler
  inspection; health department for any food service.

**Federal (TTB):**
- Distilled Spirits Plant: Form 5110.41 via Permits Online; no fee; 60-180 days (vendor
  guides say 4-6 months). Bond: since the PATH Act (2017) a DSP that reasonably expects
  excise liability of $50,000 or less a year and paid no more than that the prior year
  may claim the bond exemption (27 CFR 19.151(d), 19.73, 19.136 — verify threshold); at
  Route C scale (<=5,000 PG x $2.70 = <=$13,500) the exemption applies.
- Excise tax (CBMA, permanent since 2020): $2.70 per proof gallon on the first 100,000
  PG per calendar year for spirits distilled or processed (not merely bottled) at the
  plant; $13.34 on the next 22.13 million PG; $13.50 standard. Per 750 ml at 80 proof:
  $0.43 reduced, $2.14 standard. A Route B brand's spirits qualify at the co-packer's
  reduced rate only if the co-packer distils or processes them (ours are distilled
  there) and the co-packer's own volume stays under 100,000 PG; the tax is the
  co-packer's and is passed through in the fee.
- Labels: Certificate of Label Approval (COLA) per label, filed by the DSP, no fee;
  TTB median processing 4-5 days in 2026. Formula approval (Form 5100.51) is required
  before the COLA for flavored products, color additives, novel processes and most gins
  with non-standard botanicals; standards of identity (27 CFR 5): "straight" needs 2
  years in new charred oak and a state of distillation on the label if different from
  the bottler; "bourbon" >=51% corn; "wheat whiskey" >=51% wheat; sorghum-grain whiskey
  is "whiskey" (grain mash) and may be labeled by grain if >=51%.
- Operations: monthly reports (5110.40 production, 5110.11 storage, 5110.28 processing),
  excise returns (quarterly or annual if under thresholds), proof-and-fill records,
  bonded-inventory records; trade-practice rules (tied house, exclusive outlets).
- Other: FDA food facility registration is not required for a DSP's alcohol beverages
  (TTB-regulated) but the brand owner should carry product liability; Kansas requires
  servers over 21; dram-shop/liquor liability for the tasting room.

**Trust and structure (binding):** Under `knowledge/operations/family-trust-constraints.md`,
alcohol manufacturing and retail on trust land is a non-agricultural commercial use
that (1) requires a trustee resolution citing the trust power relied on, (2) raises
§2032A qualified-use and Kansas ag-use valuation questions for the building footprint,
(3) exposes trust assets to liquor and product liability unless the business sits in a
separate LLC with its own insurance and a commercial site lease at documented market
rent, and (4) may simply be prohibited by the trust instrument (unknown terms are
treated as prohibitions). Routes A and B keep every alcohol activity off trust land —
grain sale and a brand LLC — and are therefore trust-compatible with a one-page
trustee notice. Route C on trust land is a conditional item at best; a leased
commercial site in Sedgwick County avoids the land question but adds rent and removes
the "farm distillery" visitor experience.

## 7. Operations plan
- **Route A:** winter outreach (Nov-Feb) to distilleries and maltsters with samples and
  lab sheets; LOIs by March for spring crops and by August for wheat; one dedicated bin;
  harvest, clean, test each lot, deliver on the buyer's schedule (distillers take
  grain monthly, not at harvest — storage is part of the service). Owner labor only.
- **Route B:** Y1: brand and trademark; contract with one Kansas DSP (test mash in
  spring, first vodka/white whiskey run after wheat harvest, bottled by October;
  whiskey laid down in 10-20 barrels); distributor appointment; Wichita launch at
  retailers and 2-3 restaurants. Y2: repeat; first 1-year "young" release only if the
  spirit merits it. Y3: first straight wheat whiskey and sorghum whiskey releases; add
  a Kansas City or Oklahoma City distributor if Kansas depletions justify. Labor: owner
  ~12 h/week on sales plus 8 h/week from stage 2 — this is the real cost; the farm has no
  one to do it today.
- **Route C:** 12-18 months of permitting and construction before the first mash; hire a
  head distiller before equipment is ordered; one mash a week in stage 1, two from stage
  2; tasting room Thu-Sat; inventory: unaged for cash, 60% of distillate to barrel.

## 8. Financials (summary; detail in assumptions.csv, own-distillery/assumptions.csv and the two generated models)
**Model mapping (both CSVs):** `acres` = grain acres devoted; `yield_per_acre` in bu;
`yield_unit_lb` = 60; `conversion_pct` = 750 ml bottles per 100 lb grain (26 base =
2.5 PG/bu bottled x 6.31 bottles/PG / 1.667 bu per 100 lb); product "lb" = bottles;
`product_price_per_lb` = net per bottle to the farm after distributor and retailer
margins (Route B $20; Route C $25 blended with 30% tasting-room direct at ~$38);
`grain_price_per_lb` = elevator opportunity cost ($6.45/bu base); `operating_variable`
rows are per bottle produced (distilling fee, bottling, glass/closure/label/case,
federal excise $0.43, Kansas gallonage $0.50, barrel and aging storage, freight,
marketing); capital stage 1 = brand, legal, initial barrels, website/POS, working
capital (Route B) or the still, building and tasting room (Route C). The builder values
unsold output at zero, so the share of each year's distillate that is aging in barrels
is treated as a cost with no revenue — a deliberate stress on both brand routes; the
inventory-adjusted view is given below.

**Route A — contract-grow (simple table; no model needed):**
| Case | Premium over elevator cash ($/bu) | Less cleaning, IP storage, testing, hauling ($/bu) | Net ($/bu) | Contracted bushels (buyers x volume) | Acres at 45 bu | Net income per year |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Basis for the premium: distillers paid $9-12/bu for conventional grain delivered (Iowa
2018) when cash corn was ~$3.50, and $12.50-15/bu for rye (Iowa 2022-23) — premiums of
$2-8/bu for small, traceable, delivered lots; Kansas buyers with a farm-to-bottle story
already in place (Boot Hill) will pay less. The premium is "estimate, verify" until a
quote exists. Route A's economics are modest but have no capital, no license, no trust
issue and no downside beyond owner time; they also build the relationships Route B
needs. Sold as commodity instead, the same 100 acres earn $29,000 at $6.45 x 45 bu; the
premium is a 13% uplift on those acres.

**Comparison with grain:** 12 acres of wheat sold as grain = ~$3,500/yr; Route B base
turns that into $143,000 of year-5 revenue and a -$24,500 EBITDA; Route C base turns 40
acres ($11,600 of grain) into $562,000 of revenue and a +$15,000 EBITDA on $689,000 of
capital. The value multiplier is enormous and almost all of it is consumed by
distribution, tax, packaging, labor and aging.

## 10. Risks and mitigations
| Risk | Route | Likelihood / impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Market glut and three-year craft decline continue; distributors cut small brands | B, C | high / high | stay in Route A; keep B at pilot scale; do not build C before the ACSA count stabilises |
| Capital locked in aging inventory for 2-4 years; no lender takes it as collateral at this scale | B, C | certain / high | lead with unaged products; barrel no more than 60% of distillate; fund barrels from farm cash, not debt |
| Trust prohibits or trustee declines non-agricultural use; §2032A recapture or ag-use reassessment | C (and B's LLC) | unknown until the instrument is read / high | read the trust first; keep A and B off trust land; site C on leased commercial land if needed |
| Liability (liquor, product, premises) reaching farm or trust assets | B, C | medium / high | separate LLC, $1M/$2M product and liquor liability, named insured on co-packer policy, no trust-owned building |
| Key person: no labor today; a head distiller is a $55-65k hire and the brand needs 12-20 h/week of selling | B, C | high / high | B is the test of whether anyone in the family wants to sell spirits; C needs a named distiller before equipment is ordered |
| Regulatory: Kansas distributor franchise lock-in; no self-distribution; no DTC; TTB formula delays; fire code on a farm building | all | medium / medium | counsel reviews the distributor agreement; stock standards-of-identity products first; fire marshal pre-application meeting |
| Co-packer failure or closure mid-aging (common in 2025-26) | B | medium / high | farm owns barrels and spirits under a written bailment; TTB transfer-in-bond to another DSP is permitted; keep barrels in a DSP with a sound balance sheet |
| Grain quality: DON/aflatoxin year, low test weight, high protein hurting yield | all | medium / low for A, medium for B-C | test every lot; buyer spec in contract; fall back to elevator |
| Price realisation below $20 net | B, C | medium / high | the single most sensitive variable; set a $17 kill price |
| Mycotoxin or contaminant in spirit, recall | B, C | low / high | lot testing, co-packer QA, recall insurance |
| Sorghum spirit flavour or category confusion ("is it whiskey?") | B, C | medium / medium | pilot one barrel first; label plainly as "Kansas sorghum whiskey"; also offer a wheat flagship |

**Disqualifiers (any one stops the route):**
- No Kansas DSP will co-pack a 5-acre lot on terms at or below $5.50/bottle for
  distilling plus bottling, or requires the farm to buy spirit rather than supply grain
  (Route B).
- The trust instrument prohibits non-agricultural commercial use or the trustee declines
  (Route C on trust land; Route B only if the trustee objects to the family operating an
  alcohol LLC at all).
- No Kansas spirits distributor will list a sub-500-case brand (B, C).
- A named head distiller cannot be hired or a family member will not commit full time
  (C).
- Route B sells fewer than 1,200 bottles in its first 12 months or nets under $17 a
  bottle (stop B; C is off the table).
- Fire marshal or Sedgwick County requirements push Route C building cost above $450k
  for a new build or $250k for a retrofit.
- Two consecutive crop years fail distiller spec (A and B grain supply; buy-in grain
  destroys the single-estate story).

## 11. Decision rules
1. **Route A go now**: begin outreach this winter; plant contracted acres only against a
   signed LOI with price, spec and delivery schedule. Review each February.
2. **Route B go** only when all of: trustee has approved the brand LLC in writing;
   a co-packer quote and a distributor letter exist; the compliance officer has
   confirmed Kansas supplier registration requirements; total stage 1 commitment is
   under $55,000 cash with no debt. Kill at <1,200 bottles or <$17 net in 12 months.
3. **Route C go** only when all of: Route B has sold >=6,000 bottles/yr at >=$20 net
   for two consecutive years; a non-trust site or a written trustee approval with
   commercial lease and §2032A/ag-use opinion exists; a head distiller is hired or
   named; equity covers at least 50% of stage 1 capital without pledging land; ACSA
   data show craft distiller count and volume flat or rising for two years; a fire
   marshal pre-review has priced the building. Re-evaluate no earlier than the 2029
   planning cycle.
4. Favorable cases never size debt; adverse cases size the cash reserve.
5. Every bottle price, fee and yield in the CSVs is replaced by a quote before stage 1b
   money is spent.
