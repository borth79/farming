# Local produce plots

## 1. Concept
Set aside one to five acres of the best-watered, most accessible ground for high-value
produce sold directly to Wichita consumers, restaurants and grocers, where gross revenue
per acre is 10 to 50 times that of grain. The grain operation supplies land, tillage,
some equipment and a brand; the produce plot supplies cash flow in summer, a customer
list, and the retail face for the flour, popped sorghum, oil and birdseed verticals. The
constraint is labor and management attention, not land or market, so the plan starts
small, mechanizes what it can, and grows only with proven sales.

## 3. Market
- **Farmers markets**: Kansas Grown Farmers Market (west Wichita, Saturdays, April or May
  through October, indoor dates in winter) and Old Town Farm & Art Market (downtown,
  April through December). Vendor fees not found online; typically $20–40 per Saturday or
  a season fee (verify with each market). Expect $500–2,500 gross per market day for a
  full table in peak season (verify by attending and counting).
- **CSA subscriptions**: 20–60 shares at $400–700 per season paid in spring give
  pre-season cash and committed demand; requires 15–20 weeks of consistent variety.
- **Restaurants and grocers**: Wichita's chef-driven restaurants buy tomatoes, specialty
  peppers, herbs, sweet corn and flowers; Dillons and natural grocers run local programs
  with GAP or food-safety requirements. Fewer, larger drops than markets.
- **On-farm stand and u-pick**: viable if a field sits on a traveled road near Wichita;
  pumpkins in October are the classic draw.
- **Online pre-order with pickup**: a simple web store with Saturday pickup at the market
  or farm reduces waste and ties to the flour, popped sorghum and birdseed products.
- **Market size**: Wichita metro ~650,000 people; farmers markets draw a few thousand
  visitors on a peak Saturday (verify). A 3-acre mixed plot producing $30,000–60,000 of
  product is a small share of local produce spend and should sell if quality and
  presence are consistent.

## 4. Raw material and agronomy link
- Site: the best-drained, deepest soil with water available, near the road and the shop;
  the water-management agent's receiving zones on loam are candidates, ponding zones are
  not. Avoid fields with recent long-residual herbicides (atrazine, sulfonylureas,
  clopyralid) and check plant-back intervals for every vegetable.
- Water: produce needs 1–2 inches a week in July. A well with a domestic or stock
  water right may legally irrigate a garden-scale plot in Kansas below a threshold
  (verify with the Division of Water Resources); anything larger needs an irrigation
  water right. Drip irrigation with plastic mulch is standard for tomatoes, peppers and
  melons.
- Soil tests the same as grain fields, with attention to pH 6.2–6.8 and higher organic
  matter; compost and cover crops in rotation.
- Rotation: vegetables rotate within the plot by family (nightshade, cucurbit, allium,
  brassica, legume) on a 3–4 year cycle; the plot itself can rotate across the farm every
  few years to break disease.

## 5. Process and equipment
- Existing: tractor, tillage, row planter for sweet corn and pumpkins, sprayer with a
  dedicated clean tank or a separate small sprayer to avoid herbicide contamination.
- Add (stage 1, $8,000–25,000 total, verify): drip irrigation system and pump, plastic
  mulch layer (used), walk-behind or small tractor cultivation tools, harvest totes and
  wash station with potable water, shade and cold storage (used reach-in or CoolBot room,
  $3,000–8,000), market tent, tables, scales, point-of-sale.
- Stage 2: high tunnel (30 × 96 ft about $10,000–15,000; NRCS EQIP cost share often
  covers most of it), transplanter, bed shaper, small potato digger, delivery van shared
  with the flour vertical.
- Automation and labor saving: drip with timers, plastic mulch for weed control, precision
  seeder for direct-seeded crops, flame weeding, harvest conveyors for pumpkins, pre-order
  system to cut unsold product. Picking stays manual; budget 1,000–2,000 labor hours per
  acre per season for mixed vegetables (verify), far less for pumpkins and sweet corn.

## 6. Regulatory and food safety
- FSMA Produce Safety Rule: farms below the inflation-adjusted produce-sales cut-off
  (about $33,000 average over three years in 2025; verify 2026 figure) are not covered;
  farms under $500,000 inflation-adjusted with majority direct sales have a qualified
  exemption with labeling and recordkeeping. Follow Good Agricultural Practices anyway;
  grocers and some restaurants require GAP audits.
- Kansas: no license needed to sell raw whole produce; processed items (salsa, jams, baked
  goods) fall under cottage food or KDA licensing. Sales tax collection applies at markets.
- Water testing for wash and irrigation water; worker hygiene and wash-station design.
- Insurance: product liability rider; USDA Whole-Farm Revenue Protection or Micro Farm
  policy covers diversified revenue.
- Labor: seasonal hires or H-2A at larger scale; family and part-time labor at stage 1.

## 7. Operations plan
- One person owns the produce enterprise through the season; grain work cannot absorb its
  daily picking and market schedule.
- Weekly cycle: pick Thursday–Friday, wash and pack, two markets Saturday (or one market
  plus restaurant drops), CSA pickup, Monday planting and cultivation.
- Succession planting every 2–3 weeks for sweet corn, beans, cucumbers to keep the table
  full.
- Records: planting dates, yields, sales by crop and channel in a simple spreadsheet or
  farmOS; this feeds the model and tells which crops earn their labor.

## 8. Financials (summary; detail in assumptions.csv and the model)
Model mapping: product = mixed produce in pounds at a blended direct-sales price;
conversion 100%; no byproduct; grain opportunity cost set to zero and replaced by a fixed
land charge per acre in operating_fixed equal to the grain margin forgone. Labor is the
dominant variable cost and is entered per pound of product.

Stage 1 is 3 acres of sweet corn, pumpkins, melons, tomatoes and flowers; stage 2 adds a
high tunnel and 2 more acres; stage 3 adds a CSA and restaurant route at 8 acres.

Model results (regenerate with `python3 scripts/build_vertical_model.py knowledge/verticals/local-produce`):
| Case | NPV @8% (5 yr) | Payback | Breakeven acres |
|---|---|---|---|
| Base (3→5→8 acres) | −$27k | none in 5 years | 3.0 |
| Adverse (2 acres, low yield, low price) | −$143k | none | n/a |
| Favorable (3→6→10 acres, cost-shared tunnel) | +$251k | year 1 | 0.8 |

Reading: at a $1.60/lb blend and $0.70/lb of labor, stage 1 roughly covers itself and the
return comes only at stage 2–3 volumes with waste below 20%. Labor cost per pound is the
number that decides this vertical; cut-flower and tomato share raises the blend, pumpkins
and sweet corn lower labor per pound. The base case counts owner labor at $18/hour; if
owner labor is treated as otherwise idle in summer, base NPV turns positive (sensitivity
to run).

## 8a. Channel comparison: chef-direct storables vs grocery/wholesale (independent models)
Two independent channel models sit in subfolders, each with its own assumptions.csv and
workbook, so they can be pitted against each other and also run in tandem on separate
acres. Both grow mechanizable storables (specialty potatoes, garlic, alliums, sweet
potatoes, winter squash, pumpkins, popcorn) and differ in price, volume, paperwork and
capital.

| | Chef-direct storables (`chef-direct-storables/`) | Grocery / wholesale (`grocery-wholesale/`) |
|---|---|---|
| Acres | 2 → 3 | 4 → 8 |
| Price blend | $1.65/lb | $0.70/lb |
| Labor per lb | $0.40 (route and accounts) | $0.24 (grading line) |
| Capital stage 1 | ~$23k | ~$64k |
| Paperwork | $1M liability, spec sheets | $3M liability, GAP audit, PTI labels for chains |
| Volume ceiling | account count (~20–25 chef restaurants in Wichita) | wholesaler and chain acceptance |
| Base NPV @8% | +$57k, payback year 3, breakeven 0.5 acre | −$39k, no payback in 5 years, breakeven 4.2 acres |
| Adverse | −$77k | −$201k |
| Favorable | +$255k | +$293k |

Reading: the chef channel is profitable at small scale because price per pound is high and
the farm's mechanized storables keep labor low; its limit is the number of accounts. The
wholesale channel loses money until it reaches chain volume, then has the larger upside;
its limit is acceptance and compliance cost. Run in tandem, the chef channel takes the top
grades at $1.65 and the wholesale channel absorbs volume and seconds at $0.70, which
raises sell-through in both models; the tandem case is the original mixed model in this
folder plus these two, and should be re-run once Tony's acreage and labor view is known.

## 9. Staged growth plan
- Stage 0 (this winter): attend both Wichita markets, count vendors and prices, talk to
  market managers about fees and waiting lists, visit two restaurants, confirm water
  legality for the chosen plot, soil test it, plan the drip system.
- Stage 1 (year 1): 3 acres, two markets, no CSA; goal is $25,000–45,000 gross and a
  customer list. Decide by October whether labor was sustainable.
- Stage 2 (year 2–3): high tunnel for early tomatoes and late greens, 5 acres, 20–30 CSA
  shares, two restaurant accounts, online pre-order; integrate flour and birdseed sales.
- Stage 3 (year 4+): 8 acres, 50+ CSA shares, grocer account, seasonal hire, on-farm
  pumpkin event.
- Triggers: advance only if the prior stage sold at least 80% of what it grew at the
  planned blended price and the operator wants to continue.

## 10. Risks and mitigations
| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Labor shortfall at peak picking | high | high | start small; choose low-labor crops first; pre-orders reduce harvest waste |
| Time conflict with wheat harvest and planting | high | medium | plant sweet corn for late July onward; pumpkins and melons need little in June |
| Hail, heat, drought | medium | high | drip irrigation; shade cloth on tomatoes; diversify crops and planting dates |
| Herbicide drift from grain fields | medium | high | buffer rows, spray timing, separate sprayer, notify custom applicators |
| Market saturation or weak sales day | medium | medium | CSA and restaurant base; storable crops; online pre-order |
| Food safety incident | low | high | GAP practices, water testing, wash station, records |
| Burnout | medium | high | one owner, defined hours, kill rule honored |

## 11. Decision rules
1. Do not plant more than 3 acres in year 1 regardless of enthusiasm.
2. Confirm water legality and a soil test before buying any equipment.
3. Each crop must earn at least a target gross per labor hour (set it in assumptions,
   starting at $25/hour); drop crops below it after one season.
4. Advance a stage only on the sales trigger in section 9.
5. Kill rule: if year-2 gross is below $20,000 or the operator does not want year 3, stop
   and return the plot to grain.
