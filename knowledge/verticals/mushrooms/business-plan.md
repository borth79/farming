# Indoor specialty mushrooms

## 1. Concept
Grow fresh specialty mushrooms (oyster varieties, lion's mane, shiitake; later king oyster
and chestnut) year-round in a small insulated fruiting room built inside the existing farm
shop, and sell them fresh within 48 hours to Wichita chefs, natural grocers, farmers
market customers and a CSA list. The farm contributes wheat straw (oyster substrate),
tractors and a bale processor for straw handling, a building with power and water, field
ground to compost spent substrate, and a brand. It contributes no labor today: a mushroom
room at 100 lb/week is a 35-55 hour/week job, so the plan either hires a part-time grower
or costs the owner's hours at $18/hour. The strategic question is not whether mushrooms
beat grain per square foot (they do, by three orders of magnitude, because a 200 sq ft
room displaces no cropland) but whether they beat grain per dollar of capital and per
hour of labor, and whether Wichita can absorb the volume alongside the one established
local grower.

Why this farm can do it: straw is free and on site; the shop has three-phase or heavy
single-phase power to be confirmed; farm compost ground removes the spent-substrate
disposal problem that urban growers pay for; the grain operation's winter labor slack
matches the indoor calendar; and the farm's other verticals (flour, popped sorghum, oil)
share the same chef and grocer buyer list.

Pack formats: 3-5 lb vented produce boxes or paper bags for restaurants and grocers; 8 oz
and 4 oz PLA clamshells for retail; mixed "chef's blend" clamshell at a premium. Dried
mushrooms (lion's mane, shiitake) and lion's mane powder absorb unsold product at roughly
one-tenth the fresh weight; tinctures and supplements are out of scope (FDA dietary
supplement rules, trust constraint on non-agricultural lines). Claims: "grown in Sedgwick
County on our own wheat straw"; no organic certification in stage 1 (purchased blocks and
spawn would need to be certified; revisit in stage 2 if grocers require it).

## 3. Market
### Size within reach
- National commercial specialty sales were about 16.4M lb in 2025-26 ($75.0M at $4.56/lb,
  NASS), roughly 0.05 lb per person. Scaled to the Wichita metro (~650,000), the
  commercial specialty pool through grocery and foodservice distribution is about
  31,000 lb/yr, or ~600 lb/week, most of it branded packs from Monterey Mushrooms and
  Phillips Gourmet through Dillons, Walmart, Sprouts, Natural Grocers and Whole Foods
  (verify by shelf survey). Local direct sales by small growers sit on top of that pool.
- Chef demand: Wichita has 1,200+ restaurants (Visit Wichita) but the chef-driven,
  specialty-buying set is perhaps 20-40 kitchens (Dōma, Favor, The Belmont, First Mile
  Kitchen, Georges, Chester's and peers; count to be made by visits). At 5-20 lb/week
  each in season, that is 150-500 lb/week of specialty demand, part of it already served
  by Myers Mushrooms (below) and by Sysco/PFG specialty lines.
- Direct retail: Kansas Grown and Old Town markets, a CSA add-on for the local-produce
  vertical's list, and pickup orders. Cornell's grower survey puts direct prices 37%
  above wholesale; a market table moving 30-60 lb on a Saturday is typical for an
  established grower (verify by attending).

## 4. Raw material and agronomy link
- Wheat straw: oyster substrate. About 3 lb dry straw per 2 lb of oyster; at base scale
  (half the volume on straw) about 6,000-10,000 lb straw/yr, i.e. 150-250 small square
  bales or 6-10 large rounds from the farm's ~1,000 acres (a trivial share of residue;
  no agronomic cost beyond the nutrient value of the straw removed, ~$5-8/ton). Straw
  must be clean, bright, dry, and herbicide history recorded (verify residue carryover
  concerns for food use). Chop to 2-4 in with the bale processor or a leaf shredder.
- Sawdust and supplements for blocks (stage 2): hardwood sawdust or pellets, wheat bran
  or soy hull, gypsum; bought in (no hardwood on farm). Sorghum or wheat grain from the
  farm can be the grain-spawn carrier once the lab exists.
- Water: well water tested for coliform and minerals; RO for humidifiers.
- Spent substrate (about 4 lb per lb of mushrooms): composted and spread on fields or
  sold to gardeners; it is a mild fertilizer and soil amendment, which ties the vertical
  back into the soil plan.
- Identity preservation, protein and mycotoxin specs do not apply; the link to the grain
  plan is straw, spawn grain, compost and shared labor calendar.

## 5. Process and equipment
### Flow
Straw (farm) -> chop -> hot-water pasteurization (150-180 F, 1.5-2 h) or cold lime bath
(pH 11-13, 16-24 h) -> drain -> inoculate with grain spawn in filter-patch bags -> incubate
10-14 days at 70-75 F -> fruiting room (60-68 F, 85-95% RH, CO2 under ~800-1,000 ppm,
fresh-air exchange 4-8 volumes/h, 12 h light) -> harvest -> cool to 34-38 F within 1 h ->
pack -> deliver within 48 h. Purchased ready-to-fruit blocks (lion's mane, shiitake,
later king oyster, chestnut) skip the first four steps. Stage 2 adds a sterile lab:
sawdust mix -> bag -> sterilize (15 psi autoclave or long atmospheric steam) -> cool ->
inoculate under a flow hood -> incubate 3-8 weeks by species.

Kansas climate design: the fruiting room needs a 12k BTU mini-split that cools in 100 F
July and heats in 0 F January; fresh-air exchange is the dominant energy load, so intake
air is filtered and tempered; ultrasonic humidifiers on RO water; CO2 monitor driving
the exhaust fan; FRP-lined walls, sealed epoxy floor with a drain, no wood. A backup
generator covers outages. Energy is budgeted at 5 kWh/lb (industry large-farm figure is
0.8-3 kWh/lb; small rooms do worse; meter the pilot).

### Labor
Industry average 0.56 hr/lb across five small farms; well-run farms under 0.5 hr/lb.
Plan 0.35 hr/lb in stage 1 (about 35 hr/week at 100 lb/week), 0.25 hr/lb in stage 2,
all hours valued at $18/hr loaded. Tasks: substrate prep and inoculation (1 day/week),
daily misting checks and harvest (1-2 h/day, 7 days), packing and delivery (1 day/week),
market day (Saturday), cleaning and records.

## 6. Regulatory and food safety
- Kansas license: K-State MF3138 (rev. 2024) and KDA: cultivated mushrooms that are only
  cleaned (not heat-treated or processed) may be sold without a food establishment
  license, at farmers markets, direct and wholesale, provided they are grown in protected
  environments and kept free of contaminants. Dried mushrooms and powders are processed
  foods and need a KDA food processing license (or fit cottage-food style exemptions only
  for direct sales; verify). Cottage food rules are not what applies to fresh mushrooms.
  Wild mushroom identifier rules are irrelevant (nothing foraged).
- FSMA Produce Safety Rule: mushrooms are covered produce. Farms under the inflation-
  adjusted de minimis (about $34,000 average produce sales over three years, verify FDA's
  current figure) are not covered; between that and $500,000 with more than half of sales
  to qualified end users (consumers, local restaurants and retailers within the state or
  275 miles) the farm is qualified-exempt and must keep sales records and label with the
  farm name and address. Base-case sales cross the de minimis in year 2, so follow
  Mushroom Good Agricultural Practices (American Mushroom Institute) from day one: water
  test, sanitation SOPs, worker hygiene, lot codes, traceability.
- Labeling: product name, net weight, farm name and address; "Kansas grown"; From the
  Land of Kansas mark after enrollment. Grocers want UPC and a certificate of insurance
  ($1-2M product liability).
- Zoning and building: Sedgwick County agricultural zoning generally permits on-farm
  production buildings; confirm electrical permit and any commercial-use question for the
  shop. Trust: a permanent room is an improvement on trust land; see section 10.
- Insurance: product liability, premises, and whether the farm policy covers an indoor
  food enterprise (verify with agent).
- Records: production log by batch (spawn lot, block supplier lot, dates, yields,
  contamination), temperature logs for the cooler, sales by account, water tests.

## 7. Operations plan
- Staffing: one part-time grower-seller (owner or hire) at 25-40 hr/week in stage 1;
  a second part-time person for the lab and second room in stage 2; a route driver or
  distributor in stage 3.
- Weekly schedule: Monday pasteurize and inoculate straw, receive blocks; daily harvest
  early morning and cool; Tuesday-Thursday afternoon sales calls; Thursday pack and
  Wichita delivery route; Saturday market; Sunday clean and reset racks. Blocks are
  staged so a third of racks are in each fruiting phase to level weekly output.
- Logistics: farm pickup with chest coolers and ice packs in stage 1; walk-in and
  insulated van later. Delivery within 48 h of harvest; standing orders confirmed by text
  Wednesday.
- Packaging and inventory: fresh inventory never exceeds 3 days; unsold product dried
  Friday; dried stock sold at market and online.
- Quality: grade A (whole clusters, dry caps) to chefs and grocers; grade B to market
  blends and drying.

## 8. Financials
Detail: `assumptions.csv` and `mushrooms-financial-model.xlsx` (regenerate with
`python3 scripts/build_vertical_model.py knowledge/verticals/mushrooms`).

### Model mapping (the model is built for crop verticals)
- `acres` = fruiting rooms in use; fractional values are occupancy (0.6 = one room run at
  60%). One room = 8x16 ft chamber plus shared support space.
- `yield_per_acre` = lb fresh mushrooms per full room per year (base 5,000 = 100 lb/week).
- `yield_unit_lb` = 1, `conversion_pct` = 100 (product is fresh whole mushrooms).
- `byproduct_pct` = spent substrate lb per 100 lb mushrooms if sold (favorable 400 at
  $0.02/lb); 0 in base and adverse (composted on farm).
- `grain_price_per_lb` = 0; the building footprint is charged as an arm's-length site
  rent in `operating_fixed` (`site_charge_building_lease_to_trust`) and straw in the
  substrate row.
- Stage 2 cost reductions (own blocks, labor routine) are negative per-lb rows because
  the model sums variable rows for all stages up to the current one.
- All labor, including the owner's, is costed at $18/hr. "Breakeven acres" reads as
  breakeven rooms at the stage 1 (buy-blocks, no lab) cost structure.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base | $33,800 | $77,200 | 12.50 | 1.10 | 3.0 (not reachable in one room) | -$45,700 | none in 5 yr | $90,000 | $13,500 |

Reading the base case: stage 1 (purchased lion's mane and shiitake blocks, farm-straw
oyster, paid labor at 0.35 hr/lb) earns about $1.10/lb over variable cost, which cannot
cover $9,000 of fixed cost and $3,400 of depreciation in one room; the operation loses
~$11,000 a year in EBITDA in years 1-2. Stage 2 (own blocks, routine labor) lifts
contribution to about $4.40/lb, and 1.6 rooms earn ~$13,500 EBITDA on $77,000 of
capital: a 7-8% return after depreciation, below the 8% hurdle, so NPV is negative but
close. The stage 2 breakeven, computed from the model's year-3 cost structure, is about
4,800 lb/yr, roughly one full room (fixed $11,700 + depreciation $7,700 over $4.40/lb).
The favorable case is an upper bound (lion's mane heavy mix, direct sales half of
volume, own blocks from year 2, 120 lb/week per room, a KC/OKC route) and must not size
debt. The adverse case (bought blocks at 1.3 lb each, 0.56 hr/lb, $9 blended price,
never leaving stage 1) is the common failure mode of small mushroom farms.

### Comparison with row crops on the same footprint, per dollar and per hour
| Measure | Mushrooms, base stage 2 | Dryland row crops (knowledge/crops) |
|---|---|---|
| Footprint | ~1,100 sq ft of building (0.025 acre) | 0.025 acre of cropland |
| Revenue per sq ft per year | ~$80 | $0.007 (wheat ~$322/acre at 50 bu, $6.45) |

Conclusion: per square foot mushrooms win by a factor of a thousand, but that is the
wrong test because the room displaces no cropland. Per hour, a stage 2 mushroom room
pays about a farm wage ($21-25/hr) and stage 1 pays well under minimum wage; grain
farming pays more per hour of the operator's time in normal years because the hours are
few and the acres are many. Per dollar, the base case does not clear 8%. Mushrooms are
therefore a labor business that makes sense only if (a) a family member wants a
year-round farm job that grain does not provide, (b) the direct-sales share and lion's
mane share come in at or above the favorable assumptions, and (c) substrate is made on
farm from the start of year 2. They are not a better use of capital than grain on the
numbers available today.

| Contribution per lb | −$0.95 base; positive only in the favorable case ($9.50 price, $6.25 cost) |
| Fixed costs per year | ~$115k (manager $60k, site lease $12k, route $15k, insurance, repairs, compliance) |
| Capital, all stages | ~$334k base |
| Labor | ~12,500 crew hours/yr (about 6 full-time equivalents) plus the manager |
| NPV @8% | base −$1.03M; adverse −$1.31M; favorable −$114k |

Reading: scale makes mushrooms worse, not better, for this farm. Price falls faster than
cost as volume outruns the local chef market, and labor stays near half of cost because
harvest and packing are manual. Ten containers is a 7-person perishable-food business
selling at distributor prices against Monterey and Phillips; it would need either
automation that cuts labor below $2.50/lb or a contracted premium buyer for 500+ lb/week
before it could be considered. The one-room case remains the only version that pays, and
it pays a wage, not a return.

## 8b. Vertical density sensitivity (`high-density/assumptions.csv`)
Both models already assume racked (vertical) production; the base figure of ~100 lb/week
per container is conservative. This case uses 5-tier dense racking at ~300 lb/week per
40 ft container (vendor claims 200–400; verify in a pilot), so 3 containers deliver the
same ~45,000 lb/yr as 10 low-density containers.

| Case | Capital | Base NPV @8% |
|---|---|---|
| 10 containers, low density, $7.00/lb | ~$334k | −$1.03M |
| 3 containers, high density, $7.00/lb | ~$185k | −$0.90M |
| 3 containers, high density, price held at $12.50/lb (hypothetical) | ~$185k | −$0.26M |

Reading: density cuts capital per pound roughly in half but does not change the two
numbers that decide the business, price per pound (set by how much the market absorbs)
and labor plus substrate per pound (set by manual harvest and packing). Even if 900 lb a
week could be sold at chef prices, which the Wichita market cannot do, the case stays
negative because fixed costs (manager, route, site, compliance) sit on a margin of a few
dollars a pound. Density is worth using in a one- or two-room operation to cut capital;
it does not make a ten-container operation viable.

## 10. Risks and mitigations
| Risk | Likelihood | Effect | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contamination (Trichoderma green mold, bacterial blotch, cobweb) | high in year 1 | lost batches, 20-50% yield loss | buy colonized blocks in stage 1; strict pasteurization temperature logs; filtered intake air; quarantine and remove infected bags; do not build the lab until straw bags run below 10% loss |
| Humidity and mold damage to the building | medium | rot, mold in the shop, insurance issues | vapor-barriered FRP-lined room with drain, dehumidified anteroom, keep humidity inside the room, inspect wall cavities quarterly; container alternative isolates the building |
| Power outage (summer heat) | medium (rural grid) | crop loss within 12-24 h in July | generator with transfer inlet; temperature alarm to phone; harvest early if outage forecast |
| Labor: no one on the farm has 35 hr/week | high | plan stalls or quality slips | stage 0 measures hours; hire a part-time grower before stage 1 or do not proceed; routine standing orders cut selling time |
| Price competition (Myers Mushrooms, Monterey and Phillips packs, KC and OKC growers) | medium | wholesale $9 instead of $12.50 | lead with lion's mane and chef relationships, direct share >= 40%, freshness within 48 h; do not compete on oyster price |
| Demand softness (NASS specialty value -21% in 2025-26; Smallhold failure) | medium | slower ramp | grow to order; LOIs before each room |
| Trust approval and improvement ownership | high until the instrument is read | room cannot be built, or belongs to the trust with no reimbursement | checklist in family-trust-constraints.md: trustee resolution, lease clause covering the room and removal/reimbursement, operate through the LLC, site rent at market; container option if a permanent room is refused |
| Food-safety incident | low | recall, loss of grocer accounts | Mushroom GAP, lot codes, cooler logs, product liability insurance |
| Key person | high | enterprise stops if the grower leaves | written SOPs from day one; cross-train one family member |
| Energy cost higher than 5 kWh/lb in Kansas extremes | medium | +$0.30-0.60/lb | meter the pilot; insulate; time fresh-air exchange; consider fruiting oyster in shoulder seasons only |
| Herbicide residue in straw | low | product safety or yield issue | use straw from fields with recorded herbicide history; test one batch |

## 11. Decision rules
- Go to stage 0 now (winter 2026-27): cost under $2,500, no structure, teaches yields,
  hours and prices. Conditions: a named person commits 10 hr/week; informal trustee
  consent.
- Go to stage 1 only if all hold: five accounts or LOIs totaling >= 60 lb/week at
  >= $10/lb wholesale or >= $14/lb direct; measured stage 0 labor <= 0.45 hr/lb; a grower
  (owner or hire) committed to 30 hr/week for 24 months; written trustee approval and
  lease clause for the room; stage 1 capital <= $40,000.
- Kill criteria (any one): blended price below $10/lb for two consecutive quarters;
  contamination losses above 25% for three months after month 6; fewer than 60 lb/week
  sold by month 12; labor above 0.5 hr/lb at month 12; trustee refuses the room and a
  container route costs more than $45,000.
- Do not build the sterile lab (stage 2) until stage 1 has sold 85% of output for 12
  weeks and a second trained person exists.
- Favorable-case numbers never size debt; borrow against base at most.
- Review dates: end of stage 0 (March 2027); month 6 and month 12 of stage 1; annually.
