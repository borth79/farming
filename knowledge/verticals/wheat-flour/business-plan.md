# Hard red winter wheat → stone-ground flour → bread

> Numbers in this plan trace to `assumptions.csv` in this folder (model inputs) or to the
> sources listed above. Items marked **verify** were not confirmed by a primary source or
> a dated quote in this review and must be checked before capital is committed.

## 1. Concept
Mill a small, identity-preserved share of the farm's own hard red winter wheat (HRW) into
stone-ground flour and sell it within roughly 30 miles of the farm to Wichita-area
bakeries, pizzerias, restaurants, independent grocers and farmers-market customers; later
add other milled products and e-commerce, and eventually capture the bread margin either
through a bakery partner or an owned licensed bakery.

Why this farm: it already grows the raw material at commodity cost (~$0.11/lb farm-gate,
`wheat-hard-red-winter.md`, Oct 2026) and can control variety, protein, cleanliness and
storage. The product sells for roughly ten to fifteen times the grain price per pound
($1.15–1.95/lb wholesale benchmarks, see section 3). Milling, sifting and bagging are
mechanical steps that can run largely unattended with gravity flow and a semi-automatic
bagging scale, so the labor added is mostly selling and delivering. A 650,000-person metro
is 20–30 minutes away. The 2026 Kansas crop averaged 12.1–12.3% protein with 63% of samples
grading No. 1 (`wheat-hard-red-winter.md`), which is bread-flour range.

Why not scale faster: the binding constraint is sales, not capacity. One 20-inch stone
mill can turn the output of 50 acres in under 400 run-hours; finding 5–6 bakery accounts
that each take a 50-lb bag a day is the hard part. The plan therefore starts at 10–20
acres and grows acres only behind signed accounts.

## 3. Market
### Target customers
- **Wholesale (stage 1 core)**: artisan and sourdough bakeries, pizzerias (Wichita has a
  notable pizza scene; a 2026 Pizzello ranking placed it 45th nationally, a quality
  ranking not a count), independent restaurants with in-house bread, tortilla/flatbread
  makers, breweries (spent-grain-adjacent partnerships), independent grocers and food
  co-ops, Wichita State and school food-service programs (farm-to-school).
- **Retail**: farmers markets (Kansas Grown! Farmers Market, Old Town Farm & Art Market,
  Derby, Andover **verify** which are active), on-farm pickup, e-commerce (stage 2),
  specialty grocers and gift/holiday boxes.
- **Institutional/regional (stage 2–3)**: Kansas City and Oklahoma City artisan bakeries,
  home-milling and sourdough hobbyists online.

### Market size within reach
- Wichita metro population 647,610 (2020 census). Restaurant counts conflict: about
  1,284 restaurants in the city (Oysterlink) and 962 "restaurants and other eating
  places" company profiles (D&B). No clean count of bakeries or pizzerias was found; a
  directory snapshot listed 13 bakeries (Birdeye), which undercounts home-based and
  in-store bakeries. **Verify** with Census County Business Patterns (Sedgwick County,
  NAICS 311811 retail bakeries, 311812 commercial bakeries, 722511/722513) and a
  hand-built account list before the first sales call.
- Volume logic (`vertical-integration-options.md`): one 50-lb bag per bakery per
  working day is ~18,000 lb/yr per account. Stage 1 needs 1–2 anchor accounts plus
  10–20 smaller accounts to reach 20,000–40,000 lb/yr. Stage 2 at 50 acres produces
  ~128,000 lb flour/yr and needs 5–6 daily-bag equivalents or a retail/e-commerce brand.

### Channel strategy and first accounts
1. Mill 500–1,000 lb of test flour on a rented or borrowed mill (or a Mockmill Pro 200,
   ~$850–900, ~200 g/min) before buying any equipment; send protein, falling number and
   DON to a lab; deliver 5-lb samples with a spec sheet to 20 bakeries and pizzerias.
2. Win 2 anchor accounts with a 12-month price commitment and a standing weekly order.
3. Farmers-market presence for brand and retail margin; collect emails for e-commerce.
4. Year 2: independent grocers (3–5 doors), restaurant group, school program pilot.
5. Year 3: e-commerce (shelf-stable, ships well), Kansas City/OKC bakery outreach.

### Sales ramp (model, share of flour produced that is sold)
Base 50/70/80/85/90% in years 1–5; adverse 35/50/60/70/75%; favorable 65/85/90/95/95%.
In practice unsold flour is wheat that is never milled and is sold as grain; the model
conservatively charges milling cost on all output.

## 4. Raw material and agronomy link
- **Acres**: base 15 → 20 → 30 → 40 → 50 in years 1–5; adverse 10 → 30; favorable
  20 → 50 by year 4. At 50 bu/acre dryland (trend for good south-central soils; Kansas
  state average 34–51 bu in 2022–2026), 50 acres = 2,500 bu = 150,000 lb grain. The mill
  fields are a rounding error on 1,000 acres; the rest of the wheat is marketed normally.
- **Variety**: choose from the farm shortlist (`wheat-hard-red-winter.md`: KS Providence,
  Bob Dole, Zenda, KS Hatchett, KS Mako, KS Flintlock, KS Tradition, AP Roadrunner) for
  Kansas Wheat Commission milling/baking quality and protein stability, planted on the
  best bottomland fields after soybeans (highest protein and yield; 40 lb N credit).
  Heritage varieties (Turkey Red) are a marketing option for a premium line but yield
  20–40% less **verify** and lodge; try 2–5 acres only.
- **Quality specs for the mill**: protein ≥12.0% (12% mb) for bread flour, ≥11.5%
  acceptable for pizza/all-purpose blend; test weight ≥58 lb/bu; moisture ≤13.5% into
  storage; DON <1 ppm in grain (FDA advisory for finished wheat products is 1 ppm, so
  grain going to the mill should test below it); falling number ≥300 s **verify**;
  no treated seed, no sprouted or insect-damaged kernels; herbicide pre-harvest intervals
  met and documented. The 2026 Kansas crop (12.1–12.3% protein, 60.3 lb/bu test weight,
  falling number ~356 s nationally) fits. Protein drives late-N decisions: a split
  jointing-stage N application raises protein more than yield.
- **Agronomic implications**: the Bread Bakers Guild artisan spec is 11–12% protein and
  0.48–0.54% ash; a trade spec runs 10.5–12.5%; sourdough bakers often prefer 12–14%.
  Kansas HRW at 12–13% is squarely usable; very high protein (>14%) is not better for
  artisan bread. Target 12–13% and let the sifting level, not protein, define products.
- **Identity preservation**: combine the mill fields first after a clean-out, haul in
  a cleaned truck, store in a dedicated aerated bin (3,000 bu), sample every load for
  moisture and protein and every bin for DON; keep field-to-bin-to-lot records.
- **Storage**: cool to <60 F by aeration, monitor monthly, fumigate or heat-treat empty
  bins only; no in-bin insecticide on mill grain unless labeled and documented.
- **Off-spec grain**: anything failing protein or DON goes to the elevator as commodity
  wheat; the farm loses only the premium, not the crop. If the mill fields fail entirely
  (drought, hail, scab), buy identity-preserved grain from a neighbor under contract and
  label truthfully ("Kansas-grown", not "single-farm") for that year.

### Automation and labor
- Mill and sifter run continuously with hopper feed; an operator loads grain, changes
  bags and checks temperature. Target 150 lb finished flour per operator-hour all-in
  (cleaning, milling, sifting, bagging, cleanup) in stage 1, improving toward 250 lb/hr
  with gravity flow and a semi-automatic bagging scale. Modeled at $0.13/lb labor base
  ($20/h loaded; Wichita bakers mean $15.34/h, BLS May 2024; food batchmaker wage
  **verify**), $0.18 adverse, $0.10 favorable.
- Stone temperature must stay below ~110–120 F to protect flavor and germ **verify**;
  larger stones at lower rpm run cooler, which is why a 20-in mill is preferred over
  pushing a 12-in.
- Electricity: ~0.05 kWh/lb all-in at ~12.3 c/kWh Kansas commercial average (Oct 2026;
  **verify** against the Evergy Kansas Central small-general-service tariff) = <$0.01/lb.

### Facility
Existing farm building, 1,000–2,000 sq ft: sealed concrete floor, washable walls and
ceiling in the milling/bagging room, screened and sealed openings, door sweeps, bait
stations outside only, separate dry storage for bags and finished flour on pallets off
the wall, handwash sink, restroom access, pest-control contract, no wood pallets in the
bagging room. Separate the raw-grain receiving side from the finished-product side.
Dust is a combustion hazard: dust collection, grounding, and no open motors in the
flour cloud. If sorghum (gluten-free) is added later it needs its own room and line.

## 6. Regulatory and food safety
- **Kansas Department of Agriculture (KDA), Food Safety & Lodging**: a flour mill is a
  food processing plant and must hold a KDA food processor license before selling to
  anyone other than the end consumer. Fees: K.S.A. 65-688 caps the annual license at
  $200 for plants under 5,000 sq ft and $400 at 5,000 sq ft or more, with a one-time
  application fee capped at $350; KDA's published tier table shows $300 application +
  $325 license for 10,001–50,000 sq ft. Licenses expire March 31 and are not prorated.
  Plan review and a pre-opening inspection are required (KDA Food Processor Licensing
  Guide, updated 2025-11-13). **Verify** the current fee for the sub-5,000 sq ft tier.
- **FDA food facility registration**: required for a mill that sells wholesale; free;
  needs a DUNS/UFI number; biennial renewal (2026 is a renewal year, Oct–Dec).
- **FSMA Preventive Controls for Human Food**: a "very small business" (< $1 million
  in annual human-food sales, inflation-adjusted, averaged over three years) is a
  qualified facility with modified requirements: attestation to FDA, compliance with
  applicable state law, and a label or point-of-purchase statement with the facility
  name and address. Good Manufacturing Practices (21 CFR 117 subpart B) still apply
  in full. Write a simple food safety plan anyway (hazard analysis: DON, foreign
  material, allergen = wheat, pests, moisture). Flour is a raw agricultural product;
  label "raw flour, do not eat uncooked" after the E. coli flour recalls.
- **Labeling**: statement of identity, net weight, ingredient (wheat flour), allergen
  (contains wheat), name and address, lot code; no nutrition facts panel is required
  for a business with fewer than 100 FTE and under 100,000 units/yr if the small-business
  exemption notice is filed with FDA **verify**; otherwise a panel is needed for retail.
- **Allergens**: wheat is a major allergen; a later gluten-free sorghum line must be
  physically separate and tested if labeled gluten-free (<20 ppm).
- **Mycotoxins**: FDA advisory level 1 ppm DON in finished wheat products for human
  consumption (2010 guidance; advisory, not a binding limit); 10 ppm for grain and
  byproducts going to some livestock. Test every bin at harvest and every milling lot
  with lateral-flow strips; confirm by lab once a year and in any scab year. Keep
  results for 2 years.
- **Weights and measures**: bagging scale must be legal-for-trade for net-weight sales
  **verify** Kansas requirements.
- **Sales tax**: Kansas sales tax on retail food sales (state rate 6.5% plus local;
  Kansas has been reducing the state food sales tax, **verify** 2026 rate); wholesale
  sales are exempt with a resale certificate.
- **Kansas cottage-food (home-kitchen) route for bread**: under K.S.A. 65-689(d)(4) a
  person who produces food for direct distribution to the end consumer is exempt from
  licensing if the food does not require time/temperature control for safety or
  specialized processing. Secondary 2026 guides agree that breads, cookies, cakes without
  cream/custard and similar non-TCS baked goods qualify, that there is no license, fee,
  inspection or sales cap, and that sales must be direct to the consumer (home, farm,
  farmers market, roadside stand). Sources conflict on whether a "home-produced" label
  statement is required and whether online sales or shipping are allowed; third-party
  (wholesale) sales clearly are not. **Verify** with KDA Food Safety before the first
  loaf is sold; label with name, address, ingredients, allergen and net weight
  regardless. This is the stage-3 demand test, not a business model: it cannot supply
  wholesale accounts.
- **Owned bakery (stage 3)**: KDA food establishment or processor license, local
  (Sedgwick County/Wichita) health, building, fire and zoning permits, grease and hood
  code, FDA registration if wholesale. Partner/co-pack route puts these on the partner.
- **Insurance**: product liability ($1M/$2M typical requirement from grocers), property
  and stock, vehicle, workers' compensation once there is an employee. Estimated
  $2,500–5,000/yr **verify**.
- **Recordkeeping**: lot traceability one step back (field, bin) and one step forward
  (customer, date); recall plan; pest-control log; cleaning log; temperature and
  moisture log; DON results; equipment maintenance; employee training.

## 7. Operations plan
- **Staffing**: year 1–2 the operator (or a family member) at ~12 h/week fixed for
  sales, deliveries and records plus milling hours that scale with volume (~150 lb per
  hour). Stage 2 adds a second part-time person (~12 h/week) for fulfilment and added
  products. Stage 3 owned bakery adds a head baker (full time, $40,000–55,000 **verify**)
  and 1–2 production/packing staff.
- **Production schedule**: mill weekly to order (freshness is the selling point) in
  2–4 hour runs; whole wheat is milled within 7 days of delivery; sifted flour can be
  held 2–4 weeks. At stage 2 volumes (~2,500 lb/week) a single 6–8 hour milling day per
  week suffices on a 20-in mill.
- **Logistics**: one delivery day per week into Wichita (route of 8–15 stops, 1,500–
  2,000 lb per trip in a cargo van or enclosed trailer); farmers markets Saturday;
  farm pickup by appointment; e-commerce ships 2 days per week via UPS/USPS.
- **Packaging**: 50-lb multiwall paper bags (sewn or pinch-top), 25-lb on request, 5-lb
  printed paper bags with window-free design and lot code; bran in 40-lb feed bags.
- **Inventory**: grain in bin (whole crop year), finished flour inventory held to 2–4
  weeks of sales, bags and labels 3 months. Whole wheat flour stored cool; stage 2 adds
  a walk-in cooler.
- **Quality**: every lot gets moisture, DON strip, visual and a bake test (a loaf or
  pizza base from each lot) with results logged against the lot code.

### Grain comparison
50 acres at 50 bu and $6.45/bu is $16,100 of grain. The same acres as flour at 90% sold
gross $173,900 and net ~$82,500 EBITDA before debt service in the base case: about five
times the grain value as added margin, for roughly 900–1,000 hours of labor and
$141,500 of capital.

### Stage 3 bakery side-calculation (not in the sheet; **verify** every line)
A 1.5-lb artisan loaf uses ~1 lb flour (~$1.50 of farm flour at wholesale value, or
~$0.25 of grain) plus ~$0.40 of other ingredients and packaging, and sells for $7–10
retail or $4–5.50 wholesale in comparable markets **verify Wichita**. At 600 loaves/week
(a small wholesale/retail bakery), revenue is ~$190,000–280,000/yr; labor (head baker
plus 1.5 FTE) ~$110,000–130,000; ingredients, packaging, utilities ~$45,000–60,000;
rent/occupancy if off-farm ~$24,000–36,000; owned-bakery capital $90,000–240,000. The
bakery roughly doubles the per-pound value of the flour it uses but adds a labor-heavy,
perishable, daily business. The partner/co-pack route (a Wichita bakery bakes a
co-branded "farm loaf" and buys the flour) captures a share of this with no capital and
is the default; the owned bakery is only justified if cottage-food tests sell out and a
head baker is identified.

### Sensitivities
- Price: each $0.10/lb on base year-5 volume is ~$11,500/yr EBITDA.
- Sales ramp: moving the base ramp down to the adverse ramp alone cuts year-5 EBITDA by
  about $26,000.
- Capital: a used cleaner, a dedicated existing bin and a farm pickup instead of a van
  cut stage-1 capital by ~$35,000, the single largest lever on payback.
- Yield: 35 bu instead of 50 bu lowers flour output 30%; at fixed sales it is covered by
  more acres, so yield risk is really grain-cost and quality risk.

## 10. Risks and mitigations
| Risk | Likelihood / impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Sales do not materialize; account churn | High / high | Stage-0 test with letters of intent; 12-month anchor agreements; diversify across bakeries, pizzerias, grocers, retail; never buy stage-2 equipment ahead of demand |
| Price pressure from Farmer Direct Foods, Hudson Cream, Heartland Mill and national brands | Medium / medium | Sell single-farm provenance and freshness (milled this week); 5-lb retail margin; do not chase the $0.60–0.80/lb grocery flour price |
| Crop failure, low protein or DON above 1 ppm in the mill fields | Medium (drought and scab are Kansas facts) / medium | Several fields and varieties; contract backup grain from a neighbor; test every bin; off-spec goes to the elevator |
| Food safety incident (foreign material, pathogen in raw flour, mycotoxin) | Low / very high | GMPs, magnets and sifting, lot traceability, "raw flour" labeling, recall plan, product liability insurance |
| Pests (insects, rodents) in stored grain or flour | Medium / medium | Aeration, clean-out, sealed room, pest contract, short flour inventory, cooler |
| Dust explosion or fire | Low / very high | Dust collection, grounding, housekeeping, no ignition sources, insurance |
| Regulatory: license delays, labeling errors, cottage-food interpretation | Medium / low | Engage KDA before fit-out; follow the KDA Food Processor Licensing Guide; confirm cottage-food scope in writing |
| Key person: one operator does milling, sales and delivery | High / high | Written SOPs; cross-train a second person by year 2; keep the line simple enough to run from a checklist |
| Equipment supplier risk (New American Stone Mills fire Nov 2025, Meadows lead time 12–16 weeks) | Medium / low | Order early; Meadows as primary; used market as fallback |
| Capital overrun on facility fit-out | Medium / medium | Use an existing building; firm quotes before start; adverse case $45,000 |
| Grain price spike raises opportunity cost (adverse $7.50/bu) | Medium / low | Contribution is still >$0.60/lb; flour price adjusts annually |

## 11. Decision rules
- **Go to stage 1 only if** all of: (a) ≥2 bakery or pizzeria letters of intent at
  ≥$1.25/lb for 50-lb bags totaling ≥15,000 lb/yr; (b) test flour from farm wheat
  tests ≥12% protein, DON <1 ppm, and bakes acceptably in two bakeries; (c) firm quotes
  put stage-1 capital at ≤$125,000 with an existing building; (d) KDA confirms the
  licensing path and fee; (e) finance-controller confirms the farm can carry a year-1
  cash draw of ~$80,000 (base) to ~$140,000 (adverse) without stressing operating credit.
- **Kill stage 1 if** year-2 sales are below 25,000 lb, or EBITDA is negative in both
  year 2 and year 3, or a food-safety event occurs that insurance would not cover.
- **Go to stage 2 if** year-2 sales ≥35,000 lb with ≥10 recurring accounts and the mill
  is below 40% utilization; otherwise hold at stage 1 and add acres only.
- **Go to stage 3 (partner) if** a Wichita bakery agrees to a co-branded loaf buying
  ≥10,000 lb/yr of flour; **go to stage 3 (owned) only if** cottage-food bread sells
  out most markets for a full season, a head baker is identified, and flour EBITDA is
  ≥$60,000/yr.
- **Reviews**: after the stage-0 market test (before any equipment order); every
  March (license renewal) with the prior calendar year's sold pounds, price realized,
  EBITDA and DON results; and immediately after any crop failure in the mill fields.
