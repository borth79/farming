# Soybeans — double-crop after wheat

> Scope: soybeans planted immediately after hard red winter wheat harvest (mid-June
> to early July) in Sedgwick County, Kansas. Shared agronomy (variety traits,
> fertility, weeds, insects, diseases, harvest, marketing) is in
> `soybeans-full-season.md`; this file covers only what differs and the go/no-go
> decision. Confidence is low because the county yield history, the K-State
> double-crop budget totals and the 2026/2027 RMA Sedgwick County double-crop
> status could not be opened in this environment (see gaps).

## Fit for this operation
- Why consider it: a second crop on the same acre with the wheat already paying the
  land, machinery ownership, P/K and most of the weed-control cost. The soybean
  N credit (about 40 lb N/acre; K-State L778) then benefits the sorghum or corn that
  follows, and the crop keeps living cover on wheat stubble through the summer.
- Why it is marginal here: K-State's MF3461 review says double cropping works best in
  east-central and southeast Kansas and that a long-term average of about 20 bu/acre
  is often quoted for double-crop soybeans in central and northeast Kansas, versus
  20–40 bu/acre farther southeast. Sedgwick County is at the western edge of the
  reliable double-crop zone: June–July moisture is the deciding variable, and the
  crop flowers and fills in August–September heat.
- Irrigated fields (if water rights are confirmed) change the picture: with water the
  practice becomes a dependable 35–50 bu/acre crop (K-State irrigated double-crop
  figures; verify) and the go/no-go question is mostly about calendar and insurance.
- Equipment fit: needs a no-till drill or planter able to cut through heavy wheat
  straw within days of harvest, and a sprayer available for burndown the same week.
  If the combine crew and the planter crew are the same people, the practice fails on
  timeliness.
- Disqualifiers: wheat harvest not finished by the RMA double-crop final planting
  date (believed July 5 for Kansas; verify for Sedgwick County); dry soil at seeding
  depth with no rain in the 7-day forecast; a Palmer amaranth population already
  escaped in the wheat stubble; fields where the following crop is wheat again
  (double-crop beans mature too late for timely wheat planting in some years).

## Agronomic requirements (differences from full-season)

### Planting date and cutoff
- K-State: soybeans after wheat are planted 2–6 weeks after the optimum date;
  planting promptly after wheat harvest is critical because the goal is the longest
  possible growing season, and earlier planting raises yield potential when moisture
  is not limiting (eUpdate 1105, 2026).
- K-State planting-date response: about 0.3 bu/acre per day of potential lost;
  mid-July planting potential about 50 bu/acre statewide under good conditions, far
  less on dryland in SC Kansas.
- SE Kansas (Parsons) optimum for double-crop planting is late June to early July
  (K-State 2015 study); Sedgwick County is drier, so the practical window is tighter.
- Farm cutoff: plant by 5 July; do not plant after 10 July under any circumstances
  (frost risk to MG IV planted mid-July is low in Wichita, but yield potential and
  insurability fall away). The RMA double-crop soybean final planting date in Kansas
  is listed as July 5 with a 10-day late planting period (Harris 2025; Lund & Smith
  2026) — verify Sedgwick County's 2027 actuarial date.
- Wheat harvest in Sedgwick County typically runs 10–30 June (verify from
  wheat file / crop progress history); in a normal year this leaves a 1–3 week
  double-crop window. In a late wheat year (harvest after 1 July) the window closes.

### Moisture at wheat harvest
- Rule of thumb for this farm: proceed only if the top 12 in of soil is moist enough
  to germinate seed placed at 1.5–2 in, and either (a) 1 in or more of rain fell in
  the 10 days before harvest or (b) the 7-day forecast carries a 50% or better chance
  of 0.5 in or more. K-State: "earlier planting generally raises yield potential when
  moisture isn't limiting" — the converse is the failure mode. Dusting seed into dry
  soil waiting for rain is acceptable only before 1 July; after that, seed waiting on
  rain loses its calendar as well.
- Full-season soybeans and corn are already using water by late June; the double-crop
  bean starts from whatever the wheat left. Wheat stubble that was weed-free and
  harvested at a 10–14 in cut preserves the most moisture.

### Residue management
- Harvest wheat with a stripper header or a conventional header set high (10–14 in
  stubble) and spread straw and chaff evenly; tall standing stubble shades the soil,
  cuts evaporation and wind, and does not drag on the drill the way flat straw does.
- Do not bale or burn the straw: the moisture conserved is worth more than the bales
  in a dryland double-crop system (K-State MF3461), and burning removes the P/K and
  carbon the wheat paid for.
- Use a no-till drill or planter with sharp coulters/openers and adequate down
  pressure; check seed-to-soil contact every few hundred feet; hairpinning of straw
  into the furrow is the main cause of poor stands.
- Volunteer wheat and summer annuals must be killed at planting (see weeds).

### Variety and maturity group
- K-State (eUpdate 1105, 2026): choose the same or a slightly later maturity rating
  than the full-season variety so the plant builds growth before flowering; an
  earlier-maturing variety planted double-crop often leaves very short plants with
  pods near the ground that are hard to harvest.
- Farm rule: MG 4.2–4.9 for planting 15 June–5 July in Sedgwick County; do not drop
  to MG III. Varieties should be tall, indeterminate, with good pod height, shatter
  resistance and SCN resistance (SCN pressure is unchanged by the planting date).
- Trait platform: Enlist E3 by default — the double-crop POST application lands in
  July when K-State found only about 9 days on average had 2 or more hours of legal
  OTT dicamba conditions (eUpdate 1109, 2026). Glufosinate tolerance is essential.

### Seeding rate, row spacing, depth
- K-State: double-crop soybeans often benefit from narrow rows and higher seeding
  rates to offset the late planting date (MF3154); a slightly higher rate helps build
  canopy (eUpdate 1105).
- Farm rule: drill or 15-in rows at 150,000–180,000 seeds/acre (upper end after
  1 July); 30-in rows at 140,000–160,000 only if no narrow-row tool is available.
  Depth 1.5–2 in to reach moisture; never deeper than 2 in.
- Seed treatment: base fungicide/insecticide treatment is less critical in warm July
  soils; include it when planting into dry, hot residue where Phytophthora is not the
  issue but stand establishment is. Inoculant on first-time fields as for
  full-season. Skip SDS seed treatments (disease pressure is lower on late-planted,
  warm-soil beans and the margin does not carry the cost).

### Fertility
- No N. P and K were applied for the wheat; do not apply more for the double crop
  unless soil test is below critical, in which case charge it to the rotation, not to
  the bean. Lime decisions are made at the wheat stage.
- IDC is generally less severe in July-planted beans (warmer, drier soils) but
  high-pH fields remain poor candidates.

### Weeds (double-crop specifics)
- Burndown at planting is mandatory: glyphosate plus 2,4-D or dicamba (observe
  pre-plant intervals for the platform used; Enlist E3 has no 2,4-D plant-back
  interval, XtendFlex none for dicamba) or paraquat plus metribuzin, applied the day
  of or the day after planting. Target: volunteer wheat, kochia, Palmer amaranth that
  emerged in the ripening wheat, crabgrass.
- PRE residual at planting: metribuzin plus a Group 15 (S-metolachlor, acetochlor or
  pyroxasulfone); K-State double-crop studies found PRE followed by POST gave greater
  Palmer amaranth control than either alone and listed metribuzin among residuals
  that help; 2,4-D alone gave highly variable Palmer and waterhemp control. Group 14
  (flumioxazin/sulfentrazone) is useful but watch label plant-back and injury risk
  on stressed seedlings.
- POST: platform herbicide plus glufosinate plus a layered Group 15 by V3–V4, which
  will be late July; the R1 cutoff for 2,4-D choline and dicamba (K-State 2026 table)
  arrives fast on July-planted beans, often within 4–5 weeks of emergence.
- Palmer amaranth that is 6 in or taller in the stubble at harvest is a no-go signal:
  the burndown will not reliably control it and the crop canopy will not close.

### Insects and diseases (double-crop specifics)
- Podworm (corn earworm) is the main insect risk: double-crop beans flower in August
  when moth flights peak; scout from R2 and use the K-State threshold (2–3 pods fed on
  per plant with larvae present). Budget for one insecticide pass in about half of
  years (verify with field history).
- Stink bugs and grasshoppers move into late green fields as surrounding crops dry.
- Dectes stem borer adults are still laying eggs in late June and July; late-planted
  fields can be heavily infested — harvest promptly at maturity.
- Charcoal rot risk is higher because pod fill lands in late August–September heat.
- Frost: MG 4.5 planted 1 July in Wichita typically reaches R7 in early-to-mid
  October; the average first 28°F freeze is late October to early November (verify
  with weather file), so frost loss is uncommon but seed size is reduced in cool
  Septembers.

### Harvest
- Late October to mid-November; shorter plants and lower pods require a flex or
  draper header run low and slow; expect higher header loss than full-season.
- Harvest moisture and storage rules are as in the full-season file; cool November
  air dries slowly, so the window below 11% is rarely an issue; wet November weather
  and shatter are.
- Wheat is not normally planted back after double-crop soybeans in this county;
  the field goes to sorghum or corn the next spring.

## Machinery and labor requirements
- The binding constraint is timeliness, not hours: planting must follow the combine
  by 0–3 days. Plan a dedicated tractor/drill operator during wheat harvest or accept
  that double-crop acres are limited to what can be drilled in the evenings.
- Hours per acre (approx.; verify with equipment-inventory.md): drilling 0.12–0.15;
  spraying 0.08 per pass; harvest 0.15–0.25 (slower in short, low-podded beans).
- Custom drilling as a relief valve: Kansas custom row planting averaged $24.71/acre
  in 2026 (Kansas Custom Rates 2026); drilling rate not read — verify.

## Marketing
- Same outlets and basis logic as the full-season file. Double-crop beans are
  harvested in November when harvest basis has usually begun to recover and the
  Jan/Mar carry is visible (Jan 2027 futures were about 15 cents over Nov 2026 on
  2026-10-29); storing to January often pays if bins are empty after wheat.
- Do not forward-contract double-crop bushels before emergence; yield uncertainty is
  too high. Price them with the full-season crop after a stand is established, or
  hold unpriced under RP coverage if insurable.
- Price reference, fall 2026: Kansas cash $11.47–11.72/bu (AMS, 2026-10-23); USDA
  2026/27 season-average forecast $12.00/bu (ERS, Sept 2026).

## Crop insurance (double-crop specifics)
- Kansas double-crop soybeans became insurable in central Kansas counties including
  Sedgwick several years ago (Hillsboro Free Press, background). The RMA Topeka
  Regional Office 2024 list showed Sedgwick County "Coverage Available" for double
  cropping soybeans; in the 2026 Topeka list the Sedgwick row appears blank in the
  extract, and the 2026 map includes categories "Available for Irrigated Double
  Cropping Without Written Agreement" and "Written Agreement Required". Status for
  2027 must be confirmed with the agent before the March 15 sales closing date
  (verify date) — this is the single most important open item for the double-crop
  decision.
- If coverage is available: soybeans "following another crop" (FAC) practice is
  reported separately from "not following another crop" (NFAC); separate APH
  databases; the final planting date for double-crop soybeans in Kansas is listed as
  July 5 with a 10-day late planting period (1%/day reduction), versus 25 days for
  full-season (Harris 2025; Lund & Smith 2026; verify for Sedgwick).
- If coverage requires a written agreement: RMA's standardized "Following Another
  Crop Blanket Written Agreement" exists for some states but the 2026 document states
  FAC practice was not available for 2026 in the states it covered (IL, IN, OH), and
  Kansas is handled by the Topeka office's own lists; a written agreement typically
  needs 3 years of double-crop production history ("Written Agreement - 3 Years
  History" is one of the Topeka list categories).
- Interaction with the wheat policy: harvesting wheat for grain and then planting
  soybeans does not affect the wheat indemnity; but if wheat is destroyed or
  released early, soybeans planted on that acreage may be treated as first crop /
  second crop under the double-cropping provisions (reduced second-crop indemnity
  unless the county is in the double-crop list) — verify with the agent.
- Uninsured double-crop acres: acceptable only if the direct cost at risk (about
  $200/acre) times the acres is within the risk budget in
  `knowledge/finance/risk-management-framework.md`.

## Risk profile
- Weather: the entire crop depends on July–August rainfall; a dry late June kills the
  decision before it starts; a dry August produces 1–1.5 seeds per pod (K-State yield
  estimation guidance) and yields below 10 bu/acre.
- Moisture debt to the next crop: double-crop beans use water that summer fallow
  would have stored for next year's sorghum or corn; in dry falls the following
  crop's yield penalty can exceed the double-crop profit (K-State MF3461 discusses
  this trade-off; quantify locally — verify).
- Weeds: Palmer amaranth seed production in a thin, short, late canopy is a
  multi-year cost; a failed double crop that is abandoned must still be sprayed or
  mowed before pigweed sets seed.
- Timeliness: late wheat harvest removes the window; wet November harvest reduces
  quality and delays fall field work.
- Price and policy: as for full-season; the small expected margin makes the practice
  more sensitive to a $1.00/bu move than full-season beans.

## Rotation effects
- Next crop: sorghum or corn, receiving the soybean N credit (about 40 lb N/acre,
  K-State; worth roughly $20–36/acre at fall 2026 N prices) minus the moisture
  penalty noted above. Do not follow double-crop soybeans with wheat in the same
  fall unless harvest finishes by late October and the soil is moist.
- Herbicide carryover into the double crop from the wheat: ALS herbicides used in
  wheat (chlorsulfuron, metsulfuron, sulfosulfuron, pyroxsulam) carry plant-back
  intervals to soybeans that are longer on high-pH soils and in dry years — check
  the wheat herbicide record for each field before committing. 2,4-D and MCPA
  applied to wheat in spring are not a concern by June.
- Disease: SCN and charcoal rot populations are maintained by any soybean crop; a
  wheat–double-crop soybean–sorghum sequence still gives one soybean crop every three
  years, which is acceptable for SCN management with resistant varieties.

## The go/no-go decision rule
Run this check field by field during wheat harvest, on the day the combine finishes
each field. All of A–E must be true to plant.

A. Calendar: planting can be completed by 5 July (prefer 30 June). After 5 July: no.
B. Moisture: soil moist at 1.5–2 in across the field AND (1 in or more of rain in the
   prior 10 days OR 50%+ forecast chance of 0.5 in or more within 7 days). Dry
   seedbed with no rain in the forecast: no (exception: before 25 June, planting
   into dry soil is allowed if the operator accepts a 45% success probability).
C. Weeds: Palmer amaranth and kochia in the stubble are under 4–6 in and the burndown
   can be applied within 48 hours of planting. Escaped 8-in-plus Palmer: no (spray
   or mow the stubble instead).
D. Economics: expected yield for the planting date (24 bu/acre by 25 June; 20 by
   5 July; cut by one third if B is met only on the forecast leg) times the current
   Nov futures plus expected harvest basis (-0.25 to -0.60) must exceed the direct
   cost ($200/acre planning figure; update from quotes) plus a $30/acre risk margin.
   At $11.50 net that requires an expected 20 bu/acre, so the rule passes for
   planting by about 30 June in a moist year and fails after that unless prices are
   above about $12.50 or the profile is unusually full.
E. Insurance and risk budget: either the double-crop practice is insurable for the
   field (RP, 65–70%) or the uninsured direct cost at risk fits within the risk
   budget for the year.
If A–E pass: drill MG 4.2–4.9 Enlist E3 at 150,000–180,000 seeds/acre in 15-in or
drilled rows, burndown plus PRE the same day, POST by V4, scout for podworm from R2,
harvest first among soybean fields if Dectes is found.
If any fail: leave the stubble (chemical fallow, control Palmer before seed set) and
bank the moisture for next year's sorghum or corn; alternatively consider a
forage/cover crop for grazing if the wheat-grazing enterprise wants it.

## Comparison vs alternatives after wheat
- Chemical fallow to sorghum/corn: zero cost, maximum stored moisture; the default
  when B or D fails. The N credit ($20–36/acre) and the double-crop margin ($0–80 in
  trend years) are what fallow gives up.
- Double-crop grain sorghum: lower seed cost, better drought tolerance, but needs
  60–80 lb N (about $40–55/acre at fall 2026 prices), has a frost-sensitive grain
  fill, a later/wetter harvest and no N credit; K-State generally favors soybeans
  over sorghum as the double crop where moisture allows (eUpdate 1105). Prefer
  double-crop sorghum only on class C soils when N is cheap (urea under about
  $450/ton) and sorghum price is strong relative to soybeans.
- Summer forage (sorghum-sudan, millet) for hay or grazing: consider when the
  livestock/grazing enterprise needs feed; costs $40–60/acre and uses as much water
  as the beans.

## Decision rules for this farm (summary)
1. Treat double-crop soybeans as an opportunistic enterprise, not a planned one;
   budget acres at zero in the base plan and add them in the favorable scenario.
2. Confirm RMA double-crop insurability for Sedgwick County and the final planting
   date before March 15 each year; record in `knowledge/regulatory/` and here.
3. Pre-buy or reserve 160,000 seeds/acre of MG 4.2–4.9 Enlist E3 seed for up to the
   wheat acres that pass a pre-harvest screen (deep soils, clean stubble, no
   ALS-carryover risk), on returnable terms where the dealer allows.
4. Apply the five-part go/no-go rule above, field by field, on the day wheat harvest
   ends on that field; log the decision and the inputs in the plan file.
5. Harvest the wheat tall (10–14 in), spread residue, drill within 3 days, burndown
   plus two-mode PRE residual the same day.
6. Never plant after 10 July; never plant into an escaped Palmer amaranth stand.
7. Account for the practice at the rotation level: credit the N to next year's
   sorghum/corn, debit the moisture, and charge no land or P/K to the beans.
8. Close the data gaps: Sedgwick County wheat harvest dates 2015–2025; farm-level
   double-crop yield records; K-State double-crop budget totals; local drilling and
   harvest custom rates; the June–August rainfall versus yield relationship from
   Wichita station data.
