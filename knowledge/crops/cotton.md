## Summary verdict
Cotton grows in Sedgwick County. K-State ran harvest-aid trials at Mount Hope and
Bentley, both in Sedgwick County, in 2023, and the PCCA cotton warehouse is in
Clearwater, also in Sedgwick County. Two co-op gins are within about 45–60 miles. At
the planning prices below, dryland cotton on a typical Sedgwick County acre is **$63/ac
behind dryland grain sorghum in the base case with custom harvest**. It wins only in a
favorable year (+$88/ac) and loses much more in an adverse year (−$117/ac). Cotton
becomes the better crop when expected lint yield is at least about 720 lb/ac at 68 ¢, or
the net lint price is at least about 79 ¢ at 600 lb. Owning a used on-board-module
stripper does not pay below about 1,000 cotton acres at base yields. An older basket
stripper with a boll buggy and module builder breaks even near 375 acres, but it carries
more labor and reliability risk. Fit: **agronomically marginal but workable;
economically a minority or test crop** until the farm has a gin contract, a custom
harvester and field yield history.

## Fit for this operation

**Why it can fit**
- South-central Kansas is the northern edge of the Cotton Belt, and it is the warmest
  part of the state for cotton. K-State says cumulative cotton heat units are "generally
  greatest in south central Kansas" (eUpdate 663).
- Kansas has grown 77,000–124,000 harvested acres a year from 2022 to 2026 (NASS). Four
  large gins operate in the state, two of them (Southern Kansas Cotton Growers, Winfield
  and Anthony) about 45–60 miles from Wichita. PCCA's 470,000 sq ft Clearwater warehouse
  (opened 2021) consolidates bales from Winfield, Anthony and Pratt.
- Cotton is a deep-rooted, heat-loving dryland crop. It tolerates midsummer drought by
  shedding fruit and recovering, and its water use per dollar is competitive with
  sorghum in hot, dry years. It also adds a non-grain market, which diversifies away
  from China sorghum exposure (it brings its own China exposure; see Risk profile).
- It needs little new equipment for establishment. It plants with the existing
  row-crop planter (cotton seed plates or discs) and is sprayed with the existing
  sprayer. Harvest is the gap.

**Disqualifiers and cautions**
- **No harvest equipment.** The combine cannot harvest cotton. A custom stripper
  commitment, or a stripper purchase, is needed before planting.
- **Gin access and capacity** must be secured before planting. Gin membership,
  equity and pool rules are unknown.
- **Thermal limit.** Late planting (after about June 5), a cool May, or an early freeze
  (average Oct 21–28) cuts yield and micronaire.
- **Herbicide plant-back conflicts with the sorghum and wheat program** (atrazine,
  Huskie and wheat sulfonylureas; see Weeds). This is the single most important
  agronomic constraint in this rotation.
- **Drift sensitivity.** Cotton varieties tolerant to 2,4-D (Enlist) are not tolerant
  to dicamba, and dicamba-tolerant (XtendFlex) varieties are not 2,4-D tolerant. Spring
  2,4-D and dicamba use on neighbouring wheat, pasture and sorghum is common.
- **Harvest timing** runs from November through December. That is too late to plant
  wheat behind cotton in the same fall, so cotton fits after wheat (from fallowed
  stubble) or before sorghum and soybeans, not before wheat.

### Variety selection
- **Maturity is the first choice:** early or very early only. In the K-State SW KS
  trial (KAES 2025), very early PHY 205 W3FE out-yielded early PHY 332 W3FE (and was
  shorter, 30 vs 36 in at season end). PHY 205 W3FE is marketed as a fit for the Texas
  Panhandle, Oklahoma and "parts of Kansas". DP 2115 B3XF and NG 3195 B3XF appear in
  other states' trials, but no Kansas data were found (**verify**).
- **Stripper vs picker types:** stripper-harvested varieties need good storm tolerance
  (bolls hold lint in wind before harvest) and a compact plant. Picker types are bred
  for open, fluffy bolls. In Kansas use storm-tolerant, stripper-adapted early varieties
  unless a picker harvester is contracted.

### Weeds
Palmer amaranth is the key weed. Kansas Palmer populations are resistant to as many
as six sites of action, including glyphosate, ALS, atrazine and HPPD, and one
population is resistant to dicamba and 2,4-D (K-State eUpdate 1080; K-State 2025
sorghum study). Kochia is in fallow and on field edges.

**Program skeleton (overlapping residuals; product choices to verify against K-State
SRP1194 2026 and the labels):**
1. **Fallow and burndown (wheat stubble, fall to spring):** glyphosate plus dicamba or
   2,4-D, observing preplant intervals for the trait chosen (non-tolerant cotton needs
   a 21-day/1-in-rain interval after dicamba and 30 days after 2,4-D, typical labels,
   **verify**). Paraquat where kochia or Palmer is glyphosate resistant. Flumioxazin
   (Valor) preplant has a cotton interval that depends on tillage and rate (**verify**).
2. **PRE at planting:** a dinitroaniline (pendimethalin/Prowl H2O, trifluralin where
   incorporated) and/or prometryn (Caparol) or fluometuron (Cotoran); fluridone
   (Brake) is an option with long rotation restrictions.
3. **Early POST (Palmer under 3–4 in):** glufosinate (Liberty) or Enlist One (W3FE) or
   dicamba (XtendFlex, 2026 label) **plus a layered residual** such as acetochlor
   (Warrant) or S-metolachlor (Dual Magnum) so there is no gap (about 4 weeks of
   residual per application).
4. **Mid-POST or layby:** a second glufosinate/auxin pass with a residual. Hooded or
   layby diuron or prometryn where labelled.
5. Hand-rogue escapes. Never let Palmer set seed in a cotton year, because the
   seedbank carries into sorghum, where POST Palmer options are weak.

**Dicamba and 2,4-D label status (2026):**
- EPA issued time-limited OTT registrations on **2026-02-06** for Engenia, Tavium and
  Stryax on dicamba-tolerant cotton and soybeans for the **2026 and 2027 seasons**, with
  temperature-based cutoffs, a maximum of 0.5 lb ae per application and 1.0 lb ae per
  year (summaries by SDSU, Ohio State and Arkansas extension, 2026). A petition
  challenging the registration is pending, and a court stay would remove OTT dicamba
  mid-season, as happened in 2020 and 2024. Kansas state registration and any
  state-specific cutoff: **verify with KDA**. Plan the 2027 program so it does not
  depend on dicamba.
- Enlist One and Enlist Duo (2,4-D choline) on Enlist cotton: registered (EPA
  registration through 2029 per the 2022 decision, **verify**); ESA county
  restrictions apply (check Bulletins Live! Two for Sedgwick County).

Practical rule for this farm: the cleanest cotton slot is **wheat (harvest June) →
fallow (glyphosate and auxins, no long-residual SUs on that wheat) → cotton the

atrazine sorghum.

### Diseases
- **Seedling disease** (Rhizoctonia, Pythium, Thielaviopsis) in cool, wet, no-till
  soils: fungicide seed treatment and planting at ≥64 °F soil temperature are the main
  controls.
- **Verticillium and Fusarium wilt:** variety tolerance matters on fields with history.
  Cotton root rot (Phymatotrichopsis) is not a Kansas problem (general; verify).
- **Bacterial blight:** use resistant varieties. **Boll rots** in wet falls.
- Root-knot nematode is limited in Kansas (verify by soil sampling on sandy fields).
- Foliar fungicides are rarely economic in dryland Kansas cotton (judgment; verify).

### Growth regulation, harvest aids and freeze
- **Mepiquat chloride (Pix-type PGR):** dryland Kansas cotton rarely needs it; use it
  when rank growth follows June–July rain or on irrigated fields, to hold height and
  hasten maturity. Budget about $0–8/ac (estimate).
- **Harvest aids:** time at **60 % open bolls** (the common Kansas method) or by seed
  maturity of the uppermost harvestable boll. Applying too early lowers seed weight,
  oil and protein, and cuts the cottonseed payment, which is often 15–20 % of gross
  (K-State eUpdate 609/665). Use high carrier volume. Stripper fields need a
  **defoliant plus boll opener (ethephon-based) followed by a desiccant** (paraquat or
  sodium chlorate) so that green leaf and bark do not drive down leaf grade. Many
  Kansas growers let a killing freeze desiccate the crop and then strip.
- **Freeze risk:** a freeze before bolls mature (late plantings, cool years) leaves
  immature fiber, low micronaire and light seed. Kansas mic averaged about 3.7–4.0 in
  2025, close to the 3.5 discount line. A 28 °F freeze ends the season; time harvest
  aids against the forecast first freeze, not just boll opening.

## Risk profile
- **Weather:** heat-limited at both ends of the season (cool May, early freeze).
  Drought cuts yield but cotton recovers from midsummer stress better than corn. Hail
  can destroy the crop, but cotton replants poorly after about June 5 (switch the field
  to sorghum). Wind and snow on open cotton before harvest cause lint loss. Yield
  variability is higher than sorghum's: state yields ranged 499–966 lb from 2022 to
  2026 (a 1.9× spread, similar to sorghum's 39–90 bu), but the cotton adverse case
  costs more because the direct cost is about $75/ac higher and harvest is a cash cost.
- **Pests:** Palmer resistance (program cost and failure risk), thrips, fleahopper;
  aphid stickiness.
- **Drift:** 2,4-D/dicamba from the farm's own wheat and sorghum programs and from
  neighbors. One damaging event can cost the crop. A Kansas drift complaint goes
  through KDA.
- **Price:** ICE volatility; the farm-level price depends on quality discounts; the
  loan floor of 55 ¢ (less discounts) limits the adverse case.
- **Counterparty and logistics:** gin capacity and terms, custom harvester
  availability and timeliness (the largest operational risk for a new grower), module
  hauling.
- **Geopolitics:** China tariffs and purchases (`knowledge/geopolitics/watchlist.md`
  W-004 currently covers only sorghum and soybeans; **add a cotton row** if cotton
  enters a plan); Brazil export growth; textile tariffs (Section 301) affect
  mill-demand routing. Inputs: N and P exposure is lower per acre than sorghum's
  (60 vs about 100 lb N); herbicide supply is China-linked
  (glufosinate, 2,4-D; watchlist W-007).
- **Regulatory:** dicamba OTT litigation; ESA herbicide label limits; atrazine label
  changes (these affect the sorghum side of the rotation and therefore cotton
  eligibility).

## Rotation effects
- **Cotton after wheat (best slot):** wheat harvest in June, about 11 months of fallow,
  cotton in May. This uses stored moisture and residue, and avoids atrazine carryover
  if the wheat herbicides allow cotton (no Finesse/Glean-type SUs on that wheat).
- **Cotton after sorghum: usually blocked** by June atrazine (and possibly Huskie or
  mesotrione) carryover. Allowed only if the sorghum PRE was atrazine-free or applied
  by June 10 at a low rate, and the label allows it (verify).
- **Cotton after soybeans:** agronomically fine; check soybean herbicide labels
  (imazethapyr/Pursuit and chlorimuron have cotton intervals).
- **After cotton:** cotton is harvested Nov–Dec, too late for wheat in Sedgwick County
  that fall. Follow with sorghum or soybeans the next spring (cotton residue is light,
  and stalks should be shredded and destroyed for boll weevil and pink bollworm
  program compliance where required; Kansas boll weevil program status: verify). Grass
  crop after cotton breaks broadleaf herbicide pressure. Cotton leaves little residue,
  so there is erosion risk on sloping fields (consider a cover after stripping, or keep
  cotton on flatter fields). There is no N credit; cotton removes less N than sorghum,
  so profile N can be higher for the next crop.
- **Rotation sequence for this farm:** wheat → fallow → cotton → sorghum (atrazine-light
  or Group 15-based PRE) → fallow → wheat, or wheat → fallow → cotton → soybeans →
  wheat. Both need the herbicide plan written across years, not crop by crop.
- **Disease:** cotton breaks the sorghum stalk-rot and shattercane cycles, and shares
  few pathogens with wheat or sorghum. Verticillium builds with cotton frequency.

   (membership, equity, rate sheet, seed policy, pool options, module hauling); (b) a
   named custom harvester with a per-lb or per-acre rate, harvest-order position and
   header row spacing matching the planter; (c) insurance agent quote with T-yield and
   STAX/ECO options; (d) seed order with a trait chosen to fit the farm's own
   auxin program.
4. **Start small:** first year 80–200 acres on 1–3 eligible fields, custom harvested, to
   build APH and quality history. Expand only after two seasons with realized net ≥
   sorghum on the same farm.
5. **Equipment rule:** do not buy a stripper until the 3-year cotton plan is ≥ 400
   acres per year (7460-class set) or ≥ 1,000 acres per year (CS690-class), or a
   custom-harvest partner cannot be secured. Re-run table (b) with actual quotes.
6. **Replant rule:** if the cotton stand fails after June 5, replant to grain sorghum
   only if the cotton herbicides used allow sorghum (fluometuron, prometryn,
   pendimethalin and acetochlor generally do; Brake, Staple and Envoke may not, so
   verify). Otherwise use a herbicide-compatible crop or fallow.
7. **Marketing:** price up to a third of expected production by planting via a PCCA
   pool or forward contract when the net price ≥ full-cost breakeven at the field's
   adverse yield. Take the CCC loan or an LDP when the AWP is below the loan rate.
8. **Trust note:** no trust checklist is needed for planting a commodity crop on
   trust land operated as now. The checklist in `family-trust-constraints.md` **is**
   required if (a) a stripper or module equipment is financed with trust land pledged
   as collateral, (b) gin co-op membership puts equity or liability in the trust's
   name, or (c) the farm starts custom stripping for others (new business line).
