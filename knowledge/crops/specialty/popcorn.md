# Popcorn

> Research note (2026-11-23): WebFetch to NASS, ESMIS and RMA was blocked in this
> environment; figures come from search-engine summaries of the cited documents. Anything
> marked **verify** is unconfirmed. Net per acre in this file means gross revenue minus
> direct (variable) costs, excluding land and machinery ownership, the same basis used for
> the sorghum comparison below.

## 1. Fit for this farm
- **Climate and water.** Popcorn is a corn (Zea mays everta) with the same heat and
  drought sensitivity at pollination as field corn and a smaller, lower-yielding plant.
  South-central Kansas dryland corn is already the riskiest summer crop in the rotation
  (see `knowledge/crops/corn-dryland.md`); dryland popcorn inherits that risk with no
  insurance certainty (popcorn RMA coverage in Sedgwick County **verify**). It is a real
  option only on an irrigated field or the best water-holding upland.
- **Rotation.** Fits where corn fits: after wheat or soybeans. Follow with soybeans or
  wheat. Atrazine-based corn programs are usable but the next crop restrictions are the
  same as for sorghum (see sorghum file).
- **Equipment.** The farm's planter and sprayer work. The combine needs a corn head
  (assumed owned for field corn **verify**), low cylinder/rotor speed and lowered augers
  to avoid cracking kernels (John Deere popcorn checklist). Lacks: a popcorn-capable
  cleaner/gravity table, a low-temperature dryer or aeration-only bin to bring kernels to
  13–14% without stress cracking, and an identity-preserved bin.
- **Market fit.** No popcorn processor was found within reach of Sedgwick County. The
  nearest large contractor is Preferred Popcorn (Chapman NE, ~280 miles, **verify**
  distance and whether it contracts Kansas acres). Raw popcorn without a contract has no
  elevator outlet.
- **Trust and labor fit.** Growing is ordinary agriculture. Every finished product is food
  processing and belongs in the separate LLC off trust land (default structure). The
  retail products (kettle corn, gift tins) are labor-heavy weekend and holiday work, a poor
  fit for a farm with no dedicated labor unless a family member takes them on.
- **Commitment.** Annual; easy to exit.

## 2. Agronomy summary
- **Hybrids.** Contract processors normally supply hybrid seed (yellow butterfly,
  mushroom, white). Heirloom colored types (red, blue, black, Ladyfinger) are
  open-pollinated or low-yielding hybrids from specialty seed houses (**verify** sources
  and yields).
- **Isolation.** Popcorn crossed with field, sweet or dent corn loses popping quality;
  keep 200+ ft isolation or a planting-date offset of 2+ weeks from neighbors' corn
  (**verify** distance against Purdue NCH-5).
- **Planting.** Late April to mid-May at soil temperature ~50 °F; 24,000–30,000 seeds/ac
  dryland (**verify**); 30-in rows with the existing planter.
- **Fertility.** N by yield goal at roughly the field-corn rate per bushel-equivalent,
  P and K by soil test (K-State MF2586).
- **Weeds.** Popcorn is more sensitive than field corn to some herbicides; a UNL trial saw
  4–8% injury from PRE products and none from POST, with 42% yield loss when weeds
  competed. Check every label for popcorn; many ALS and HPPD products exclude it or carry
  hybrid-sensitivity warnings (**verify** per product).
- **Pests.** Same as corn (corn earworm, western bean cutworm, rootworm if corn-on-corn);
  popcorn hybrids generally lack Bt traits (**verify**). Ear rots raise mycotoxin risk.
- **Harvest.** At physiological maturity, combine when kernels have field-dried toward
  16–18% (**verify**; not confirmed in this research) and finish to 13–14% with
  low-temperature air (UNL 2026). Gentle handling is mandatory: cracked or stress-checked
  kernels do not pop.
- **Cleaning and storage.** Air-screen cleaning plus a gravity table to remove light
  and damaged kernels; store at 13–14% in sealed containers or bins with aeration.
  Over-drying destroys expansion.

**Comparison to dryland sorghum on the same acre.** Sorghum base: 75 bu x $4.00/bu (KC cash

gives $295). Popcorn base **$115/ac** is roughly equal to sorghum with far higher drought,
contract and freight risk, so raw popcorn does not beat sorghum on a risk-adjusted basis.

## 4. Raw-crop market
- Buyers: Preferred Popcorn (Chapman NE, farmer-owned, ~17,000 contracted acres in
  Nebraska in an older report); Indiana processors (Weaver, Palmyra IN plant); Nebraska is
  the #2 state after Indiana (UNL). None found in Kansas (**verify** with KDA and From the
  Land of Kansas).
- Terms: fixed price per cwt delivered, sometimes indexed to corn; processor supplies or
  specifies the hybrid; quality discounts for expansion, moisture and damage.
- Volume ceiling: whatever one contract allows; processors favor irrigated growers close
  to the plant.
- Volatility: contract prices are stable within a year but acres are cut when processors
  carry stocks.

## 5. Finished products
Default structure: the LLC buys clean popcorn from the trust farm at the contract-equivalent
price ($0.18/lb, $450/ac base) and processes off trust land. Labor at FAMILY $11/hr;
HIRED_SEASONAL $18/hr shown as a sensitivity. Uplift = product revenue - variable - labor
- raw crop at market value - fixed share, per acre of popcorn processed; net per acre
equivalent in the CSV = raw net ($115) + uplift.

**P1. Heirloom colored kernels, 2-lb retail bag.**
Process: clean, gravity-sort, condition to 13–14%, bag, label. Equipment: used air-screen
cleaner and small gravity table (~$8,000 **verify**), scale and heat sealer ($1,500),
label printer ($500), sealed storage totes ($3,000), test popper and moisture meter
($1,000); stage-1 capital ~$14,000. Heirloom yield 2,000 lb/ac (penalty vs yellow,
**verify**) = 1,000 bags. Price $7.00 per 2-lb bag blended retail/online (Amish Country
colored kernels $15.99 per 2 x 2-lb, Walmart, accessed 2026-11-23). Variable $1.10/bag
(bag, label, market and card fees, shipping share). Labor 53 hr/acre-eq ($583 FAMILY).
Fixed $5,200/yr (depreciation, liability insurance, license, space) over 3 acres.
Uplift **$3,134/ac** ($2,763 at HIRED). Market ceiling ~3 acres (3,000 bags/yr through
Wichita markets, gift shops, online). Regulatory: KDA food processing license unless the
direct-to-consumer exemption applies (K.S.A. 65-689(d)(4), **verify** scope for packaged
kernels). Competitors: Amish Country, Pop Pop and national heirloom brands online.
Verdict: **test** (1 acre, irrigated if possible, presold via the buyer network).

**P2. Kettle corn, 8-oz bag at farmers markets and events.**
Process: pop in a 160-qt kettle with sugar, oil and salt; cool, bag, sell. Capital ~$9,000
(kettle and burner, sifting table, canopy, sealer; **verify**, no popper quote found).
1 lb kernels makes ~2.4 bags; 2,125 lb clean per acre = 5,100 bags. Price $5 per 8-oz bag
(Kernel Dan's). Variable $0.75/bag (sugar, oil, propane, bag, booth fees, card fees).
Labor 0.09 hr/bag = 459 hr/acre-eq ($5,049). Fixed $2,500/yr over 0.8 acre. Uplift
**$13,051/acre-eq** ($9,838 at HIRED) but the market caps it at ~0.8 acre (about 4,000
bags/yr over ~30 market days), i.e. roughly **$10,000/yr** of margin for ~370 hours of
weekend work. Regulatory: KDA food establishment or mobile unit license, local event
permits, sales tax (**verify**). Competitors: established kettle-corn vendors at every
Wichita market and event. Verdict: **test** only if a family member wants the weekend
business; the farm-grown kernel contributes ~$0.08 of a $5 bag.

**P3. Wholesale 50-lb kernels to concessions, schools, fundraisers.**
Capital ~$10,000 (cleaning line shared with P1). 2,125 lb clean/ac at $0.70/lb ($35 per
50-lb bag). Commodity competition is fierce: Reist 50-lb $26.99 and Snappy $28.00
($0.54–0.56/lb) at WebstaurantStore; branded concession bags $69–150. Variable $0.06/lb
(bag, delivery, testing). Labor 6 hr/ac. Fixed $3,500/yr over 10 acres. Uplift
**$494/ac**. Ceiling ~15 acres (independent concessions, school boosters, fundraisers;
theater chains buy on national contracts). Regulatory: KDA food processing license for
packaged kernels (**verify**). Verdict: **test** with 2–3 fundraiser and concession
accounts before planting; price above $0.60/lb is the hurdle.

**P4. Pre-popped flavored gift tins (holiday).**
Process: caramel/cheese/plain popped corn in a rented licensed kitchen; 1-gal tins.
Capital ~$6,000 (caramel mixer, sealer, smallwares). ~0.4 lb kernels per tin; price $20
per tin (**verify**; no benchmark retrieved). Variable $7.55/tin (tin, flavorings,
labels, kitchen rent, fees). Labor 0.15 hr/tin. Fixed $2,000/yr over 0.25 acre. Uplift
looks enormous per acre-eq (**$48,917**) only because a quarter-acre makes ~1,300 tins;
the holiday market caps it at ~0.25 acre, about **$12,000/yr**, entirely dependent on
gift-buyer demand. Regulatory: KDA food processing license, licensed kitchen, allergen
labels. Competitors: national gift tin brands, Wichita confectioners. Verdict: **test**
as an add-on to P1/P2 only.

Screened out: microwave popcorn (susceptor bags, co-packer minimums, PFAS-free packaging
issues; no co-packer within reach) — **skip**.

## 6. Risks and disqualifiers
- **Top disqualifier: no raw-popcorn buyer within reach.** Without a processor contract,
  every bushel must go through the LLC's own small retail channels, which absorb only
  ~4 acres in total.
- Dryland drought and heat at pollination; insurance availability **verify**.
- Kernel damage at harvest and drying ruins popping quality; the farm has no
  low-temperature drying experience with popcorn.
- Isolation from neighbors' corn.
- Retail products are labor-heavy (hundreds of hours) and depend on a family member.
- Food liability; keep in the LLC.

## 7. Decision rules
1. Grow contract popcorn only with a signed processor contract that covers freight or
   nets at least $0.17/lb at the farm, and only on irrigated or top-quartile fields.
2. Grow heirloom popcorn for the LLC only on 0.5–1 acre and only after 60% of expected
   bags are covered by letters of intent (buyer-network rule 1).
3. Kettle corn and gift tins go ahead only if a named family member commits the hours;
   model at HIRED $18/hr too.
4. Drop any product whose second-year sales are below half of the market ceiling used
   here.
