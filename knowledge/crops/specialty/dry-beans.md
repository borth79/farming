# Dry edible beans (pinto, black, navy, heirloom)

## 1. Fit for this farm
- **Climate**: dry beans (Phaseolus vulgaris) are a western-Kansas, Nebraska Panhandle,
  Colorado and northern-Plains crop, almost all of it irrigated. Kansas production has been
  mostly pinto under pivots in the northwest and southwest counties (Kansas Crop Profile,
  2001). Sedgwick County's July–August heat is the main problem. Blossoms drop above about
  95 °F by day or 80 °F at night (Bugwood HPIPM), and Wichita routinely sees both during the
  bloom window of a May–June planting. Humid nights also favor white mold and bacterial
  blights. On this farm, dryland beans are a yield gamble. Irrigated beans would be
  reasonable if a water right is confirmed.
- **Soil**: well-drained silt loams. Beans are sensitive to crusting, compaction and salinity.
  Avoid wet bottoms (root rots, white mold).
- **Season**: plant late May to mid-June, harvest late August to September. A June 10–20
  planting after wheat is too late in most years; heat at bloom and frost risk at the end.
  Double-cropping is not recommended.
- **Rotation**:
  - Beans fit after wheat (fall fallow, then spring beans) or after soybeans.
  - They do **not** fit the year after atrazine. Standard atrazine labels bar dry beans in
    the year after application. Most of this farm's sorghum and corn fields carry atrazine,
    so beans cannot follow them in the next season.
  - Group 2 residuals used in wheat (chlorsulfuron, metsulfuron and similar) carry
    plant-back intervals for dry beans. Check each label.
  - Beans share Sclerotinia with soybean and sunflower (3–4-year break per
    `knowledge/crops/sunflower.md`). Do not stack beans, soybeans and sunflower in a short
    rotation.
- **Equipment**:
  - **Planter and sprayers**: the existing row-crop planter works (30-in or 15-in rows).
    The existing sprayers work.
  - **Combine header**: direct harvest needs upright varieties and a flex draper header with
    an air reel and lifter guards. NDSU puts loss at 5–15% even with that setup. **Unknown:**
    whether the farm's combine has a flex draper. A rigid platform or corn head is not
    suitable. Undercutting and windrowing would need a bean cutter and windrower the farm
    lacks.
  - **Handling**: beans crack below ~16% moisture. Run the combine slowly and handle with
    belt conveyors. Augers cause splits that cost grade.
  - **Cleaning**: if the beans are sold as food, they need a cleaner, and a gravity table or
    color sorter. The farm has neither.
- **Trust and labor**: growing beans is agriculture and stays with the farm under the trust
  framework. Cleaning, bagging and selling go to the separate LLC, off trust land.
  Harvesting beans competes with sorghum and soybean harvest for the farm's single combine.
- **Commitment**: annual. Easy to test on 10–40 acres.

## 2. Agronomy summary
- **Varieties**:
  - Upright (type II) pintos selected for direct harvest in Nebraska on-farm trials:
    Torreon, Monterrey, La Paz, Sinaloa, Santa Cruz (UNL 2013, 2016).
  - Black and navy beans are type II and direct-harvest more easily. Their buyers are in
    Michigan, the Dakotas and Minnesota, far from Wichita.
  - Heirloom types (Anasazi, Jacob's Cattle, Vaquero, Good Mother Stallard and similar) are
    mostly vining type III. They suit hand or plot harvest on 1–5 acres for the retail
    product only.
  - All variety performance here is unknown. Verify with any K-State or Colorado State trial
    (Colorado State 2025 dry bean report exists; not read).
- **Planting**:
  - Soil at 60 °F or above, late May to early June.
  - Seed 50–60 lb/ac irrigated (Kansas Crop Profile). Dryland rates in that 2001 profile
    (12–15 lb/ac) look low and need checking against current guidance. This file assumes
    35 lb/ac dryland.
  - Plant 1.5–2 in deep. Rhizobium inoculation gives little N for Phaseolus, so plan on
    fertilizer N.
- **Fertility**:
  - N by soil test. Irrigated programs run to ~150 lb N/ac including credits (K-State
    guidance as cited by NDSU; verify against K-State's own publication). Dryland uses
    ~40–60 lb N (estimate).
  - Apply P and zinc by soil test. Beans are sensitive to zinc deficiency.
- **Weeds**: no atrazine. PRE options are pendimethalin, S-metolachlor, EPTC and dimethenamid;
  POST options are bentazon, imazamox, fomesafen and clethodim, as labeled for dry beans.
  Palmer amaranth with Group 2 and Group 14 resistance is the biggest risk, because the
  options are narrower than in soybeans. Clean fields only.
- **Pests and diseases**:
  - Diseases: white mold, common and halo bacterial blight, rust, root rots
    (Fusarium, Rhizoctonia), bean common mosaic virus (choose resistant varieties).
  - Insects: western bean cutworm, spider mites in hot dry weather, potato leafhopper.
- **Harvest**:
  - Desiccate at physiological maturity with a labeled harvest aid. Direct harvest in the
    morning at 16–18% moisture.
  - Deliver to a dealer or clean on farm. Store at 14–16% moisture in bins with aeration.
- **Quality**: food buyers grade on splits, color, foreign material, stones and dirt
  (US No. 1). Color darkening in storage lowers the value of pintos. Sell by spring.

## 4. Raw-crop market
- **Who buys**: dry bean dealers and processors with receiving stations in western Kansas,
  the Nebraska Panhandle (Scottsbluff/Bridgeport area) and northeast Colorado. They contract
  acres before planting at a fixed $/cwt or open price, with grade discounts for splits and
  foreign material.
  - **Gap:** no dealer within a day's haul of Wichita was identified. The nearest dealers
    are likely 250–350 miles away. Verify, and get a 2027 contract offer.
- **Terms**: acreage or production contracts, delivery at harvest or on a call schedule,
  US No. 1 grade, and a moisture spec.
- **Volume ceiling**: dealer contract acreage, likely a few hundred acres for a new grower
  (estimate). The heirloom and retail channels are tiny by comparison (section 5).
- **Competitors**: established irrigated growers in Nebraska, Colorado, the Dakotas and
  Minnesota, with lower cost and better yields.
- **Price history**: 2024–2026 bids ran from about $21–22 (low) to $42–43/cwt (high).
  Driven by Mexico demand, food-aid purchases, tariffs and northern-Plains acreage. Highly
  volatile.

## 5. Finished products
The default structure applies: the LLC buys beans from the trust farm at a documented
market price and cleans, bags and sells off trust land. Labor is costed at FAMILY $11/hr,
with HIRED_SEASONAL $18/hr shown for sensitivity. Raw crop is charged at market value
(900 lb × $0.32 = $256 per acre-equivalent).

**How the schema calculates uplift**: `net_per_acre_equiv` already subtracts the raw crop at
market value, so it is the LLC's margin. The schema then subtracts `raw_net_per_acre` again
to get `uplift_per_acre_over_raw`. The table follows the schema, so its uplift is
conservative. The whole-chain gain over selling raw equals `net_per_acre_equiv`.

### 5a. Cleaned bagged heirloom and pinto beans, 1–2 lb (retail and online)
- **Process**: clean with an air-screen cleaner, hand-pick or color sort, weigh, bag, label.
  Heirloom varieties grown on 1–5 acres, harvested by plot methods. Pintos and blacks come
  from the field crop.
- **Capital**: about $8,000. A used Clipper-type cleaner runs $0.5–6k in asking prices; add a
  legal-for-trade scale, impulse sealer and label printer. A 1-chute color sorter is
  optional, $2–5k new from China, verify.
- **Labor**: 0.06 hr/lb (clean, sort, bag, sell) × 720 lb = 43 hr = $473 at FAMILY rate
  ($774 at HIRED).
- **Raw needed**: about 1.1 lb field-run beans per 1 lb sold (10% cleanout).
- **Price**: blended $5.00/lb across retail at $6–7 and wholesale at ~$3.50.
  - Benchmark: Rancho Gordo heirloom $6.50–7.50 per bag, pack size believed 1 lb but
    undated (verify).
  - Grocery commodity pinto is far cheaper (verify current shelf price).
- **Margin**: revenue $3,600 − variable $648 (bags, labels, card and market fees, shipping
  share at $0.90/lb) − labor $473 − raw $256 − fixed share $600 ($3,000/yr ÷ 5 ac) =
  **$1,623 per acre-equivalent**. Schema uplift $1,555. At HIRED rate, $1,322.
- **Regulatory**:
  - Direct sales to consumers of a non-TCS dry food may fall under the K.S.A. 65-689(d)(4)
    exemption (verify bagged beans with KDA). Sales to stores or restaurants need a KDA food
    processing license.
  - FSMA: likely a qualified facility.
  - Labeling: net weight, name and address.
  - Allergens are not relevant, except for cross-contact if the LLC also handles soy, wheat
    or peanuts.
- **Competitors and market**: Rancho Gordo and other national heirloom brands online; bulk
  bins at Natural Grocers and Whole Foods.
  - Wichita market reach (farmers markets, a few grocers, an online store) is estimated at
    3,000–4,000 lb/yr, or **about 5 acres**.
- **Verdict: test.** High margin per pound, but small volume. Needs the buyer network. Pair
  it with the cowpea and soup-mix line on one cleaner and one label system.

### 5b. Bean soup mix, 1 lb bag with seasoning packet
- **Process**: blend 3–5 bean types (farm pinto, black, heirloom plus bought split peas or
  lentils) and add a sealed seasoning packet from a spice supplier.
- **Capital**: about $10,000 (5a equipment plus a mixing bin, packet sealer and recipe
  testing).
- **Labor**: 0.05 hr/bag × 1,000 bags = 50 hr = $550 ($900 at HIRED).
- **Raw needed**: about 0.72 lb farm beans per bag. One acre's 720 lb of cleaned beans
  makes about 1,000 bags.
- **Price**: blended $4.75/bag (retail ~$6, wholesale ~$3.75; estimate, verify against
  shelf prices of national 15-bean soup brands and local gift shops).
- **Margin**: $4,750 − variable $1,370 ($1.37/bag: bought pulses $0.22, seasoning $0.35,
  packaging $0.40, fees $0.40) − labor $550 − raw $256 − fixed $875 ($3,500 ÷ 4 ac) =
  **$1,699**. Schema uplift $1,631. At HIRED rate, $1,349.
- **Regulatory**: a multi-ingredient product needs a KDA food processing license and an
  ingredient statement. The seasoning may contain milk, soy or wheat (bouillon), so declare
  allergens or specify an allergen-free seasoning.
- **Competitors**: Hurst's HamBeens and other national brands at low price points. The local
  angle is a gift or souvenir item ("From the Land of Kansas"), sold at holiday markets.
  Estimated market about 4 acres.
- **Verdict: test** in the same LLC line as 5a. It is the best fit for heirloom variety mix
  and off-grade colors.

### 5c. Canned or refried beans via a retort co-packer, 15 oz
- **Process**: ship cleaned beans to a low-acid canned-food co-packer. The co-packer holds
  FDA LACF registration (21 CFR 113) and a process authority letter.
- **Capital**: about $5,000 for brand and label design, UPC codes and process review.
  Working capital for the first run is far larger: an MOQ of ~100,000 cans × ~$1.10
  all-in ≈ $110,000 (directory figure; verify with quotes).
- **Labor**: about 10 hr per acre-equivalent (logistics, sales).
- **Raw needed**: about 0.25 lb dry beans per can (estimate, verify), so ~3,200 cans per
  acre. One MOQ run needs ~30 acres of beans.
- **Price**: wholesale $1.60/can (estimate, verify). National brands retail around $1–2.
- **Margin**: $5,120 − $3,520 − $110 − $256 − $400 = **$834**. Schema uplift $766.
- **Regulatory**: the co-packer carries LACF. The brand owner carries recall and liability
  insurance.
- **Competitors**: Bush's, Rosarita, Ranch Style and store brands. Shelf space needs
  slotting fees and a broker.
- **Verdict: skip.** The MOQ and inventory risk outweigh the margin, and the margin
  estimate is weak.

## 6. Risks and disqualifiers
- **Top disqualifier**: no dealer or receiving station within reach of Wichita, and no RMA
  coverage in Sedgwick County without a written agreement. Dryland raw beans are an
  uninsured, freight-penalized, heat-exposed crop.
- **Atrazine plant-back**: rules out beans after most sorghum and corn fields.
- **Combine**: needs a flex draper with an air reel. Field losses and bean splits from a
  grain-set combine.
- **Heat blast at bloom; white mold in wet years.**
- **Palmer amaranth control**: weaker herbicide options than soybeans.
- **Price volatility**: $21–43/cwt in two seasons.
- **Value-added**: small market ceiling for retail and soup mix. Food-safety and licensing
  overhead falls on a small volume.

## 7. Decision rules
1. No raw dry-bean acre without a signed dealer contract that names the delivery point and
   the price. Freight to that point must leave the net price above $30/cwt.
2. Dryland beans only on a field with no atrazine in the prior 12 months, no Group 2
   carryover per label, and low Palmer pressure. Irrigated beans only after the water right
   is confirmed.
3. Direct harvest only with an upright variety and a flex draper with an air reel.
   Otherwise custom-hire or skip.
4. Value-added: grow 1–5 acres of heirloom types only after the LLC has a KDA license path,
   a cleaner, and letters of intent for at least 60% of output (buyer-network rule 1).
5. If the beans' expected return over direct cost does not exceed sorghum's ($79/ac) by
   $50/ac at the contract price, plant sorghum.
