# Lavender (bundles, oil, agritourism)

## 1. Fit for this farm
**Verdict: a retail and agritourism product, not a crop. Agronomic survival on
Sedgwick County soils is the first question, and the trust's view of public access the
second. Test at most 0.25–0.5 acre in raised beds through the LLC.**

- **Climate and soil.** Lavender wants lean, sharply drained, alkaline-to-neutral soil
  and dry air. Sedgwick County's heavier silt and clay loams, wet springs, humid summer
  nights and freeze-thaw cycles cause crown and root rot and winterkill. Growers in
  Burrton and Buhler sit on Arkansas River sand-hills soils, which is probably why they
  succeed (**inference; verify** with them). On this farm it would need raised, gravel-
  mulched beds or a sandy field.
- **Types.** *L. angustifolia* ('Munstead', 'Hidcote', 'Folgate', 'Royal Velvet') for
  culinary use, bundles and fine oil. Lavandin (*L. × intermedia*: 'Grosso', 'Provence',
  'Phenomenal') for big bundles and oil. 'Phenomenal' is noted for heat and humidity
  tolerance (**verify**).
- **Equipment.** The farm's tractor can build beds and its mower handles alleys, but
  planting, weeding, harvest and bundling are hand work. A still is needed for oil. The
  grain combine has no role.
- **Herbicide drift.** Lavender is a broadleaf; 2,4-D and dicamba drift can injure it.
- **Labor.** Harvest in June (a second flush is possible), coinciding with wheat
  harvest. ~260 hr/ac at full production (bundle cutting and tying plus maintenance;
  estimate). The farm has no dedicated labor.
- **Perennial and trust.** **An 8–10 year planting.** It is a smaller, removable
  improvement than an orchard (beds and drip), but still needs trustee approval and a
  lease term covering payback plus life. The business model's value is **agritourism**
  (u-cut, festivals, photo sessions, a shop). That means public access, parking and
  retail on trust land, which the trust framework flags as restricted
  (non-agricultural use, liability, zoning, §2032A and property-tax effects). It must run
  in the LLC with insurance and explicit trustee consent, or not at all.

## 2. Agronomy summary
- **Planting.** Plugs in spring, 18–36 in apart in rows 5–6 ft apart (~2,700–4,500
  plants/ac; modeled at 2,720). Raised beds with gravel or landscape-fabric mulch. Drip.
  No overhead irrigation.
- **Fertility.** Minimal. Excess N reduces oil and hardiness.
- **Pests and diseases.** Root and crown rot (Phytophthora), Alfalfa mosaic virus,
  Xylella (**verify** Kansas presence), spittlebugs, winter desiccation.
- **Harvest.** Bundles cut at 30–50 % bloom by hand sickle, tied (~100 stems) and hung
  to dry. Oil crops cut at full bloom and distilled fresh or wilted.
- **Life.** First small cut in year 2, full in year 3, peak in year 5, replant in years
  8–10. Annual losses of 5–15 % from rot or winterkill on heavy soil (**estimate**).

## 3. Yield and raw-crop economics
Basis: 1 acre in raised beds. "Raw" means wholesale dried bundles to florists, gift
shops and other lavender farms' shops. **Establishment $12,000/ac in year 1** (2,720
plugs at ~$2.25, beds and fabric, drip $2,000, planting labor; estimate within the
vendor range of $8,000–20,000). Mature costs $2,600/ac/yr (maintenance ~100 hr × $18 plus
replacement plants and inputs ~$800) plus $0.99 per bundle for cutting, tying and
drying. Yield 1.2 bundles per plant after losses (3,240 bundles; well below the Ohio
example of 2.5). Price $3.50 wholesale (**estimate; no benchmark found**). Amortized at
6 % over 10 years ($1,630/ac/yr).

**Versus dryland sorghum.** Sorghum nets ~$80/ac over direct costs (county trend) and
$295/ac at the K-State base (`grain-sorghum.md`). Per acre, lavender looks ~50 times
higher, but the realistic planting is a fraction of an acre, so the whole-farm effect is
a few thousand dollars a year.

## 4. Raw-crop market
- **Wholesale dried bundles and buds.** Florists, gift and home-décor shops, and craft
  makers. Small volumes. Price undiscovered (**verify** by calling Wichita florists and
  Kansas lavender farms).
- **Oil.** Bulk lavender oil trades at ~$20–40/lb wholesale (imports from Bulgaria,
  France and China). One acre of lavandin gives ~53–67 lb of oil, worth ~$1,000–2,700
  in bulk, far below bundle value. **Bulk oil is not a market.**
- **Competitors (agritourism model).** Gertie's Lavender Farm (Burrton, ~35 mi NW of
  Wichita; u-pick, gift shop, festival, registered agritourism), Owl's Lavender Patch
  (Buhler), The Lavender Patch Farm (Fort Scott), Morford Lavender Farm (24 ac), and
  roughly 11 listed Kansas lavender farms. The Wichita-area niche is already served.

part-time family enterprise, not a farm line.

## 6. Risks and disqualifiers
1. **Soil and climate survival (top agronomic disqualifier).** Root rot and winterkill
   on heavy soils.
2. **Trust (top structural disqualifier).** Value depends on public access and retail
   on trust land. Requires trustee consent, LLC operation, liability insurance and
   county zoning. Kansas agritourism immunity covers inherent risks only, not willful or
   wanton conduct.
3. A saturated local niche (Gertie's, Owl's, others).
4. Hand labor in June overlaps wheat harvest.
5. Bulk oil and buds are commodity-priced against imports.

## 7. Decision rules
1. Before planting, visit Gertie's and Owl's to learn varieties, soils and losses, and
   run a 50-plant trial bed for two winters.
2. Plant more than 0.5 acre only if trial survival exceeds 85 % and retail sell-through
   is proven at a market or shop.
3. No agritourism events on trust land unless the trustee approves in writing, the
   county confirms zoning, the operation is registered with Kansas Tourism, and the LLC
   carries an event liability policy.
