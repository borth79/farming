# Wine grapes (cold-hardy and French-American hybrids)

## 1. Fit for this farm
**Verdict: agronomically possible, but a poor fit for a grain farm. Herbicide drift from
the farm's own and neighbors' 2,4-D and dicamba is the top disqualifier, ahead of the
trust's 25–30 year commitment. Test only as a contract with a named winery on an
isolated, buffered acre.**

- **Climate.** Zone 6b–7a with hot summers is suitable for French-American hybrids
  (Chambourcin, Vignoles, Seyval, Traminette, Vidal) and some cold-hardy Minnesota
  hybrids. Hazards: spring frost after early budbreak, −10 °F winter events, hail, 100 °F+
  heat at veraison, and high wind. Pierce's disease is not a Kansas problem.
- **Soil and water.** Grapes need well-drained soil; deep upland silt loams can work, but
  wet claypans cause problems. Drip irrigation is needed in establishment years and
  drought (water right undetermined).
- **Herbicide drift (disqualifier).** Grapes are the most 2,4-D- and dicamba-sensitive
  crop in the state. The farm's sorghum and wheat program uses dicamba and 2,4-D
  (`grain-sorghum.md`). A vineyard forces the farm to change its own herbicides within
  roughly a mile, depending on product and conditions (**verify** K-State MF2588 buffer
  guidance), and still exposes it to neighbors. Ohio State calls drift "possibly the
  single greatest threat" to Midwest grapes, and a Kansas grower reported injury in 2025.
- **Equipment.** Tractor and mower are fine. It needs a vineyard sprayer (airblast or
  tunnel) for 10–15 fungicide passes, trellis, a pruning and canopy crew, and hand or
  custom mechanical harvest. The grain combine has no role.
- **Labor.** Hybrids take ~80–150 hr/ac/yr (pruning, shoot thinning, positioning,
  hedging, harvest; **estimate**). The farm has no dedicated labor.
- **Perennial and trust.** **A vineyard is a 25–30 year improvement (posts, wire, drip)
  on trust land.** It needs written trustee approval citing the trust power, a lease
  covering payback (base year ~11) plus vine life, or a reimbursement clause, and
  ownership terms for the trellis and drip. A farm winery or tasting room is a
  non-agricultural or retail use: run it through the LLC off trust land by default
  (`family-trust-constraints.md`). On trust land it needs express permission and
  counsel's confirmation of no §2032A or agricultural-use valuation harm.

## 2. Agronomy summary
- **Varieties.** Red: Chambourcin (Kansas's top variety in 2010), Norton (slow
  establishment, disease resistant), Marquette and Petite Pearl (cold-hardy, early).
  White: Vignoles, Seyval Blanc, Traminette, Vidal Blanc, Chardonel. Ask the buying
  winery what it wants before planting.
- **Establishment.** ~544–600 vines/ac (8 × 10 ft, **verify**). Vertical shoot
  positioning or high-wire cordon trellis. Drip. Weed-free strip. Train to the wire in
  years 1–2. First crop of ~1 t in year 3, full crop in years 4–5.
- **Pests.** Black rot, downy and powdery mildew, phomopsis, anthracnose, grape berry
  moth, Japanese beetle, birds and deer, crown gall after freezes.
- **Yield.** Hybrids typically 3–5 t/ac. Crop is thinned on vigorous hybrids (Seyval,
  Chambourcin) for quality (**verify** in K-State MF2370 table).

## 3. Yield and raw-crop economics
Basis: 1 acre of hybrids sold to a Kansas winery. 2026 dollars. **Establishment
$10,500/ac over 3 years** (year 1 $6,000, year 2 $2,500, year 3 $2,000 with a 1-t crop):
estimate within Bordelon's $8,000, the Midwest study's $9,500 and Cornell's $11,549, and
**to verify** against current post, wire and vine prices. Mature costs $1,700/ac/yr
cultural (sprays, pruning crew, canopy, irrigation, insurance) plus $325/t for harvest
and haul (labor at $18/hr; estimate). Amortized at 6 % over 25 years ($821/ac/yr).

2–7 times sorghum after year 5, but needs ~100+ hr/ac and a buyer. The farm's own
herbicide program on surrounding acres also has to change, at a cost not estimated
here.

## 4. Raw-crop market
- **Buyers.** Kansas had **51 farm wineries** in August 2025 (KDA). WineAmerica counts
  36 producers and 468 vineyard acres (2025). Many wineries buy Kansas fruit to meet the
  Kansas-grown requirement (30 % per the 2018 AG opinion; a 2022 bill proposed 15 %; the
  current rule must be **verified** in K.S.A. 41-316). Nearby: **Wyldewood Cellars**
  (Mulvane/Peck, Sedgwick Co.; mostly elderberry and fruit wines), Grace Hill Winery
  (Kansas; location to **verify**), and other south-central Kansas farm wineries
  (**verify** list via KDA). Buyers want specific varieties, Brix and pH, and
  delivery within hours of harvest.
- **Contracts.** Typically annual, price per ton by variety, with quality penalties.
  Multi-year contracts are rare in Kansas (**verify**). Fruit can also be sold to Missouri
  and Oklahoma wineries at freight cost.
- **Prices.** Kansas 2010 average $1,131/t (Chambourcin ~$999/t). Missouri hybrids were
  ~$700/t in the 1990s. Ontario's 2024 minimums are C$749–1,075/tonne. No Kansas price
  series after 2010 was found.
- **Volume ceiling.** Small. 51 mostly tiny wineries, many with their own vineyards.
  Assume 2–5 t per buyer, so ~1–3 acres sellable without contracts (**estimate**).

## 6. Risks and disqualifiers
1. **Herbicide drift (top disqualifier).** The farm's own 2,4-D and dicamba program and
   its neighbors' fall within damaging distance.
2. **Trust.** A 25–30 year trellis improvement on trust land, with payback in year 11.
   Winery or tasting room retail and alcohol use on trust land is restricted by default.
3. Freeze, hail, disease pressure and labor.
4. Thin, fragmented buyer base, with no current Kansas price series.
5. Kansas law changes each session (Kansas-grown percentage, self-distribution,
   shipping).

## 7. Decision rules
1. Plant no vines without (a) a written multi-year purchase agreement from a named
   winery for variety, tonnage and price; (b) a site ≥ 1 mile, or the K-State-recommended
   distance, from the farm's and neighbors' 2,4-D and dicamba fields, or a herbicide
   change agreed for those fields; (c) trustee approval and a lease ≥ 15 years with a
   trellis reimbursement clause.
2. Maximum first planting: 1 acre.
3. A farm winery is considered only in the LLC on non-trust land, only after 3 years of
   grape sales and only with a named winemaker.
