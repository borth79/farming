# Elderberry (American elderberry)

## 1. Fit for this farm
**Verdict: agronomically plausible with drip irrigation and wind protection. The best
local story in this group because the largest US elderberry processor is in Sedgwick
County. The limits are market and harvest labor, not agronomy. Test 1 acre only after a
buyer letter.**

- **Climate and soil.** American elderberry (*Sambucus nigra* ssp. *canadensis*) is
  native to Kansas, cold-hardy, and tolerant of a range of soils, including heavier ones
  if not waterlogged. Summer heat and drought stress it; drip irrigation is needed for
  commercial yield. High wind and hail break canes. A shelterbelt helps.
- **Varieties.** 'Wyldewood' (selected at Wyldewood Cellars, Mulvane, with K-State and
  the University of Missouri), plus 'Bob Gordon', 'Ranch', 'Adams' and 'York'. Missouri
  selections are the better match for Kansas heat (verify trial results).
- **Equipment.** The farm's tractor and mower suit row-middle management. The sprayer
  can apply in row middles but is not an orchard sprayer. Harvest is hand-cut clusters
  (cymes); destemming is frozen-berry shaking or a destemmer (capital). The grain combine
  has no use.
- **Herbicide drift.** Elderberry is a broadleaf shrub and sensitive to 2,4-D and
  dicamba from adjacent wheat, sorghum and pasture spraying, including the farm's own.
- **Labor.** Harvest runs mid-July to late August over 4–6 weeks, before sorghum
  harvest but in peak heat. Cutting runs ~50 lb/hr and destemming ~80 lb/hr (estimates;
  **verify**), so about 130 hr/ac. The farm has no dedicated labor, so this is hired
  seasonal labor.
- **Perennial and trust.** **A 10–15 year planting on trust land**, with drip lines,
  posts and windbreak. It needs written trustee approval and a lease term covering the
  payback period (base year ~5) plus the planting life, with a removal or reimbursement
  clause for the irrigation system. It is shorter-lived and more reversible than an
  orchard or vineyard: it can be bulldozed and returned to crop.

## 2. Agronomy summary
- **Planting.** Dormant hardwood cuttings or 1-year plants in spring, 3–4 ft in rows
  10–12 ft apart (~1,000–1,450 plants/ac). Weed fabric or mulch plus drip. Plants
  sucker into a hedgerow by year 3.
- **Fertility.** Moderate N (40–60 lb/ac, soil-test driven; **verify** UK guide).
- **Pruning.** Remove 3-year and older canes in the dormant season. Prunings become
  cuttings for sale (section 5).
- **Pests.** Spotted wing drosophila (SWD) at ripening, eriophyid mites, elder shoot
  borer, birds (netting or harvest promptly), Japanese beetle, and tomato ringspot
  virus.
- **Harvest.** Cut whole cymes when 95 % of berries are dark. Cool immediately. Freeze
  on the cyme, shake off berries, then sort, pack and freeze in 25-lb pails.
  Stem and unripe-berry removal matters (stems contain cyanogenic glycosides).
- **Bearing.** Light crop in year 2. Near-full in year 3, full by year 4. Productive life
  ~10–15 years with cane renewal (**verify**).

## 3. Yield and raw-crop economics
Basis: 1 acre with drip, 2026 dollars. **Establishment $5,500 in year 1** (estimate:
~1,000 plants at ~$3, drip $1,200, fabric/mulch $600, site prep $300, planting labor
20 hr at $18; scaled down from UF/IFAS organic Florida $11,891; **verify** with Missouri
budgets). Mature cash cost: cultural $1,200/ac/yr (pruning, mowing, fertility, SWD and
mite sprays, irrigation water) plus $0.735/lb for cut, destem, freeze, pack and haul
(hired at $18/hr). Establishment is amortized at 6 % over 12 years ($656/ac/yr).

## 4. Raw-crop market
- **Wyldewood Cellars (Mulvane/Peck, Sedgwick County).** Calls itself the largest US
  producer of elderberry products (wines, syrup, jelly, concentrate) and is about 20–30
  miles from the farm. It is both the most likely local buyer of frozen berries and the
  dominant local competitor for finished products. Whether it buys outside fruit, and at
  what price and spec, is **unknown; call first**.
- **Midwest Elderberry Cooperative** (Upper Midwest). Historic grower targets: $0.75–1.00
  fresh on the cyme, $2.00 destemmed frozen (2017). It resells frozen at $3.00–4.50/lb.
  Freight from Kansas to the co-op erodes margin (**verify** membership and
  current grower price).
- **River Hills Harvest (Missouri)** sells syrup and has historically bought from
  growers (**verify** current buying).
- **Small syrup makers** in Kansas (Lawrence, Kansas City): small volumes, at 25–100
  lb each.
- **Demand trend: falling.** Elderberry supplement sales fell 28 % in the mainstream
  channel in 2023 and about 21 % in 2024, after the pandemic spike (ABC Herb Market
  Report). Many Midwest growers planted in 2018–2021, so new supply is meeting shrinking
  demand. Imported European elderberry (*S. nigra*) concentrate and dried berries sets
  the price ceiling for bulk.
- **Volume ceiling.** Without a contract, assume ≤ 2,000–4,000 lb/yr can be sold
  locally (≈ 1 acre).

**Practical combination (1 acre).** ~3,000 syrup bottles (~1,050 lb berries), ~2,000 lb
sold frozen to Wyldewood or another buyer, the rest dried, plus cuttings. That gives
roughly $2,200 (raw net) + $22,000 (syrup) + ~$4,000 (dried) + ~$1,400 (cuttings) ≈
$29,000/yr at full ramp. Syrup sales take 2–3 years to build through markets, online
and Wichita shops.

Competitors in reach: **Wyldewood Cellars** (syrup, concentrate, wines; dominant,
local, long-established), Mindfully Made (Lawrence), Heartland Elderberry (KC), River
Hills Harvest (MO), and national brands (Sambucol, Gaia, Nature's Way) in grocery.

## 6. Risks and disqualifiers
1. **Market (top disqualifier).** Demand is falling (−28 % in 2023, −21 % in 2024) and
   the local giant is a competitor. No buyer has been confirmed.
2. **Harvest labor.** ~130 hr/ac in July–August heat with no dedicated labor. Fruit
   cannot wait.
3. **Trust.** A 10–15 year planting with drip needs trustee approval and a lease term.
   Syrup and processing must be in the LLC off trust land.
4. Drift injury from 2,4-D and dicamba. SWD. Birds. Hail and wind.
5. Health-claim compliance. "Immune support" marketing draws FDA and FTC scrutiny.
6. Food safety: raw elderberry, stems and leaves contain cyanogenic compounds, so
   product must be cooked and stems removed.

## 7. Decision rules
1. Plant nothing until Wyldewood Cellars or another buyer gives a written letter of
   intent for ≥ 2,000 lb/yr at ≥ $1.50/lb frozen destemmed (see
   `.claude/skills/buyer-network-development`).
2. Start with ≤ 1 acre with drip next to the farmstead, upwind of grain fields or
   behind a buffer, and register the location with DriftWatch (FieldWatch) and
   neighbors.
3. Make syrup in a licensed rental kitchen via the LLC. Scale bottles only to proven
   sell-through.
4. Pull the planting if year-4 sales cover less than direct cash costs.
