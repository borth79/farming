# Hard red winter wheat (HRW)

> Scope: primary cash crop for a ~1,000 acre owned operation in Sedgwick County, Kansas.
> Dryland is the primary case; irrigated differences are called out in each section.
> Companion file: `wheat-grazing-and-dual-purpose.md` (graze-out and dual-purpose wheat).
> Numbers tagged **verify** could not be sourced in this review and must be confirmed
> before they drive a plan. Every price carries unit and date.

## Summary and decision rules for this farm
1. Wheat is the anchor of the dryland rotation here because it uses fall–spring moisture that summer crops cannot, spreads workload (Oct drill, Jun harvest), and opens a double-crop window. It is rarely the highest-margin crop per acre in a good summer-crop year; its value is risk reduction, rotation, and cash flow in June.
2. Plant inside the K-State optimum window for south-central Kansas (approximately Sep 25 – Oct 20, Zone 3; **verify** Sedgwick's exact zone and best-pest-management planting date, historically about Oct 1–5 for the Wichita area). Do not drill grain-only wheat before the best pest management planting date; do plant dual-purpose wheat earlier (see companion file).
3. Pull a 24-inch profile nitrate (and chloride, sulfur) sample every fall. Size N with the K-State formula: 2.4 lb N per bushel of yield goal, minus 10 lb per 1% organic matter, minus profile N, minus previous-crop credit. If profile nitrate-N exceeds 80–100 lb/acre, skip or minimize spring N.
4. Base yield goal on 105% of the field's own 3–5 year average, not on county or state averages.
5. Spend the fungicide dollar at flag leaf (Feekes 8–10) and only when a susceptible variety (rating 4 or worse) meets confirmed stripe/leaf rust in the field or the region; a variety rated 3 or better for stripe rust seldom pays back a spray.
6. Kill all volunteer wheat at least 2 weeks before drilling (K-State now advises a 30-day wheat-free window) to break the wheat streak mosaic green bridge; this is the single most important disease rule in this county.
7. Rotate herbicide groups; assume ALS (Group 2) resistance in kochia, marestail and bromes already exists on or near this farm. Feral rye or jointed goatgrass present in a field means a CoAXium (Aggressor) or Clearfield variety or rotation out of wheat.
8. Insure with Revenue Protection at 70–80% coverage; evaluate hail as separate private cover because Sedgwick County is hail-prone. Treat the RMA projected price as the floor for pre-harvest sales up to the insured bushels.
9. Market in thirds: pre-harvest hedge or forward contract on rallies above breakeven, harvest sale only of what cannot be stored, and storage sale into the typical post-harvest basis improvement (Gulf export demand, Mexico and Nigeria). Track protein; in high-protein years capture the premium rather than blending it away.
10. After harvest, decide double-crop soybean or sorghum by July 10 based on soil moisture in the top 2 feet and the price ratio (see "Double-crop" section). If neither pays, keep the stubble clean of volunteer and plan the next spring crop.

## Fit for this operation
- Soils: Sedgwick County is dominated by deep silt loams and silty clay loams of the Arkansas and Ninnescah valleys and the uplands (Farnum, Shellabarger, Goessel, Blanket, Vanoss series are common; **verify** per field in `knowledge/land/`). Wheat tolerates the heavier clay-pan soils better than corn or soybeans and tolerates moderate soil acidity poorly; several south-central Kansas fields now test pH < 5.5 after decades of ammoniacal N. Lime and aluminum-tolerant varieties are both options (see Variety selection).
- Climate: approximately 30–33 inches annual precipitation (farm-profile), of which roughly 12–16 inches fall during the wheat season (Oct–Jun) **verify**. Winter wheat yield is most sensitive to April–May rainfall and to heat during grain fill in late May–June. Hail, late freeze (April), and wet harvests are the characteristic risks.
- Rotation: wheat follows soybeans, grain sorghum (with a 2–3 week residue/allelopathy and N-tie-up penalty), corn, or fallow; wheat precedes double-crop soybeans or sorghum, full-season sorghum, or corn. Continuous wheat is agronomically possible but raises tan spot, Hessian fly, take-all, and grassy-weed pressure and is not recommended as a standing practice.
- Equipment: requires a drill (7.5–10 inch spacing, no-till capable preferred), sprayer, fertilizer application (dry spreader or UAN streamer, or custom), combine with a draper or auger platform, and trucks. See `knowledge/machinery/equipment-inventory.md` (stub).
- Storage: wheat stores well (see Storage). On-farm storage capacity is unknown (farm-profile). Dryland wheat in a 45–55 bu/acre year on 300–500 acres yields 15,000–27,000 bu, comfortably within a modest bin set.
- Disqualifiers: none for the farm as a whole. Field-level disqualifiers: pH below about 5.2 without lime; heavy feral rye or jointed goatgrass infestation without an herbicide-tolerant variety plan; fields that cannot be drilled by about Nov 1 because of a late summer crop.

### Planting window and the Hessian fly date
- K-State now uses the term "best pest management planting date" (BPMP) rather than "fly-free date" because Hessian fly adults are active well after it; K-State pheromone trapping in Sedgwick County (2006–2010) found adults active into early December (K-State MF2866; Sedgwick trap study).
- K-State planting zones (eUpdate 1118, 2026-08-27): Zone 1 Sep 10–30; Zone 2 Sep 15–Oct 20; Zone 3 Sep 25–Oct 20; Zone 4 Oct 5–25. Sedgwick County is in the south-central zone, believed to be Zone 3 (Sep 25 – Oct 20) **verify** against the K-State zone map.
- Optimum grain yield comes from planting within about one week of the BPMP date (K-State). The historical fly-free date for Sedgwick County is approximately Oct 1–5 **verify** (county agent or K-State map).
- Planting earlier than the BPMP increases Hessian fly, wheat streak mosaic (mite flight), barley yellow dwarf (aphid flight), army cutworm egg-laying and excessive fall growth/water use. Planting later than ~Oct 25 reduces fall tillering and yield; K-State's late-planting guidance is to raise the seeding rate and narrow the rows rather than add N in the fall.
- Irrigated: same window; irrigated wheat is often planted at the later end of the window because of the preceding summer crop and because moisture for emergence can be applied.

- Farm rule: target 1.0–1.2 million seeds/acre (about 23–28 seeds/sq ft) for dryland grain-only wheat drilled in the window; convert from the seed-tag seeds-per-pound, not a flat 15,000. Large-seeded lots at 60 lb/acre may equal 50 lb/acre of a small-seeded variety (C529). Maximum under any conditions about 120 lb/acre (K-State).
- Depth: 1 to 1.5 inches into moisture; never deeper than 2 inches, and shallower (0.75–1 in) when soils are warm and moist and emergence is quick. Semi-dwarf varieties with short coleoptiles must not be planted deep. (K-State general guidance; depth figures **verify** against C529.)
- Row spacing: 7.5–10 inches standard; narrower rows compensate for late planting.

### No-till versus conventional
- No-till wheat after soybeans or sorghum is the default for this farm: saves 2–3 tillage passes (fuel at 2026 prices is a decisive cost; see `knowledge/inputs/diesel-and-fuel.md`), preserves moisture for the fall stand, and reduces erosion on the sloping uplands.
- Costs of no-till wheat: cooler soils and slower emergence (plant at the earlier end of the window and 10–15% more seed), greater reliance on glyphosate/paraquat burndown (resistance risk in kochia, marestail), more tan spot and Septoria inoculum from wheat residue (continuous wheat only), and more pressure from wheat curl mite volunteer if stubble is not kept clean.
- Conventional or vertical tillage remains useful for incorporating lime, breaking a herbicide-resistant weed cycle, or seedbed preparation after a wet harvest; one pass of vertical tillage after sorghum helps the drill cut through stalks.
- Irrigated: pivot-irrigated wheat after corn is typically no-till or strip-till into corn residue; residue management at the drill (row cleaners) matters more than tillage system.

### Water needs
Full-season wheat uses roughly 18–22 inches of water from planting to maturity for yields in the 60–80 bu range; each inch of available water above the first ~5–6 inches is worth roughly 4–6 bu/acre **verify** (K-State MF3066 Efficient Crop Water Use). Dryland yield here is set largely by stored soil water at planting plus April–May rain.

## Variety selection
Criteria, in order of weight for south-central Kansas:
1. Yield stability across K-State south-central replicated tests (Andale, Arkansas City, Belle Plaine, Buhler, Caldwell, Clearwater, Hesston) over 3+ years, not single-year wins.
2. Disease package: stripe rust and leaf rust ratings (most frequent yield losses here), wheat streak mosaic tolerance (Wsm2 gene varieties), barley yellow dwarf, Fusarium head blight tolerance for irrigated or corn-stubble fields, tan spot/Septoria for no-till.
3. Hessian fly resistance (critical if planting early or dual-purpose).
4. Acid soil / aluminum tolerance where pH < 5.5.
5. Maturity: medium-early maturity escapes June heat; very early varieties carry more April freeze risk.
6. Straw strength and height for irrigated fields (lodging).
7. Herbicide-tolerance trait (CoAXium AX or Clearfield CL+) only where feral rye or goatgrass demands it; these carry a technology fee and a grass-only solution.
8. Protein and milling quality (Kansas Wheat Commission quality ratings); matters when the farm can capture premiums.

Current information (2026):
- The 2026 Kansas Wheat Variety Guide (K-State MF991) added profiles for KS Tradition, KS Flintlock, AP Sunbird and Orange Blossom CL+ (K-State, 2026).
- In the 2026 south-central K-State replicated tests, Kivari AX, PS 1985, KS Flintlock and K-State line KS150167-17 were near the top of the yield table (Sedgwick County Extension handout, 2026; the handout's formatting was unreadable in this review, so individual yields are **verify**).
- Statewide, KS Providence was the most planted variety for the 2026 crop at 5.8% of Kansas acres; in the south-central district Bob Dole was planted on 8.4% of acres (USDA NASS Kansas Wheat Varieties 2026).
- Varieties with a long south-central record worth keeping on the shortlist: KS Providence, Bob Dole, Zenda, KS Hatchett, KS Mako, WB4792, LCS Atomic AX, AP Roadrunner, and the newer KS Flintlock and KS Tradition. Disease ratings, Hessian fly resistance and acid-soil ratings for each must be pulled from MF991 (2026) before the seed order — **verify**; the rating tables were not retrievable in this review.
- Farm rule: plant at least 3 varieties across the wheat acres with different disease packages and maturities; never more than 50% of acres in one variety.

## Fertility

### Nitrogen (K-State formula, MF2586 / eUpdate 1116, 2026-08-13)
N rec (lb/acre) = 2.4 × yield goal (bu/acre) − 10 × soil organic matter (%) − profile nitrate-N (lb/acre, 24 in) − previous-crop credit − other adjustments.
- Default if no profile test: 30 lb/acre profile N is assumed (MF2586). Fields with fall profile nitrate above 80–100 lb/acre have shown relative yields near 100% without added spring N (K-State eUpdate 1116).
- Yield goal: 105% of the field's 3–5 year average (K-State).
- Previous-crop credit: soybeans 40 lb N/acre; alfalfa larger; sorghum/corn residue can tie up N and some guidance adds 20–30 lb for heavy no-till residue (K-State; exact credits **verify** in MF2586).
- Worked example, dryland, yield goal 50 bu, 1.5% OM, profile N 30 lb, after soybeans: 120 − 15 − 30 − 40 = 35 lb N/acre. Same field after sorghum with no credit: 75 lb N/acre. Irrigated, 85 bu goal, 1.5% OM, profile 40: 204 − 15 − 40 = 149 lb N/acre.
- Timing and forms: 20–40 lb N at or before planting (preplant anhydrous, DAP/MAP starter N, or UAN) and the balance topdressed late Feb to mid-March at green-up before Feekes 5–6. Urea (46-0-0) and UAN (28/32) are both effective topdress forms; surface urea needs rain or incorporation within a few days to limit volatilization, UAN streamed on dormant wheat risks leaf burn when applied to actively growing wheat. Later than jointing, N mostly raises protein rather than yield.
- Fall-only N programs lose N on these soils in wet winters; split programs are the farm default.
- 2026 price points for cost per lb N: urea $616/short ton (co-op quote, Kansas, Sep 24–Oct 6, 2026, Agsist; thin sample) = $0.67/lb N; anhydrous $832/ton = $0.51/lb N (same source). See `knowledge/inputs/fertilizer-nitrogen.md` for the tracked series.

### Phosphorus and potassium (MF2586)
- Critical soil-test levels (Mehlich-3 P, 0–6 in): 20 ppm P is the build/maintenance target; wheat response to P is strong below about 15 ppm. Potassium target 130 ppm (some MF2586 versions use 25 ppm P and 150 ppm K; **verify** which the current revision uses). K deficiency is a larger concern in south-central and southeast Kansas than in western Kansas (K-State).
- Crop removal: approximately 0.5 lb P2O5 and 0.3 lb K2O per bushel of grain **verify** (MF2586 removal table). A 50 bu crop removes ~25 lb P2O5/acre.
- Starter P with the seed (DAP or MAP, 30–50 lb product) consistently increases fall tillering and yield in low-P soils and on late-planted wheat; keep seed-placed N + K2O under about 30 lb/acre on 7.5-in rows to avoid injury.
- 2026 price points: DAP $936/ton; potash $470/ton (Kansas co-op quote, Sep 24–Oct 6, 2026, Agsist; thin sample). See `knowledge/inputs/fertilizer-phosphorus-potassium.md`.

### Sulfur
- K-State formula: S rec = 0.6 × yield goal − 2.5 × % OM − profile sulfate-S − other credits (MF2586). Typical where needed: 10–15 lb S/acre, applied before Feekes 5 (K-State Wildcat District, 2024). Sandy, low-OM, no-till fields in the Arkansas River valley are the most likely to respond. Ammonium sulfate (21-0-0-24S) or ammonium thiosulfate in UAN are the usual carriers.

### Chloride
- Sample to 24 inches; recommend 10–20 lb Cl/acre depending on soil test (K-State eUpdate on chloride). Preplant and topdress (November through early March) perform equally (K-State). Potassium chloride (0-0-60, 47% Cl) at 20–40 lb/acre supplies it; some varieties show leaf-spotting (physiological) and yield response of 2–5 bu when deficient **verify**.

### Lime
- Target pH 6.0 for wheat; soils below 5.5 lose yield and reduce herbicide (e.g., Group 2) and P efficiency; aluminum toxicity below about 5.2. Lime at ECCE-adjusted rates per K-State lime table; incorporate when possible. Ag lime in south-central Kansas delivered and spread typically $30–45/ton **verify** with 1–3 tons/acre as a multi-year investment. Where lime cannot be justified in time, choose an aluminum-tolerant variety (MF991 rating).

### Irrigated differences
- Higher yield goal (75–100 bu) raises N to 130–200 lb/acre; split three ways (preplant, green-up, and a late jointing application through the pivot or by streamer) to limit lodging and raise protein. Lodging risk favors semi-dwarf varieties and avoiding excess fall N. Chloride and sulfur responses are more frequent on sandy irrigated soils.

## Weed management
Principal weeds in Sedgwick County wheat: cheat and Japanese brome, downy brome ("cheatgrass"), feral (volunteer) rye, jointed goatgrass (less common here than western KS), Italian ryegrass (increasing from the southeast), kochia, marestail (horseweed), henbit, flixweed, bushy wallflower, field bindweed, and volunteer wheat/sorghum in no-till.

Program framework (consult current labels; K-State "Chemical Weed Control for Field Crops" annual publication):
| Window | Target | Options | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Preplant burndown (no-till) | kochia, marestail, volunteer wheat, summer annuals | glyphosate + 2,4-D or dicamba; paraquat alternative; Sharpen (saflufenacil) for resistant kochia/marestail | Glyphosate-resistant kochia and marestail are documented in Kansas; rotate modes of action. Volunteer wheat must be dead 2–4 weeks before drilling (WSM). |
| Pre-emergence/early post (fall) | bromes, cheat, winter annual broadleaves | Group 2 (ALS) residuals such as Olympus/propoxycarbazone, PowerFlex/pyroxsulam, Finesse; Zidua (pyroxasulfone, Group 15) delayed pre | K-State notes Group 2 PRE residuals are associated with resistant kochia, marestail, bushy wallflower, flixweed, henbit and brome populations in Kansas (eUpdate 2025-07-24). Zidua provides a non-ALS residual for bromes and ryegrass. |
| Fall post (Nov) or early spring post (Feb–Mar) | downy brome, cheat, Japanese brome, Italian ryegrass | PowerFlex HL, Olympus, Osprey Xtra (mesosulfuron), Axial (pinoxaden, Group 1; ryegrass and wild oat only) | Cheat and Japanese brome are more susceptible than downy brome (K-State). Italian ryegrass: pinoxaden and mesosulfuron are the primary tools (K-State); ALS-resistant ryegrass is documented in the region. Weeds must be green and actively growing (K-State feral rye research). |
| Feral rye or jointed goatgrass | | Only with a herbicide-tolerant variety: CoAXium (Aggressor/quizalofop; K-State found 8 oz insufficient on feral rye, use higher labeled rate and timing) or Clearfield (Beyond/imazamox) | No conventional in-crop option; otherwise rotate out of wheat 2 years and prevent seed set (K-State eUpdate 1079, 2025-11-06). |
| Spring broadleaf (before jointing) | kochia, marestail, henbit, flixweed, bindweed | dicamba (before jointing), Huskie (pyrasulfotole + bromoxynil), 2,4-D (tillering to before jointing), Starane/fluroxypyr, MCPA; Quelex (halauxifen) | Dicamba is among the more effective non-ALS kochia products but must be applied before jointing; dicamba + Starane tank mixes are very effective on kochia (K-State). Huskie gives non-ALS kochia/marestail control. |
| Harvest aid / post-harvest | volunteer wheat, kochia, pigweeds in stubble | glyphosate + dicamba or 2,4-D; paraquat; Sharpen | Keep stubble clean through August; this is a WSM, Hessian fly and double-crop decision as much as a weed decision. |

Resistance rules for this farm:
- Assume Group 2 (ALS) resistance in kochia, marestail and at least some brome; never rely on a Group 2 product alone for those.
- Do not use glyphosate as the only burndown two seasons running on the same field.
- Record every herbicide group applied per field in the plan file; the planner must reject a program that uses the same group three consecutive seasons on a field.
- Herbicide carryover into double-crop soybeans: most Group 2 wheat herbicides (Finesse, Olympus, PowerFlex) carry rotation restrictions for soybeans of several months to 18 months; check the label before choosing the fall program if double-crop soybeans are planned.

Fungicide decision rule (farm): one planned flag-leaf application on susceptible varieties when regional rust reports (K-State Wheat Disease Update, Kansas Wheat) show rust in Oklahoma or south-central Kansas by mid-April and the crop's yield potential is at least 40 bu; skip on varieties rated ≤3, on drought-stressed crops below 30 bu potential, or when grain price × expected 4–6 bu response does not cover product plus application (roughly $20–35/acre total **verify**).

## Freeze and heat risk by growth stage
Freeze injury thresholds (K-State C646; Sunflower District table; approximately 2 hours at or below the temperature):
| Stage | Injurious temp | Yield effect | Symptoms |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tillering | 12 °F | slight to moderate | leaf chlorosis, burning |
| Jointing | 24 °F | moderate to severe | growing point death, stem lesions/bending, odor |
| Boot | 28 °F | moderate to severe | floret sterility, head trapped in boot, awn damage |
| Heading | 30 °F | severe | floret sterility, white awns/heads |
| Flowering | 30 °F | severe | floret sterility, no kernel set (most sensitive stage) |
| Milk | 28 °F | moderate to severe | white, shriveled kernels |
| Dough | 28 °F | slight to moderate | shriveled, discolored kernels, low test weight |
Allow 10–14 days of warm weather before assessing damage (K-State). Injury is worse with wind, repeated nights, and on low ground; wet soil moderates it.

Sedgwick County freeze history: jointing typically occurs late March–early April; the median last 28 °F is early April and 32 °F mid-April **verify** (Kansas Mesonet, Wichita). An April 10–20 freeze with wheat in boot is the classic south-central Kansas loss event (2007, 2017 **verify**).

Heat: the critical period for yield (grain number) runs from about 20 days before flowering to 10 days after (K-State 2023). Temperatures above about 82–88 °F immediately before anthesis reduce pollen viability; optimum from flowering through grain fill is 54–72 °F (K-State eUpdates 2022–2023). Short, early heat spikes during grain fill can cut kernel weight more than longer moderate heat, and heat damage is worse under drought; moist soil lets the canopy cool 2–3 °F (K-State). Practical implications: medium-early maturity, planting in the window (not late), and adequate K and Cl all reduce exposure to the late-May heat that is the most common yield limiter in Sedgwick County after drought.

## Harvest, moisture and storage
- Harvest at 13.5% moisture or less for direct delivery; combining can begin at 15–18% with on-farm aeration drying, which protects test weight and reduces shatter and weather risk. Wheat is marketed on a 13.5% basis; each 1% moisture shifts weight about 1.1% (University of Idaho). Most Kansas elevators discount above 13.5–14% **verify** local schedule.
- Storage (K-State MF855): wheat at 12% moisture or less strongly limits insect development; mold growth slows below 14%; cool grain with aeration as soon as night temperatures allow (target below 70 °F in summer, 40–50 °F by late fall); never mix new grain onto old; clean bins and treat empty bins; monitor monthly; fumigation (phosphine) only by trained/licensed applicators. Lesser grain borer, rice weevil and Indian meal moth are the main Kansas stored-wheat insects.
- Storage economics: on-farm storage is justified when the expected post-harvest basis and futures carry exceed about $0.03–0.05/bu per month of interest and shrink plus handling **verify**; Kansas harvest basis is typically widest (most negative) in late June–July and improves into fall and winter (see Marketing).
- Harvest timing rule: start at 15–16% on fields with weak straw or sprouting-prone varieties if rain is forecast and aeration is available; otherwise begin at 13.5–14%.

## Quality
- Kansas 2026 crop (preliminary, Kansas Grain Inspection / Kansas Wheat Commission, Sep 2026): test weight 60.3 lb/bu (2025: 59.9; 2016–2025 average 60.7), protein 12.1–12.3% (2025: 12.0; 10-yr average 11.8), 63% of samples grading No. 1 (2025: 45%), damaged kernels 0.6%. National HRW protein 12.5% (12% mb) in late July 2026; falling number ~356 s (US Wheat Associates).
- Grade factors that drive price locally: test weight (No. 1 HRW ≥ 60 lb/bu; No. 2 ≥ 58), damaged kernels, foreign material, shrunken and broken, dockage, and moisture. Typical elevator discounts: 1–3 cents/bu per pound below 58 lb test weight, escalating sharply below 56; DON (vomitoxin) discounts above 2 ppm; sprout damage and low falling number discounts in wet harvests **verify** local schedule.
- Protein: KC HRW futures deliverable grade is minimum 11% protein (CME). Protein premiums for 12%+ and 13%+ and discounts below 11% vary from near zero in high-protein crop years to $0.30–1.00/bu for 13% in low-protein years **verify**; a protein premium inversion (high protein in drought years when it is abundant) is common in Kansas. Rule: sample every bin for protein at harvest; segregate 12.5%+ wheat when the elevator posts a premium.
- Agronomic levers on protein: late (jointing to heading) N raises protein roughly 0.5–1 point per 30 lb N **verify**; drought and heat raise protein and lower test weight; high yields dilute protein.

## Double-crop soybean or sorghum after wheat
- Window: wheat harvest June 15–July 5 leaves 100–120 days before a mean first frost around Oct 25–Nov 1 **verify** (Wichita).
- Double-crop soybeans: K-State's long-term average is about 20 bu/acre in central/northeast Kansas and 20–40 bu toward the southeast; the main determinants are planting date, prior wheat yield (residue and moisture use) and maturity group (K-State MF3461; eUpdate 1105, 2026-05-28). Plant immediately behind the combine (no-till drill, 15-in or narrower rows, MG 4.0–4.8, 140–160k seeds) **verify** rates; one eUpdate cites Kansas trial yields averaging ~25 bu. Herbicide carryover from Group 2 wheat herbicides must be checked.
- Double-crop sorghum: Hesston no-till trials averaged 75 bu/acre double-crop versus about 90 bu full-season over 4 years (K-State); use early-maturing hybrids and keep seeding rates modest (35–45k seeds/acre dryland **verify**). Sorghum tolerates heat and late-July dryness better than soybeans but has higher harvest/drying cost and a chinch bug risk next to wheat stubble.
- Decision rule (farm): go double-crop when (a) wheat harvest is done by July 5, (b) at least 2 inches of plant-available water is present in the top 2 feet or 1 inch of rain is forecast within 7 days, (c) the expected double-crop margin exceeds the cost of a clean fallow (~$15–25/acre herbicide) plus the moisture penalty on the following full-season crop (roughly 5–10% of next year's dryland sorghum/corn yield **verify**). Choose soybeans when Nov soybean futures / Dec sorghum (corn) ratio favors beans (above about 2.5:1 **verify**) and herbicide carryover allows; otherwise sorghum. Irrigated: double-crop soybeans are routinely 35–50 bu and almost always pay **verify**.

## Rotation effects
- Following crop: wheat leaves little residual N (N credit 0); the stubble conserves moisture for a following full-season sorghum or corn and suppresses summer annual weeds; double-crop options as above.
- Wheat after soybeans: 40 lb N credit, cleaner seedbed, generally the highest-yielding wheat on this farm type. Wheat after sorghum: allelopathic and N-immobilization penalty (often 3–8 bu **verify**), needs more N at planting and a later drill date; wheat after corn: higher Fusarium head blight risk, especially irrigated.
- Disease breaks: one year out of wheat largely resets tan spot, Septoria, take-all and Hessian fly; two years for feral rye and jointed goatgrass seedbanks (with no seed set).
- Herbicide carryover from wheat into the next crop: Group 2 residuals (Finesse, Olympus, PowerFlex, Osprey) have rotation intervals for soybeans, sorghum, corn and sunflowers ranging from 3 to 18 months depending on product and rainfall; check labels when the fall herbicide is chosen. Dicamba, 2,4-D and Huskie have no meaningful carryover.
- Soil: wheat adds 1.5–2.5 tons/acre of residue, the main carbon input in a wheat–sorghum–soybean rotation.

## Irrigated wheat notes
- Yield potential 80–110 bu with 90–120 lb seed, 130–200 lb N split three ways, planned flag-leaf fungicide, and FHB-aware variety choice after corn. Lodging and FHB are the two irrigated-specific risks.
- Irrigation scheduling: fill the profile before planting or at emergence if dry; typically 1–2 applications at jointing–boot and heading–early grain fill; the final irrigation should be timed to crop stage (ending too early reduces kernel weight, too late wastes water) per K-State eUpdate (2017-08-18). Seasonal irrigation 4–10 inches depending on spring rain **verify**.
- Water rights: irrigated wheat uses water in Oct–Nov and Mar–May when evaporative demand is low, which can fit under an annual allocation that is otherwise consumed by corn; confirm per-field rights in `knowledge/water/` before any irrigated branch is planned.
