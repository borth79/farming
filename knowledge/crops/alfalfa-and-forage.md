# Alfalfa and forage

> Scope: alfalfa hay is the main topic. Forage sorghum / sorghum-sudan and small-grain hay are
> secondary sections near the end. Dryland is the primary branch; irrigated differences are noted
> inline as **[Irrigated]**. Every number carries unit, date and source. Numbers that could not be
> sourced this session are given as ranges and marked **verify**. The K-State 2026 South-Central
> alfalfa budget PDF exists but its figures could not be read in this session; the cost table below
> is reconstructed from component prices and the 2026 Kansas Custom Rates survey and must be
> reconciled against that PDF before use in a plan.

## Fit for this operation

**Where alfalfa fits.** Sedgwick County sits in K-State's "South-Central" budget region, west of
the Flint Hills (relevant for lime rules below). Annual precipitation ~30-33 in (farm-profile.md),
drought- and hail-prone. Alfalfa is deep-rooted and drought-tolerant once established, and its
distributed harvest (3-5 cuttings) spreads weather risk relative to a single grain harvest, but it
demands timeliness at every cutting and sells into a thin, freight-sensitive, quality-graded
market. The operation is a grain farm with (per farm-profile.md) no recorded hay equipment and no
recorded livestock; alfalfa therefore enters as a **cash-hay enterprise** that competes with wheat,
sorghum, corn and soybeans for acres and for operator time in May-June (first and second
cuttings overlap wheat harvest prep and soybean planting).

**Soil fit.** Alfalfa needs deep, well-drained soil; it will not tolerate ponding or a shallow
water table (Phytophthora/Aphanomyces). Suit it to upland silt loams and well-drained bottoms;
exclude poorly drained claypan or flood-prone creek bottoms. Soil pH: K-State 2026 guidance for
land west of the Flint Hills is to lime when pH < 5.8, targeting 6.0 (east of Flint Hills: lime
below 6.4, target 6.8) (K-State eUpdate 1115, 2026-08-06). Field-level pH is not recorded in
farm-profile.md; soil test before committing any field.

**Disqualifiers.**
- Fields with pH < 5.8 that cannot be limed 6+ months ahead of seeding.
- Poorly drained or flood-prone fields.
- Any field with alfalfa in the last 1-2 years (autotoxicity, see Establishment).
- No access to a reliable custom haying operator within reasonable distance, or no willingness to
  own/lease a swather, rake and baler (see Machinery). Alfalfa cannot be "fit in" around grain work;
  a missed cutting window costs quality grade and therefore price (see Quality).
- No identified hay buyer or market channel before seeding (see Marketing).

**Rotation fit.** Best use for this farm: a 4-5 year alfalfa stand on 80-200 acres of the best-
drained ground, rotated to corn or grain sorghum (N credit 40-120 lb N/ac; see Rotation effects),
then into the normal wheat/sorghum/soybean sequence. Alfalfa also diversifies away from grain price
exposure (hay price is weakly correlated with corn/wheat and more tied to dairy margins and
drought).

## Agronomic requirements

### Climate and growth
- Perennial legume; growth starts when soil warms in late March / early April in south-central KS
  and stops after first hard freeze (< 26 F), which averages about Oct 15 in Kansas, ranging from
  about Oct 1 to Nov 1 (K-State eUpdate 1119, 2026-09-03).
- Alfalfa in Kansas is normally harvested 3-5 times per season (K-State C-683). Dryland in
  south-central KS: plan on 3-4 cuttings; **[Irrigated]** 4-5 cuttings (K-State yield ranges
  imply this; exact count by region not published — verify with local agent).
- Water use: about 4 acre-inches per ton of hay seasonal average, rising to 6-7 acre-inches per
  ton in mid-summer (K-State MF2868 / MF3066). Season-total alfalfa ET can exceed 46 in/yr
  (K-State). **[Irrigated]** Net irrigation requirement in Kansas ranges from ~14 in (Linn County,
  east) to ~27 in (Morton County, southwest) (K-State MF2868/MF2867); south-central Kansas should
  fall roughly in the 18-24 in range — **verify** with K-State's irrigation requirement map for
  Sedgwick County. Alfalfa is the most water-intensive crop in scope; under a limited water right it
  will usually lose to corn or soybeans on water-use efficiency per dollar unless hay prices are
  high. Record the per-field water right (acre-feet) before any irrigated-alfalfa branch is run.

### Variety selection (Kansas-specific)
- **Fall dormancy (FD):** K-State recommends FD 4-6 for Kansas (eUpdate 1116, 2026-08-13); for
  southern Kansas, FD 4-5 (K-State spring-planting eUpdate). Lower FD = better winter survival;
  higher FD = faster regrowth and more fall growth. For Sedgwick County FD 4-5 is the default;
  FD 5-6 only under irrigation with aggressive 5-cut management and when winter hardiness
  rating is still 2 or better (verify winter survival index with seed company).
- **Pest resistance (K-State lists these as "essential"):** Phytophthora root rot, bacterial wilt,
  Fusarium wilt, Verticillium wilt, anthracnose (race 1; look for race 5 as well), pea aphid,
  spotted alfalfa aphid (K-State eUpdate 1116, 2026-08-13). Add Aphanomyces root rot race 1 **and
  race 2** resistance (R or HR) if seeding on heavier or wetter fields; race 2 is now more common
  than race 1 in soils tested in the Upper Midwest (UW Extension) and K-State's C-683 names
  Aphanomyces among key soilborne diseases to plan for. Potato leafhopper resistance (glandular-
  haired) is worth paying for in Kansas; it reduces but does not eliminate spraying.
- **Roundup Ready (glyphosate-tolerant):** simplifies establishment-year and dormant-season weed
  control (glyphosate instead of Raptor/Pursuit/metribuzin). Trait fee historically ~$3.50/lb on top
  of conventional seed (UC Davis 2020 cost study: RR seed $8.00/lb incl. fee vs conventional
  $4.50/lb) — 2026 pricing **verify**. Not a fit if the buyer requires non-GMO hay (some export and
  some organic dairy channels); confirm market channel first.
- **HarvXtra (reduced lignin), always stacked with RR:** allows delaying cutting ~7-10 days at the
  same quality, or same schedule at higher quality. Vendor claims (not independent): ~22% lower
  ADL, 16% higher RFQ, 18% higher NDFD vs check varieties (W-L vendor data, 2026 listing). 2026
  retail HarvXtra-RR seed listings: $578.50/50 lb (WL 375HVX.RR, GF Seed Co.) and $695/50 lb
  (AmeriStand 480 HVXRR, Seed World USA), i.e. about $11.60-13.90/lb. HarvXtra premium is
  recovered only when hay is sold on an RFV/RFQ scale to dairies (see Quality); for grinding/beef
  hay it does not pay.
- Kansas variety performance tests: K-State publishes alfalfa variety test reports (SRP series);
  the current year's report was not retrieved — check bookstore.ksre.ksu.edu before buying.

### Stand establishment
- **Seeding windows (south-central KS):**
  - *Late-summer seeding (preferred):* mid-August to mid-September for dryland stands (K-State
    SRL117; eUpdate 1116, 2026). K-State: late summer / early fall is often the best time in
    Kansas because weed pressure is lower, and fall-seeded stands yield more in their first
    production season. Need 6-8 weeks of growth before the killing frost (~Oct 15), so the practical
    cutoff in Sedgwick County is about Sept 10-15; soil moisture at seeding is the gating factor
    in dry Augusts. Fits after wheat harvest with a summer fallow/weed-control period.
  - *Spring seeding:* late March to mid-April (K-State spring-planting eUpdate). More weed pressure
    (summer annuals), first-year yield lower, and seedlings face May-June storms; use only when a
    fall seeding failed or soil moisture in August was inadequate.
- **Seeding rate:** 12-15 lb/ac dryland in central and eastern Kansas (K-State eUpdate 1116,
  2026); **[Irrigated]** 12-15 lb/ac on medium-fine soils, 15-20 lb/ac on irrigated sandy soils.
  Western dryland rate of 8-12 lb/ac is not appropriate for Sedgwick County rainfall. Coated seed is
  ~1/3 coating by weight; adjust to pure live seed.
- **Depth:** 1/4 to 1/2 in on medium and fine soils; up to 3/4 in on sands (K-State). Deeper than
  ~10x seed diameter kills stands.
- **Seedbed:** firm, moist, weed-free; a footprint should sink no more than ~1/4 in. Use press
  wheels on the drill (K-State Johnson County). No-till seeding into wheat stubble after glyphosate
  burndown works well for late-summer seeding and conserves moisture; conventional seedbeds need
  rolling/packing.
- **Companion crop:** K-State does not recommend a companion crop for late-summer seedings
  (competition for water). A light oat companion (~1 bu/ac) is sometimes used with spring seedings
  on erosive ground and removed as hay at boot stage; it reduces first-year alfalfa yield.
  Default for this farm: none.
- **pH and lime:** lime when pH < 5.8 (target 6.0) in Sedgwick County (west of Flint Hills)
  (K-State eUpdate 1115, 2026-08-06). Lime takes weeks to months to react; apply 6+ months before
  seeding, ideally after wheat harvest the year before. Lime cost ~$30-45/ton ECC spread (**verify**
  local quarry; Sedgwick County has local ag-lime sources).
- **Inoculant:** use *Sinorhizobium meliloti* inoculated or pre-inoculated (coated) seed; essential
  on fields without recent alfalfa (K-State).
- **Autotoxicity and rotation interval:** alfalfa seeded into a field recently in alfalfa suffers
  reduced germination and permanently stunted roots. Minimum of 1 year out of alfalfa (UMN);
  Oklahoma State recommends 2 years. Rule for this farm: **at least 1 full year in a grass crop
  (corn or sorghum) between stands; 2 years preferred.** Never interseed into a thinning stand.
- **Starter fertility:** apply P (and K, S, B as soil test indicates) before seeding and
  incorporate; alfalfa seedlings respond strongly to P. No N needed if inoculated.
- **Establishment-year harvest:** fall seedings normally get no cutting in the seeding year;
  first cutting the following May. Spring seedings can take 1-2 light cuttings in year one at
  bud/early bloom once plants are 12+ in.

### Weeds
- **Establishment year:** the critical period. Late-summer seedings face volunteer wheat, cheat,
  downy brome, henbit, pennycress, and (if seeded early) pigweed and foxtail. Options: glyphosate
  burndown pre-seeding; post-emergence in seedling alfalfa (2+ trifoliates): Raptor (imazamox)
  4-6 fl oz/ac or Pursuit (imazethapyr); Select/clethodim for grasses; 2,4-DB (Butyrac) for
  broadleaves; glyphosate over RR alfalfa. Fall-applied Raptor/Pursuit controls winter annual
  broadleaves better than spring application (Kansas Crop Profile; K-State table).
- **Winter annuals in established stands (dormant-season):** metribuzin (Sencor) on dormant
  established alfalfa; Velpar (hexazinone) 2-6 pt/ac during dormancy before 2 in of spring
  regrowth; Chateau (flumioxazin) 2-4 oz/ac after the last fall cutting for residual control of
  winter annuals (25-day pre-harvest interval); Gramoxone for emerged weeds in dormant stands
  (K-State Frontier District herbicide table). Downy brome/cheat and henbit are the typical
  first-cutting contaminants in south-central Kansas and are a major RFV discount if uncontrolled.
- **Summer annuals / grasses between cuttings:** Prowl H2O / Treflan (pre-emergence, dormant or
  between cuttings) for foxtail, crabgrass and pigweed; clethodim for emerged grasses.
- **Dodder:** parasitic annual, a listed noxious issue in alfalfa seed and a recurring south-
  central Kansas problem on river-bottom ground. Prevention: certified dodder-free seed; pre-
  emergence dinitroaniline (Prowl H2O / Treflan) applied in late winter/early spring before dodder
  germination (Penn State lists dodder among weeds controlled at the 2-4 qt Prowl H2O rate —
  attribution to that product **verify**); spot-treat or mow/burn patches before dodder seeds;
  harvest infested spots last to avoid spreading. No K-State dodder-specific guidance retrieved —
  **verify** with current K-State Chemical Weed Control guide (SRP report, updated annually).
- Always check the current K-State "Chemical Weed Control for Field Crops, Pastures, Rangeland and
  Noncropland" report for labels and rotational restrictions (metribuzin/hexazinone carryover
  matters when the stand is taken out to corn or sorghum).

### Diseases
- **Phytophthora root rot** (*P. medicaginis*): wet, poorly drained soils; kills seedlings and
  established plants after saturation events. Management: resistant variety (R/HR), avoid poorly
  drained fields, metalaxyl/mefenoxam seed treatment (Apron) on seedlings (K-State Plant Pathology;
  C-683).
- **Aphanomyces root rot** (races 1 and 2): primarily a seedling disease in wet springs/falls;
  stunted, yellow seedlings that do not die quickly. Resistance to race 1 does not confer race 2;
  buy varieties rated R/HR to both where fields are heavy or wet. Seed treatment helps only on
  resistant varieties (UMN; K-State C-683).
- **Anthracnose** (*Colletotrichum trifolii*): warm, humid summers; "shepherd's crook" stem
  lesions, crown rot, stand loss in years 2-4. Resistance (race 1 and 5) is essential in Kansas
  (K-State eUpdate 1116).
- **Crown and root rot complex** (Fusarium, Rhizoctonia, Phoma, etc.): the usual cause of stand
  decline in years 3-6, worsened by wheel traffic on wet soil, late fall cutting, low K, and
  weevil/leafhopper stress. Managed through K fertility, cutting height (2-3 in), fall rest, and
  traffic timing, not fungicides (K-State Plant Pathology fact sheet).
- **Bacterial, Fusarium, Verticillium wilts**: controlled by variety resistance (K-State).
- **Foliar diseases** (spring black stem, Lepto leaf spot, common leaf spot): cause leaf drop and
  quality loss in wet springs; early harvest of the affected cutting is the usual response;
  fungicides (e.g., Priaxor, Headline) are labeled but seldom pay on dryland Kansas hay.

### Cutting schedule and harvest management
- **Stage:** cut at late bud to first flower (1/10 bloom) for dairy-quality hay; bud-stage cutting
  gives highest RFV but shortens stand life if repeated without a rest; a historical K-State study
  found bud-stage cutting ran about 31-day intervals but weakened plants (K-State STB015). Standard
  compromise: first cutting at late bud / first flower (roughly mid-May in Sedgwick County —
  **verify** by scouting, varies 2 weeks by year), then every 28-35 days, allowing one cutting per
  year to reach 10-25% bloom to rebuild root reserves.
- **Dryland south-central KS:** 3-4 cuttings: ~mid-May, ~mid/late June, ~late July/August (often
  light or skipped in drought), and a September cutting. K-State C-683 lists "two cuttings before
  wheat harvest, weather permitting" as a goal.
- **[Irrigated]:** 4-5 cuttings on a 28-32 day interval from mid-May through late September.
- **Fall management (K-State eUpdate 1119, 2026-09-03):** base the last cut on regrowth time, not
  bloom. Either (a) take the last cut early enough to leave 8-12 in of regrowth / 4-6 weeks of
  growth before the killing frost (~Oct 15), which puts the second-to-last cut around early
  September, or (b) wait until after a killing frost and cut dormant growth. Alfalfa needs ~500
  GDD (base 41 F) after a cut to winterize (K-State). Cutting during the Sept 20-Oct 15 window
  is the main cause of winter injury and crown rot in Kansas stands. For south-central Kansas the
  cutoff falls between the SW (last week of Sept) and SE (first week of Oct) guidance.
- **Cutting height:** 2-3 in. Avoid driving on wet fields (crown damage).
- **Curing and baling:** windrow width as wide as possible (fast drying); rake/merge at 40-50%
  moisture to limit leaf shatter; bale large squares at 12-16% and large rounds at 15-18%
  (UW large-baler research: DM loss under 4% at < 16% moisture). Bale at night/early morning for
  leaf retention in Kansas heat. Hay preservative (propionic acid) allows baling at up to ~20-25%
  moisture when rain threatens; cost ~$5-8/ton (**verify**). Dry-down in June south-central Kansas
  is typically 3-4 days for large squares; expect 1 of 4 cuttings per year to be rained on.
- **Rain damage:** rain on cut hay leaches soluble carbohydrates and causes leaf loss; hay that
  must be re-turned after rain can lose over 60% of leaves vs. up to ~40% normal shatter in a good
  operation (Montana State), translating to 10-30 RFV points and often a one-grade drop (Premium
  to Good). No K-State per-inch loss figure was retrieved.
- **Bale type:** 3x4x8 large squares (~1,800-2,000 lb at ~17 lb/cu ft, **verify**) are the
  standard for dairy and export trade (stackable, 20-24 bales per flatbed) and command the
  posted AMS "large square 3x4" prices; large rounds (net-wrapped, 1,200-1,600 lb) are the beef /
  grinding standard and are discounted $15-40/ton vs squares (**verify**). This farm has no listed
  baler; custom operators in Kansas are more commonly round-balers (46 reports for round vs 5 for
  square in the 2026 custom rate survey), so **dryland beef/grinding hay in large rounds is the
  default channel unless a dairy or broker relationship justifies squares.**
- **Storage and shrink:** barn or tarped-on-pallets: 1-2% DM loss over 9 months (OSU BAE-1716);
  uncovered rounds on bare ground 5-35% depending on rainfall (Virginia Tech), 5.8% in one study vs
  1.3% covered on pallets (USDA-ARS). Large squares *must* be covered or barned — they absorb water
  on the flat top and bottom. Plan for 3-5% shrink on covered hay and 10-15% on uncovered rounds
  stored to spring. With no hay barn, budget a tarp system or sell at harvest (seasonal low).

### Stand life and when to take it out
- Expect 4-6 productive years in south-central Kansas; peak yield years 2-4. Stands thin from
  ~25 plants/sq ft after seeding to 5-10 plants/sq ft by the end of year 2 (UMN).
- Evaluate by **stems per sq ft** in spring at 4-6 in height: > 55 stems = yield not limited;
  40-55 = somewhat limited; < 40 stems = rotate out (K-State / KSFGC 2024-09-19). Fewer than 4-5
  plants/sq ft = poor, rotate (K-State).
- Decision rule: when stem count < 40 or when weeds (brome/cheat) make the first cutting fall
  below "Good" grade, terminate after the first or second cutting and plant grain sorghum
  (late May-June) or terminate in fall and plant corn the next spring to capture the N credit.

## Quality (RFV / RFQ) and grades
USDA hay grade bands (National Hay Test Guidelines, used in AMS reports):

| Grade | RFV | Typical CP % (DM) | Typical buyer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supreme | > 185 | > 22 | Dairy (lactating), export |
| Premium | 170-185 | 20-22 | Dairy |
| Good | 150-170 | 18-20 | Dairy dry cows/heifers, horses, feedlot starter |
| Fair | 130-150 | 16-18 | Beef cows, feedlot grinding |
| Utility | < 130 | < 16 | Grinding, blending |

- RFV is computed from ADF and NDF; RFQ adds fiber digestibility and is replacing RFV in dairy
  pricing (similar 80-200 scale). Kansas AMS dairy alfalfa is quoted in **$ per point of RFV**
  ($1.10-1.30/pt in Oct 2026 — see Marketing), so a 180-RFV bale at $1.20/pt = $216/ton and a 150-RFV
  bale = $180/ton: ~$36/ton for the quality difference, before freight.
- Practical Kansas reality: first cutting (weeds, weevil, rain) and summer cuttings (heat, fast
  maturity) often grade Good/Fair; second and fourth cuttings most often reach Premium. A dryland
  operation should plan on 25-40% of tonnage reaching Premium or better, the rest Good/Fair —
  **verify** by sampling every lot (core sampler; test at a NFTA-certified lab, ~$25-35/sample).
- Dairy vs beef: dairy hay requires large squares, tested lots, low ash (no dirt from raking),
  no mold, no weeds, usually a standing relationship or broker. Beef/feedlot grinding hay accepts
  rounds, weedy first cutting, rained-on hay, at $205-230/ton (Oct 2026) vs $250-280 for
  Premium/Supreme squares (Aug 2026).

## Machinery and labor requirements

**Operations per cutting:** swath/mow-condition (self-propelled windrower or tractor-mounted
mower-conditioner, 14-16 ft), rake or merge (1-2 days after cutting), bale (large square 3x4 or
large round), move bales off field within 1-3 days (to permit regrowth and avoid bleaching),
stack/tarp. Typical field capacity: 16-ft windrower ~8-10 ac/hr; wheel rake ~15-20 ac/hr; large
square baler ~15-25 t/hr; round baler ~8-12 t/hr (**verify**; ASABE D497 capacities). For 160 acres
per cutting: ~18 hr swathing, ~10 hr raking, ~25-40 hr baling plus hauling: roughly 60-80 labor
hours per cutting, 4 cuttings/year, concentrated in 3-5 day weather windows. Timeliness penalty:
each week of delay past late bud costs roughly 15-20 RFV points and ~$20-25/ton at $1.20/point,
plus lower stand vigor.

**Equipment the farm does not list (farm-profile.md):** windrower, rake, baler, bale handling
(loader with spear/squeeze), hay trailer, tarps or barn. Grain-farm tractors and a loader tractor
are usable; the sprayer and no-till drill are usable for establishment.

**Own vs custom (indicative; recompute with knowledge/machinery/ when equipment data exists):**
- Used 3x4 large square baler $60,000-120,000; used self-propelled windrower $60,000-150,000;
  wheel rake $8,000-20,000; new large round baler $50,000-75,000 (**verify** 2026 dealer quotes).
  A used round-baler + mower-conditioner + rake package can be assembled for roughly
  $80,000-130,000; a square-bale dairy-hay line (SP windrower + 3x4 baler + rake + accumulator/
  stacker) runs $200,000-350,000 used.
- Custom harvest cost at dryland yields is ~$36-55/ton (2026 custom rates above). Ownership cost of
  a $110,000 round-bale package at ~12-14%/yr (depreciation, interest, insurance, housing) is
  ~$14,000/yr plus repairs, fuel and labor ~$12-18/ton. Breakeven acreage vs custom at ~$45/ton and
  3.75 t/ac: ~$14,000 / (($45 - $15) x 3.75) ≈ **125 acres**; with the lower whole-operation custom
  rate (~$36/ton) ≈ **180 acres**. A square-bale line ($275,000) needs ~450-600 dryland acres or
  ~250-300 irrigated acres to beat custom.
- **Decision rule:** below ~120 alfalfa acres, custom-hire everything (and prefer an operator who
  bills a whole-operation per-bale rate); 120-250 acres, own a used round baler and
  mower-conditioner and hire or rent for peaks; above 250 acres with a dairy channel, consider a
  square-bale line. Custom availability is the binding constraint: alfalfa is cut on the same days
  across the county, and the farm has no leverage over a custom operator's order of work.
  Secure a written custom agreement before seeding.

**Labor:** one operator can run a 100-160 acre alfalfa enterprise with custom baling if the
grain program is not competing for the same days; first cutting (mid-May) collides with soybean
planting and sorghum planting prep, second cutting (mid-late June) collides with wheat harvest.
Treat this as a hard constraint in the planner: alfalfa acres x 2 passes of 4-6 hours per 40 acres
in each of those windows.

## Risk profile

- **Weather:** rain at harvest (quality grade loss, 1 of 4 cuttings/year typical), drought (2024
  Kansas average fell to 2.65 t/ac), hail (leaf strip; recovery within the cutting cycle unless
  stems broken), late-spring freeze after first growth (minor), winter injury from late fall cutting
  or ice sheeting. Alfalfa's deep root and multiple harvests make it the least drought-sensitive
  crop in scope on a tonnage basis, but the most sensitive to rain *timing*.
- **Pests:** alfalfa weevil every year (one spray), potato leafhopper most years, armyworm/
  grasshopper risk to fall seedings, dodder on bottom ground. Pyrethroid resistance in weevil is a
  watch item.
- **Price:** 2021-2026 Kansas alfalfa traded roughly $130-325/ton: a wider percentage range than
  corn or wheat. Price is tied to Plains drought (feed demand spikes), dairy margins (milk price,
  cull rates, Texas/New Mexico herd expansion), and competing feeds (corn silage, distillers
  grains). No futures market: price risk is managed only by contracts, storage (barn needed) and
  diversification.
- **Geopolitical / input exposure:** low direct exposure — no N purchased; P, K, S, B and diesel
  are the inputs (see knowledge/inputs/ and knowledge/geopolitics/). Export demand (China, Japan,
  Saudi Arabia, UAE) affects western-U.S. prices first and Plains prices indirectly; tariffs and
  Chinese dairy weakness in 2025-26 are a mild negative. Dairy policy and milk price are the larger
  external drivers.
- **Operational:** custom-operator availability; stand failure at establishment (K-State notes
  producers "shy away from alfalfa because of its high establishment cost and risk of stand
  failure"); 4-6 year capital lock-in; blister beetle liability only if selling to horse owners.
- **Insurance:** 
  - *Forage Production* (APH, yield-based) — available in Kansas for established alfalfa stands
    (pure alfalfa or alfalfa-grass meeting stand requirements); county availability and T-yields
    for Sedgwick County **verify** with an agent / RMA actuarial browser. Coverage is usually low
    relative to dryland yield variability and does not insure quality.
  - *Forage Seeding* — insures the establishment-year stand against failure (adequate-stand
    definitions in RMA Forage Seeding Crop Provisions 22-032); low cost, worth buying on a
    $225-330/ac establishment investment.
  - *Annual Forage (AFI)* — rainfall-index product for annual forages (forage sorghum, small-grain
    hay); 14 x 16 mile grids; July 15 sales closing for crops planted from August (K-State AFI
    update 2026-06-12). Kansas insured over $132 million of annual forage value in 2026. Does not
    cover perennial alfalfa.
  - *PRF (Pasture, Rangeland, Forage)* rainfall index can cover established alfalfa haying in
    some states — **verify** Kansas eligibility.
  - ELAP/LFP are livestock-side programs and do not apply to cash hay.

## Rotation effects

- **N credit to following crop (K-State):** corn after alfalfa is credited 0-120 lb N/ac by stand
  density: > 5 plants/sq ft = 120 lb N; 2-5 plants/sq ft = 80 lb; 1-2 plants/sq ft = 40 lb; < 1 =
  0 (K-State Corn Production Handbook C-560; MF2586 previous-crop adjustment). The same credit
  applies to grain sorghum. Second year after alfalfa: credit is about half the first-year value
  (K-State L778). A 4-5 year-old stand taken out is usually in the 2-5 plants/sq ft class, so
  **plan 80 lb N/ac credit for the first grain crop, 40 lb N/ac for the second.** At 2026 N prices
  (see inputs/fertilizer-nitrogen.md) that is roughly $45-70/ac in year 1 and half that in year 2.
- **Soil structure and water:** deep taproot channels improve infiltration and subsoil structure;
  however, alfalfa dries the profile to depth, so the first crop after alfalfa (especially if
  terminated in spring) can start with a depleted subsoil in a dry year. Prefer fall termination
  (after last cutting, glyphosate + 2,4-D or dicamba on 6-8 in regrowth in October) ahead of corn
  or sorghum so winter recharges the profile; spring termination followed by a June-planted sorghum
  is the fallback.
- **Weed and pest effects:** alfalfa breaks grass-weed and wheat-disease cycles; expect fewer
  cheat/brome and wheat-streak issues in following wheat. Volunteer alfalfa in the following crop
  is controlled by 2,4-D/dicamba (not by glyphosate if RR alfalfa was grown — a reason to keep
  RR-alfalfa fields out of RR-soybean sequences, or to plan Enlist/Xtend/Liberty systems).
- **Herbicide carryover:** metribuzin, hexazinone (Velpar) and imazethapyr (Pursuit) carry
  rotation restrictions to corn, sorghum and small grains (hexazinone up to 2 years for some
  crops). Check the K-State Chemical Weed Control report before terminating a stand.
- **Taking alfalfa out:** chemical termination (glyphosate 1.5-2 lb ae + dicamba or 2,4-D on
  active regrowth, fall preferred) is more reliable than tillage; tillage after chemical kill if
  crowns are large. Expect to lose the stand's last cutting value when terminating in fall. Account
  the exit year's N credit against establishment cost in the enterprise analysis.
- **Rotation interval back to alfalfa:** 1 year minimum in a grass crop, 2 preferred (autotoxicity;
  UMN, OSU).

cow-calf operators, or grazed.

- **Hybrid types (K-State MF2871):** *forage sorghum* (tall, single-cut, silage; conventional,
  BMR, photoperiod-sensitive), *sorghum-sudangrass* (multi-cut hay/grazing), *sudangrass* (finer
  stem, best for hay/grazing), *pearl millet* (no prussic acid risk; lower yield).
  - **BMR (brown midrib)** hybrids: lower lignin, higher digestibility and animal performance;
    yield 5-10% less than conventional and lodge more (K-State table notes BMR may yield less).
  - **Brachytic dwarf**: shortened internodes, more leaf, stands better, lower tonnage than tall
    types; BMR brachytic dwarf forage sorghum is marketed as corn-silage-competitive in quality
    (Cornell fact sheet 92). A BMR brachytic sorghum-sudan vendor listing cites 5-8 DM tons/ac with
    60-90 lb N at planting and 40-60 lb N after each cutting (vendor claim, not Kansas trial).
  - **Photoperiod-sensitive** forage sorghum stays vegetative until day length drops below ~12 h
    20 min (late September); very high tonnage, one late cut, lower quality; useful for silage.
- **Planting:** soil temperature 65-70 F (K-State MF2871: 70-75 F for most species; Nebraska:
  above 60 F for sorghum/sudan), in Sedgwick County roughly May 20 to July 10; seeding rate
  sorghum-sudan 15-25 lb/ac drilled (Nebraska; K-State MF2871 table not retrieved — **verify**),
  forage sorghum 6-10 lb/ac in 30 in rows for silage (**verify**); increase rates 15-20% for late
  plantings (K-State 2026 sorghum eUpdate rule). N: 60-80 lb N/ac dryland for a single-cut silage
  crop, plus 30-50 lb N after each hay cutting for sorghum-sudan (**verify** MF2586 forage N recs);
  high N plus drought is the nitrate-accumulation setup.
- **Yield:** Kansas silage sorghum averaged 15.5 wet tons/ac on 140,000 harvested acres in 2025
  (USDA NASS); dryland south-central Kansas forage sorghum silage typically 10-18 wet t/ac
  (~3.5-6 t DM); sorghum-sudan hay 3-5 t DM/ac in 2-3 cuttings dryland, 5-8 t irrigated (vendor /
  general extension, **verify** with K-State summer-annual trials). A K-State south-central
  summer-annual study found all species gave similar DM yield when cut at late boot-early heading,
  and that letting them mature raised tonnage but lowered crude protein (K-State, KREx, undated).
  Irrigated SW Kansas comparisons: forage sorghum 20-26 wet t/ac vs corn 27-32 (K-State).
- **Prussic acid (HCN):** risk is highest in young regrowth (< 18-24 in), after frost, after
  drought-breaking rain, and in sorghum > sorghum-sudan > sudangrass (K-State MF2871). Graze or
  green-chop only above 18-24 in; after a frost wait 7-10 days (or until frosted material is fully
  dry/brown) before grazing; ensiling does *not* reliably destroy HCN though wilting before
  ensiling lowers it; K-State Beef Tips (2026-08-31) found dhurrin stable in stored hay and
  standing forage, so **drying/curing does not guarantee safety: test before feeding**.
- **Nitrates:** accumulate in the lower stalk under drought, cloudy weather, frost and heavy N;
  Kansas conditions produce high-nitrate forage "nearly every year" (K-State). Nitrate does not
  dissipate in hay; ensiling reduces it 30-50%. Raise cutting height to 8-10 in in a drought,
  test every lot (K-State Beef Tips 2026-08-31). Feeding thresholds: < 1,000 ppm NO3-N safe;
  1,000-2,000 limit to 50% of ration; > 2,000 ppm do not feed (common Plains guidance, **verify**
  against K-State MF3029 nitrate publication).
- **Silage vs hay vs grazing:** silage (forage sorghum, 65-70% moisture at soft dough) requires a
  chopper, trucks and a pile/bag and a nearby feedlot or dairy buyer — practical only as a custom
  harvest sold standing or delivered within ~30 miles; hay (sorghum-sudan at 3-4 ft or boot stage,
  crimped) dries slowly in Kansas humidity — large stems need 5-7 days and often go into rounds at
  18-20% with preservative; grazing fits only if the farm adds cattle or leases grazing (see
  wheat-grazing file).
- **Markets:** sorghum-sudan / forage sorghum hay sells as grinding hay in the AMS Kansas report
  (quoted alongside "grass hay" and "sudan" — Oct 2026 grass hay large rounds roughly $90-130/ton,
  **verify** in AMS_2885); silage sells standing at roughly 8-10x the corn price per wet ton
  adjusted for moisture, or $35-55/wet ton delivered (**verify** regional quotes). Margins are thin
  on dryland unless the field would otherwise be idle (failed wheat, drought catch crop). Cost:
  seed $12-30/ac, N $40-60/ac, planting $20/ac, harvest as hay ~$100-140/ac custom (3 cuttings) or
  silage chopping $120-200/ac custom (**verify** Kansas Custom Rates 2026 silage lines).
- **Insurance:** Annual Forage Insurance (rainfall index; grid-based) is the fit; July 15 sales
  closing for fall-planted, and earlier dates for spring/summer plantings (K-State AFI 2026).

## Small-grain hay (wheat, triticale, oats) (secondary)

- **When it fits:** (1) salvage of drought- or freeze-damaged wheat (cut at boot to early heading
  before investing in harvest); (2) a planned winter-forage crop on acres going to a late-planted
  summer crop (double-crop soybeans or sorghum after hay harvest in mid-May gives ~3 more weeks
  than after grain harvest); (3) spring oats seeded in March for a June hay crop ahead of a
  late-summer alfalfa seeding.
- **Yields (K-State):** oat hay 2-4 t DM/ac (FORA29); K-State Hutchinson oat variety trials
  averaged ~4 t/ac across 20 varieties, range 3-5 (eUpdate 442, 2014); wheat hay ~1.5 t/ac used in
  a 2018 K-State drought-wheat analysis (eUpdate 693) with 2-3 t/ac possible from a good stand;
  triticale yields more than wheat but tests lower in quality and its quality varies more with
  maturity (K-State MF-1072). Irrigated triticale silage can exceed 13 wet t/ac (NMSU; not Kansas).
- **Quality and stage:** highest quality at late boot (CP 14-18%, RFV 100-130); best yield/quality
  balance at early milk (FORA29); dough-stage oat hay ~56% TDN and 10% CP (eUpdate 442). K-State
  ranks small-grain hay quality barley > oats > wheat > triticale > rye (eUpdate 1120, 2026-09-10).
  Awned wheat varieties cut after heading cause mouth lesions — cut at boot or use awnless/
  forage-type wheat or triticale. Beware herbicide and fungicide forage restrictions on wheat
  intended for grain (many labels prohibit haying/grazing within set intervals).
- **Economics:** small-grain hay competes with grass hay for feedlot/cow-calf demand at
  roughly $90-140/ton (**verify** AMS "grass hay"/"wheat hay" quotes); at 2.5 t/ac that is
  $225-350/ac gross against ~$60-80/ac custom cut-rake-bale (3 passes once) plus the forgone grain.
  It is a salvage or rotation-timing tool, not a planned enterprise, unless a local cattle buyer
  contracts it.
- **Insurance:** small grains planted for forage are insurable under Annual Forage (rainfall
  index), not under the wheat MPCI policy; wheat insured for grain and then hayed requires an
  appraisal/adjuster release first.

## Decision rules for this farm

1. **Alfalfa enters the plan only if all of the following are true:** (a) a field with pH ≥ 5.8
   (or limed 6+ months prior), good drainage, and no alfalfa in the prior 2 years; (b) a written
   custom-haying arrangement or a decision to buy a round-bale line; (c) at least one identified
   buyer (feedlot/grinder within ~75 miles, or dairy/broker within ~200 miles) and a price
   expectation ≥ $160/ton blended farm-gate for the next 2 years; (d) covered storage or a plan to
   sell each cutting off the field.
2. **Acreage:** minimum economic block 80 acres (custom operators will not prioritize smaller;
   one 3x4 or round-bale load per cutting per ~10-15 acres); default 120-200 acres (12-20% of the
   farm) so a bad hay year or stand failure cannot sink the operation; maximum 25% of acres
   unless a dairy contract is in hand. Stagger establishment across two blocks 2-3 years apart so
   that one stand is always in peak years.
3. **Equipment:** custom-hire below ~120 acres; own used mower-conditioner + rake + round baler
   between ~120 and 250 acres; square-bale line only above ~250 acres with a dairy channel or
   under irrigation.
4. **Seeding:** late-summer (Aug 15-Sept 10) no-till into wheat stubble, conventional or RR seed
   with FD 4-5 and full disease/aphid resistance; spring seeding only as a fallback.
5. **Variety traits:** HarvXtra only when selling on an RFQ scale to dairies; RR only if no
   non-GMO requirement from the buyer and the following-crop herbicide plan is adjusted.
6. **Harvest:** cut at late bud/first flower; never cut between ~Sept 20 and the killing frost;
   bale rounds for feedlot hay unless a square-bale operator and dairy buyer exist; test every lot.
7. **Exit:** terminate when spring stem count < 40/sq ft or weeds push first cutting below Good;
   fall-kill ahead of corn/sorghum and take the 80 lb N/ac first-year credit.
8. **Forage sorghum / small-grain hay** are opportunistic (failed wheat, drought catch crop, stand
   exit year) and are planned only when a local buyer or contract exists; always test for nitrate
   and prussic acid before sale or feeding.
9. **[Irrigated]** run alfalfa in the irrigated branch only once per-field water rights are
   recorded; below ~15 in/yr allocation alfalfa loses to corn/soybeans unless Premium hay is
   ≥ $220/ton.
