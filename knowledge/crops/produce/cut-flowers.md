# Cut flowers

> Scope: field-grown specialty cut flowers for the Wichita market: single-stem sunflowers,
> zinnias, celosia, dahlias, snapdragons, lisianthus, plus fall-planted tulips and ranunculus
> for the April–May window. Sold as bunches to florists and event designers (channel
> `chef_direct` in the economics CSV), bunches and sleeved bouquets to grocers and wholesale
> florists (`grocery_wholesale`), and bouquets, subscriptions and wedding buckets direct to
> consumers (`market_csa`). Oilseed and confection sunflower are in `knowledge/crops/sunflower.md`.

## 1. Fit for this farm
- **Why it is on the list.** Highest revenue per square foot of any crop in the produce plan
  (business-plan.md: $15,000–40,000 gross on small plots; SARE 11-farm benchmark $91,581 gross
  and $30,257 net per acre; Utah survey $64,000/acre average). Flowers are not food, so FSMA,
  GAP audits and wash-water rules do not apply (Kansas sales tax does). The farm's sunflower
  identity carries over. Perishable: 5–14 days of vase life; nothing is stored.
- **Soil and water.** Any well-drained loam; the Arkansas River corridor sandy loams drain
  well. K-State MF1034: field cut flowers need more total water than many field crops; plan
  1–1.5 in/week by drip in June–August (verify against MF1034 table). Needs a water source
  within hose reach of the plot: a domestic well, a pond, or the irrigation right once per
  field status is confirmed (farm-profile.md open item). Requires medium-high soil P for
  sunflower (K-State sunflower sheet).
- **Season.** Wichita frost-free ~Apr 15–20 to ~Oct 17–25 (verify K-State freeze map), about
  180 days. Tulips and ranunculus flower April–May from fall planting; snapdragons and
  lisianthus take the June window; sunflowers, zinnias and celosia carry July–September;
  dahlias peak September–frost. Kansas July–August heat (frequent 100 F days) is the main
  agronomic limit: dahlias stall, zinnia stems shorten, lisianthus and snapdragon finish.
- **Equipment.** Existing tractor for tillage and bed forming; everything else is hand or
  walk-behind scale. No harvest mechanization exists for cut flowers (mechanization file:
  hand-picked crops are capped at what one person picks in 20 hr/week until a second person
  is committed).
- **Labor.** Among the most labor-intensive crops per acre in this project (1,500–1,900
  hr/acre, Section 5) but the highest net per labor hour of any produce crop at the florist
  and direct channels (Section 8). The enterprise is sized by labor, not land: a quarter acre
  is 400–500 hours May–October.
- **Trust and structure.** No new structure, lease or water change at stage 1; a cooler and a
  deer fence are personal property. Weddings and subscriptions create consumer contracts;
  check liability cover and the trust checklist before taking deposits
  (`knowledge/operations/family-trust-constraints.md`).
- **Grain calendar conflict.** Peak flower harvest (July–September, 3 mornings a week) sits
  between wheat harvest and fall planting, so it conflicts less than tomatoes do; the
  fall-bulb planting (late October–November) overlaps wheat drilling and sorghum harvest.
  Dahlia tuber lifting (after first frost) overlaps soybean harvest.

## 3. Agronomy
**Layout.** 4-ft-wide beds on 6-ft centers, 100 ft long; ~70 beds per acre after headlands.
Permanent beds covered with woven landscape fabric burned with planting holes at 6 x 6, 9 x 9
or 12 x 18 in grids; one drip tape line per two rows under the fabric (two to three tapes per
bed). Fabric lasts 5–8 seasons and removes most weeding; plastic mulch is the one-season
alternative for sunflower successions. Paths mowed or mulched.

**Planting windows (Wichita, verify against K-State MF315 and freeze map).**
- Oct 25–Nov 20: tulip bulbs (crates or trenches 4–6 in deep), ranunculus and anemone corms
  pre-sprouted 10 days, under a low tunnel of frost cloth on hoops; snapdragon plugs for
  overwintering under the same cover (trial first).
- Mar 15–Apr 10: snapdragon, stock, bupleurum, ammi transplants; sweet peas.
- Apr 20–May 10 (after frost): first zinnia, celosia, basil, cosmos transplants; first
  sunflower direct seeding Apr 25–May 5 when soil is 55 F.
- May 1–May 15: dahlia tubers (soil 60 F); lisianthus plugs (started 12–14 weeks earlier or
  bought as 5-week plugs).
- Succession: sunflower every 7–10 days to Aug 5 (last crop blooms early Oct); zinnia and
  celosia second plantings Jun 10–25 for September bouquets; cosmos Jul 1.
- Pinching: zinnia, celosia (plume types), cosmos, snapdragon (group 3–4), dahlias at 12 in to
  force branching; never pinch single-stem sunflowers or lisianthus.
- Support: Hortonova netting (one layer at 12–18 in) over dahlias, celosia, lisianthus,
  snapdragons and tall zinnias; Kansas wind (30–50 mph gusts in June storms) makes netting
  non-optional for anything over 30 in; T-posts every 10–12 ft.

**Fertility.** Soil test (fields in `data/processed/soil_tests.csv`); target P medium-high for
sunflower (K-State), K medium; 1–2 in compost on beds at establishment; 50–80 lb N/acre
pre-plant then fertigate 10–20 lb N every 3 weeks for heavy feeders (sunflower, dahlia,
celosia); keep N moderate on zinnia (soft stems) and lisianthus. Many Sedgwick County soils
and well water run alkaline (pH 7.5–8); lisianthus tolerates alkaline conditions (K-State),
dahlias and snapdragons prefer 6.5 — check Fe chlorosis.

**Water.** Drip 1–1.5 in/week in summer, split into 3–4 sets; sunflowers 2 in/week in the
10 days before harvest for stem length. Overhead water on open blooms causes botrytis and
fabric is hot in July, so irrigate early morning. A 1/4 acre of drip at 0.45 gpm/100 ft tape
runs on a domestic well or a 1,500-gal tank.

**Weeds.** Fabric and mulch handle in-bed weeds; Palmer amaranth and bindweed come through
planting holes and along edges, hand pull weekly in June. Paths: mow or wood chips. No
herbicides labeled for the mixed crop; pre-plant tillage or stale seedbed only.

**Insects (Kansas).**
- Japanese beetle: established in the Wichita area (KSN, K-State Johnson Co.); adults chew
  buds and petals June–August on zinnia, dahlia, sunflower, celosia; hand collect mornings into
  soapy water, pyrethroids (bifenthrin, cyfluthrin) every 2–3 weeks if used, spray dawn or
  dusk for pollinators; traps only far from the plot.
- Western flower thrips: hide in dahlia, lisianthus and sunflower buds, scar petals, vector
  Dahlia mosaic virus (UMass); spinosad rotation, blue sticky cards, remove spent blooms.
- Grasshoppers: a Kansas problem in dry years, strip foliage and petals August; row cover on
  young plantings, Nosema bait on borders.
- Cucumber beetles and striped blister beetles on zinnia and sunflower petals; sunflower
  head moth is an oilseed problem, less so on cut stems harvested at first petal lift.
- Aphids on snapdragon, celosia, dahlia: beneficials, insecticidal soap.
- Deer: a disqualifier for an unfenced plot; deer browse zinnia, sunflower heads, dahlias and
  tulips. Plan a 7.5-ft polypropylene or 3-D electric fence around the whole block
  (Section 6); rabbits eat lisianthus and snapdragon seedlings (2-ft hardware cloth skirt).

**Diseases.**
- Botrytis (gray mold): dahlia blooms and zinnia in humid, wet spells (Penn State 2025);
  cut at the right stage, strip lower foliage, do not pack wet, cooler at 36–40 F, airflow.
- Powdery mildew: zinnia and lisianthus late season; resistant zinnia types (Oklahoma, Profusion
  not for cutting), sulfur or potassium bicarbonate, second plantings replace tired first ones.
- Alternaria leaf spot on zinnia (K-State MF1034) and bacterial leaf spot: avoid overhead water,
  rotate out of zinnia for 2 years.
- Snapdragon rust (K-State, UC IPM): resistant cultivars, spacing, no overhead water.
- Crown gall on dahlia (K-State): buy clean tubers, discard galled plants.
- Aster yellows (leafhopper-vectored) on zinnia, celosia, sunflower: rogue infected plants.
- Lisianthus: damping off and Fusarium on plugs; sterile media, no overwatering.
- Sclerotinia and head rot do not matter at cut-stem stage.

**Harvest stage and timing.** Cut early morning or evening into clean water. Sunflower: petals
just lifting off the disk; zinnia: "wiggle test" stem stiff, flower fully open (does not open
further after cutting); celosia: fully colored before seed set; dahlia: nearly fully open
(does not open after cutting); snapdragon: lower third of florets open; lisianthus: 1–2
flowers open; tulip: colored bud, pulled bulb and all; ranunculus: marshmallow-soft bud.
Harvest 3 mornings a week in peak (Mon–Wed–Fri or Tue–Thu–Sat for markets).

**Post-harvest.** Clean buckets (bleach or quat), hydration solution (acidified, UMass: 130 F
solution for woody and wilted stems), then holding solution; zinnias are "dirty flowers":
use a chlorine or quat-based solution and change water daily; cut sunflowers tolerate dry
handling up to 48 h but sell better hydrated; dahlias dislike cold below 40 F for long periods
and have a 4–6 day vase life; zinnia 7–10 days (13 days with 5 C then 1 C storage, 6.5 days
after 48 h at 20 C); celosia 10–14 days; lisianthus 10–14 days; snapdragon 7–14 days with
holding solution (NCSU trials 16–22 days); tulips and ranunculus 5–7 days. Cooler at 36–40 F
(dahlias, zinnias and celosia toward 40 F; tulips, ranunculus, snapdragon 34–36 F); hold no
more than 2–3 days before sale. Ethylene-sensitive: snapdragon, lisianthus (floret drop); keep
away from ripening produce in a shared cooler. Sleeves, rubber bands, kraft wrap for
bouquets; 10-stem bunches for florists in a 5-gal bucket of 6–8 bunches.

**Blended planning yield used in the economics:** 1,430 sold stems per bed-season across a
mixed plan (70 beds per acre: 20 sunflower, 15 zinnia, 10 celosia, 8 dahlia, 5 lisianthus,

**Per-lb-equivalent conversion (required by the economics schema):** 1 lb-equivalent =
one 10-stem wholesale bunch. A 10-stem bunch of zinnia or sunflower weighs roughly 0.8–1.5 lb,
so the equivalence is a convention, not a weight. Yield in the CSV is therefore
**10,000 bunch-equivalents ("lb") per acre** and price is per 10-stem bunch.

## 9. Profitability verdict
- **Best channel:** florists and event designers for every stem that meets their grade, with
  farmers-market bouquets and subscriptions absorbing the rest and all seconds; grocers only
  as a third outlet for sunflower and sleeved bouquets once volume exceeds the first two.
- **Stage-1 scale: 0.25 acre (about 17 beds of 100 ft)**, one family member, ~450 hours,
  two markets shared with the vegetable table, 4–6 florist accounts approached in February
  with a sample bucket in June, one subscription of 20–30 members, no weddings beyond DIY
  buckets in year 1. Expected gross $18,000–26,000; **family income (wages plus net at FAMILY)
  $14,000–20,000**; net to the enterprise after paying family wages $9,000–13,000.
- **Stage 2** (year 2–3, if year-1 stems per bed and florist orders hit base): 0.5–0.75 acre,
  caterpillar or high tunnel for March–May and October stems (tulips, ranunculus, anemone,
  snapdragon; these fetch the highest prices when field supply is zero), a second cooler,
  wedding bulk and a la carte service.
- **What changes the verdict:** (a) no family member available in July–September mornings
  moves everything to HIRED and halves net; (b) florist price below $9/bunch or fewer than
  4 accounts drops the blend toward the grocer column; (c) deer or Japanese beetle damage
  above 25% of stems; (d) a high tunnel with EQIP cost share raises the net per bed 1.5–2x on
  spring crops; (e) a Wichita wholesale flower hub or a second grower collective would open
  the florist channel beyond the farm's own delivery capacity.

## Japanese beetle management note (added 2026-11-18)
Companion-plant repellents (garlic, chives, rue, tansy, catnip) lack trial evidence of
protecting adjacent crops; treat as folklore. Geranium (Pelargonium) petals paralyze
feeding beetles (USDA-ARS) and can serve as a trap-and-kill border; four o'clocks and
larkspur are toxic to beetles but also to livestock and people. Beetles prefer zinnias,
sunflowers, roses, grapes, raspberries and silking sweet corn, so a sacrificial zinnia or
sunflower block 50+ ft from dahlias and premium blooms, hand-collected into soapy water
in the cool morning, is the practical cultural control; avoid pheromone bag traps near the
crop. Dependable tools: fine netting over high-value beds during the late June–August
flight, kaolin clay as a feeding deterrent, neem or azadirachtin for light pressure, Bt
galleriae for adults and grubs, milky spore or nematodes for grubs in nearby turf;
pyrethroids only as a last resort and never on open blooms. Verify Kansas labels.
