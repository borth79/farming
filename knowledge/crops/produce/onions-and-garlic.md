# Onions and garlic (storage onions, shallots, leeks; hardneck garlic incl. seed garlic)

## 1. Fit for this farm
- **Garlic is the best calendar fit of any produce crop here.** Planted in the first part of
  October (K-State Sedgwick County) right after wheat drilling, dormant through winter, scapes in
  May, harvested late June to mid-July — it overlaps wheat harvest for a week or two but garlic
  tolerates a few days in the ground and is dug in two or three half-days with the shared
  digger or undercutter. Curing is in July–August when the farm has shade and barn space.
- **Storage onions and specialty alliums** are a spring crop: transplants set mid-March to early
  April, harvest July–August, cure in the field or barn, sell September through March. They
  compete with potatoes for the same spring labor and with wheat harvest for July days.
- **Soil and water.** All alliums want loose, fertile, well-drained loam or sandy loam, pH
  6.0–7.0 (Sedgwick soils fit), and even moisture: ~1 in/week for onions April–July, less for
  garlic (stop watering 2 weeks before harvest). Drip on raised beds is standard; dryland garlic
  is possible on the farm's wetter soils but bulbs are smaller.
- **Equipment.** Tractors do bed prep, plastic/drip laying, cultivation and undercutting; the
  shared one-row digger or a $3,000–10,000 undercutter/lifter loosens bulbs. Planting garlic
  cloves and setting onion transplants are by hand at stage 1 (a waterwheel transplanter sets
  onions; garlic planters exist from $3,000 used). Topping, cleaning and grading are hand work.
- **Storable.** Onions 32°F 65–70% RH 4–8 months; garlic 32°F 65–70% RH (or 55–60°F dry) 6–9
  months; shallots like onions; leeks are fresh (1–2 months at 32°F, or overwintered in the
  field). All sell in the Oct–Apr winter window.
- **Trust.** Nothing at stage 1 triggers the trust checklist.
- **Verdict on fit:** garlic is the highest-value, best-timed crop in the produce set; storage
  onions are a low-price commodity that only make sense as a chef and market add-on
  (competitor-analysis: never commodity yellow onions to a DC).

## 3. Agronomy
### Garlic
- **Planting.** First half of October in Sedgwick County (K-State), after soil cools below 60°F
  and 4–6 weeks before hard freeze so roots form without top growth. Crack bulbs within 1–2 days
  of planting; plant largest cloves, pointed end up, 2–3 in deep, 6 in in-row, 3–4 rows per
  30–36 in bed (or 18 in single rows) → ~45,000–60,000 cloves/ac, 1,000–1,500 lb seed. Mulch
  with 3–4 in straw or chopped stover after planting (winter protection, weed control, soil
  moisture).
- **Fertility.** 100–120 lb N/ac total: a third in fall, the rest in two spring side-dressings
  by early May (N after bulbing starts delays maturity); P and K to test; sulfur improves flavor
  on low-S soils. Budget $200/ac plus $300 straw mulch.
- **Irrigation.** Spring moisture March–May is critical for bulb size; drip 0.75–1 in/week in dry
  spells; stop 2 weeks before harvest to prevent staining and rot.
- **Scapes.** Remove hardneck scapes in late May when they curl (sell to chefs at $4–8/lb; a
  10–20% bulb-size gain).
- **Pests and diseases.** **Garlic bloat nematode** (Ditylenchus dipsaci) is the industry's
  disqualifying pest: it arrives in seed, persists years, and bars seed sales. Buy only tested
  seed, test the farm's own seed stock yearly ($50–100 per sample, Cornell/UMaine labs), rotate
  4 years away from alliums, celery, parsley and salsify. Also white rot (Sclerotium cepivorum;
  permanent soil infestation — never plant in a field with history), Fusarium basal rot, Botrytis
  neck rot in wet curing, rust and purple blotch in wet springs, onion thrips (less damaging on
  garlic), and onion maggot. No Kansas-specific garlic pest problems beyond these were found.
- **Harvest.** Late June to mid-July when 40–50% of leaves have browned (5–6 green leaves left);
  undercut with a bar or shallow digger pass, hand lift, keep tops on, keep out of sun. Do not
  wash.
- **Curing.** Hang or rack in a shaded, airy barn with fans at 75–90°F and moderate humidity for
  3–6 weeks until necks are dry; then trim tops to 1 in, trim roots, brush, grade by diameter
  (≥2.5 in seed/jumbo, 2–2.5 in chef, <2 in market or peeled).
- **Storage.** 32–40°F at 60–70% RH for 6–9 months, or 55–60°F dry ambient for 4–6 months; never
  a humid cooler (sprouting and mold). Seed garlic is held at 50–60°F until October planting.

### Storage onions, shallots, leeks
- **Planting.** Onion transplants (pencil-thick, 8–10 weeks old) mid-March to early April when
  soil can be worked (K-State window mid-Feb southeast to mid-April northwest), 4 in in-row in
  3–4 rows per bed on drip (80,000–100,000 plants/ac); intermediate-day varieties bulb in Kansas
  day lengths (K-State MF761). Shallots from bulbs in October or transplants in March at 6 in.
  Leeks transplanted April–May at 6 in in 6 in-deep furrows, hilled for white shank.
- **Fertility.** 120–150 lb N/ac split, heaviest before bulbing (mid-May), P and K to test;
  stop N by early June. Budget $300/ac.
- **Irrigation.** 1 in/week April–early July on drip; stop when 50% of tops fall over.
- **Weeds.** The main labor sink: alliums do not shade weeds; plastic mulch or 2–3 cultivations
  plus hand weeding (~40 hr/ac); pre-emergence options exist (pendimethalin, DCPA) — check labels.
- **Insects.** **Onion thrips** are the key Kansas allium pest, worst in hot dry weather and when
  onions sit next to wheat or alfalfa (thrips migrate from drying small grains in June — relevant
  on a wheat farm; keep onion beds away from wheat edges). Scout weekly from May; threshold ~1–3
  thrips per leaf; spinosad, spinetoram, or conventional programs rotated by class (bio products
  alone failed in Michigan trials); overhead irrigation knocks them back. Onion maggot in cool wet
  springs on ground with rotting residue.
- **Diseases.** Purple blotch and Stemphylium leaf blight in humid June weather (fungicide
  program at 7–14 days in wet years), downy mildew, pink root (rotation, resistant varieties),
  Botrytis neck rot and bacterial soft rot in storage (cure fully, store dry and cold).
- **Harvest.** Late July–August when 50–80% of tops are down; undercut/lift with the digger or
  bar, windrow 3–7 days in the field if dry (shade from direct sun in Kansas heat to prevent
  sunscald), or lift into crates and cure in the barn with fans 2–4 weeks until necks are tight.
  Top to 1 in, grade (jumbo ≥3 in, medium 2–3 in), bag in 50 lb mesh or 25 lb for chefs.
- **Storage.** 32°F, 65–70% RH, air movement: sweet intermediate-day types (Candy) 2–4 months,
  pungent storage types 5–8 months; shallots 6–8 months; leeks 1–2 months at 32°F, 95% RH, or
  field-stored under mulch for winter harvest.

## 9. Profitability verdict
- **Best channel: chef-direct for both, with the farmers market close behind for garlic** (the
  market pays $12 but Wichita's winter markets move only 50–100 lb a day; chefs take 3–5 lb a
  week each all winter). Seed garlic sales become a second channel from year 3 once the farm's
  stock is tested clean. Grocery takes garlic at $5 as overflow; **storage onions to grocery lose
  money** and are grown only for the chef and market mix (chefs want a local onion on the same
  invoice as the garlic and potatoes).
- **Stage-1 acres: 0.75 ac = 0.25 ac hardneck garlic (≈ 1,500 lb marketable, 250 lb saved as
  seed) + 0.5 ac onions, shallots and leeks (≈ 12,500 lb)**, planted after LOIs; garlic seed
  (~250–300 lb, $5,000–6,000) ordered by August for October planting.
- **Family income at FAMILY rate (blended): ~$10,600/ac, ~$7,900 for 0.75 ac** (~$2,500 wages
  on ~225 hr plus ~$5,400 profit); garlic alone ~$19,800/ac, onions ~$5,900/ac. Net at
  HIRED_SEASONAL ~$5,200/ac blended.
- What changes the verdict: garlic yield below ~3,500 lb/ac or chef price below ~$4.50/lb still
  clears costs — garlic survives every adverse case tested, so the only garlic disqualifiers are
  pests (bloat nematode, white rot) and labor availability in October and July; onions drop out
  if chefs pay under $0.85/lb or yield is under ~21,000 lb/ac; if garlic proves itself at 0.25
  ac, grow it to 0.5–1 ac and buy a used planter before adding any onion acres.

## 10. Risks and disqualifiers
| Risk | Likelihood | Effect | Control / disqualifier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Garlic bloat nematode in purchased seed | low–medium | multi-year field infestation; no seed-garlic sales | buy tested seed only; test own stock yearly; **disqualifier for seed-garlic channel if a positive test** |
| White rot in a field | low | permanent loss of the field for alliums | never plant alliums in a field with any history; rotate 4 yr |
| Year-1 garlic seed cost ($18–25k/ac) | certain | $5–6k cash out at 0.25 ac before any sale | start at 0.25 ac; save seed from year 1 |
| Garlic harvest overlaps wheat harvest | high | week delay, over-mature bulbs split | plant an early Asiatic/Turban block; dig early porcelain in the last week of June before wheat; mulch keeps soil cool |
| Onion thrips from adjacent wheat and alfalfa | high on this farm | 20–40% yield loss, small bulbs | isolate onion beds from wheat edges; weekly scouting; rotation of classes |
| Wet June–July curing (Botrytis neck rot) | medium | storage losses 10–30% | barn curing with fans; cull before storage |
| Commodity onion price ($0.42–0.60 terminal) | certain | grocery onions negative | onions only in chef/market mix; cap at 0.5 ac |
| Chefs will not pay $8 for garlic | medium | net falls ~$4,200/ac per $1 | sample first; floor $5; market and seed channels absorb |
| Hand planting and cleaning labor in October and July–August | medium | 60 + 80 hr for 0.25 ac garlic lands on busy weeks | family labor scheduled; planter at 0.5 ac+ |
| Deer, rabbits | low | garlic is rarely browsed; onion tops sometimes | fence if needed |
