## Fit for this operation

**Why sorghum belongs in the Sedgwick County rotation**
- South-central Kansas (~30–33 in annual precipitation, hot, drought- and hail-prone) is
  core dryland sorghum country. Kansas is the number-one U.S. sorghum state (NASS).
- Sorghum is the C4 summer feed grain with the lowest drought and heat risk of the
  summer crops in scope; it is the natural dryland summer-crop partner to winter wheat
  in a wheat–sorghum–(soybean/fallow) rotation and the default choice when corn is too
  risky without irrigation.
- Equipment fit: planted with the row-crop planter (30-in rows) or an air drill / planter
  on 15-in rows; harvested with the combine using the same platform/draper used for
  wheat (or a row-crop header); no dryer required in most years. Fits a 1,000-acre
  wheat-centered operation with no added machinery.
- Storage fit: stores like corn at 13–14 % moisture (verify against K-State storage
  guidance); small, round kernels pack tightly and bins need good aeration.

**Disqualifiers / cautions**
- Herbicide program is atrazine-based and sets rotation constraints (soybeans the next
  year are restricted on late atrazine applications; see Weeds and Rotation effects).
- Market is thin and export-dependent: China took the large majority of U.S. sorghum
  exports and the 2025 trade war pushed Kansas cash bids below $3.00/bu (Brownfield,
  Dec 2025). Basis is normally under corn and can collapse when China is absent.
- Sugarcane (sorghum) aphid is a recurring, scouting-intensive pest since 2015, though
  Kansas economic infestations have been "far less frequent since 2017" (MF742 2026).
- Wheat planted immediately after sorghum harvest is late and faces residue/N issues
  (allelopathy is inconsistent; the +30 lb N/ac requirement is the firmer constraint).
- Irrigated branch: under irrigation, corn usually out-yields and out-earns sorghum in
  this region; irrigated sorghum is mainly a water-limited (low well capacity) option.

**No-till into wheat stubble (the standard system here).** Plant directly into standing
wheat stubble the following late May–June after a fallow period (wheat harvest year 1 →
sorghum year 2). Benefits: stored moisture, erosion control, cooler soil. Requirements:
good residue flow on the planter (row cleaners), a burndown + residual program in the
stubble (see Weeds), and attention to seed-to-soil contact. Chinch bugs migrate from
maturing wheat into adjacent seedling sorghum; avoid planting sorghum next to wheat where
possible and consider an insecticide seed treatment in that situation (MF742; myfields).

### Hybrid selection
- Decide on **maturity** first (see table), then **stalk strength / stay-green** (lodging
  and stalk-rot tolerance in a region where charcoal and Fusarium stalk rot follow late-
  season drought), then **sugarcane aphid tolerance**, then herbicide-tolerance trait.
- K-State's Kansas Performance Tests with Grain Sorghum Hybrids (annual SRP report)
  list maturity, SCA rating and yield by location; use the south-central entries. In
  older tests DKS37-07 was rated the most SCA-resistant commercial hybrid (Southwestern
  Entomologist 2019); current SCA-tolerant lines exist from DeKalb, Pioneer, Sorghum
  Partners/Warner, Alta/Advanta and regional brands (Kauffman) — **get the 2025/2026
  SRP report and seed-guide ratings; not retrieved in this research**. Tolerant hybrids
  still require scouting.
- **Herbicide-tolerant systems available in Kansas, 2026** (K-State, Campbell 2026):

| System | Herbicide (active) | Timing / rate from sources | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| igrowth | ImiFlex (imazamox, Group 2) | PRE at 9 oz/ac with a Group 15 acetamide, or POST | Grass + broadleaf activity; imazamox-resistant shattercane confirmed in NW Kansas (K-State 2021); one source cites an 18-month plant-back to non-igrowth sorghum (**verify label**) |
| Inzen | Zest WDG (nicosulfuron, Group 2) | POST at V3–V7 and sorghum <20 in tall (label 4–20 in) | Grass control (incl. volunteer corn, foxtails); ALS-resistant weeds not controlled |
| Double Team | FirstAct (quizalofop, Group 1) | POST; one source gives 10 oz/ac on sorghum >11 in (**verify**) | Grass-only; do not plant Double Team in consecutive years (stewardship); Sorghum Partners (Double Team seed) acquired by Warner Seed Sept 2025 |

  None of these is labeled to control shattercane or johnsongrass (close relatives).
  They do nothing for Palmer amaranth; the broadleaf program is unchanged. Seed-cost
  premium plus herbicide cost must be weighed against the grass problem on each field.
- Seed safener: Concep III seed treatment is required to use S-metolachlor / Dual-type
  Group 15 herbicides on sorghum.

### Fertility detail
- **Nitrogen.** K-State's current recommendation (MF2586, as summarized in eUpdate 1102,
  2026) scales with expected yield, soil texture, cropping sequence and environment,
  with credits for profile nitrate (sample 0–24 in), previous legume, manure and soil
  organic matter (older editions credit ~20 lb N per 1 % OM for warm-season crops —
  **verify current table**). The older C687 formula was N = (yield goal × 1.25) − credits;
  per-bushel factors in different editions range 1.0–1.6 lb N/bu (**verify** current
  factor before use). Working rule for this farm: ~1.2 lb N per bushel of realistic
  yield goal minus profile-N and OM credits → ~80–110 lb N/ac for a 75–90 bu dryland
  goal; after a poor wheat crop or a wet spring, profile-N testing often cuts this.
  Timing: preplant or split with a side-dress (UAN) before the 8-leaf stage; in no-till
  stubble, surface urea/UAN losses argue for incorporation, placement or a stabilizer.
- **Phosphorus.** K-State sufficiency recommendation: P2O5 rec = 50 + (0.16 × expected
  yield) − (2.5 × Mehlich-3 P ppm) (C687/MF2586). Where soil P is adequate and only
  starter is suggested, 15 lb P2O5/ac. In-furrow starter must carry no more than
  10 lb/ac of N + K2O in contact with seed. Sorghum responds well to starter P on cool
  soils and in no-till.
- **Potassium.** Most Sedgwick County silt loams test medium-high; apply by soil test
  (K-State build/sufficiency tables in MF2586).
- **Iron chlorosis.** Sorghum is sensitive to iron deficiency on high-pH (>7.5),
  calcareous, often wetter or eroded spots. C687 names specific application methods as
  "most effective" (extract truncated); the standard tools are in-furrow iron chelate
  (EDDHA-type), foliar iron (iron sulfate / chelate) at first sign, planting hybrids with
  better iron efficiency, and avoiding high-pH spots for sorghum (**verify rates in
  C687/MF2586**). Band-placed acid-forming P fertilizer lowers pH in the band and helps.
  Sedgwick County soils are mostly neutral to slightly alkaline; map the problem spots.

### Weeds
- Key weeds: Palmer amaranth (reported resistant in Kansas to atrazine, glyphosate and
  other groups; one population resistant to dicamba and 2,4-D — eUpdate 1080, 2025),
  kochia (triazine-resistant kochia "found throughout Kansas"; glyphosate-resistant
  kochia common), large crabgrass, foxtails, shattercane/johnsongrass, volunteer wheat.
- **Preplant / PRE (the core of the program)**: burndown of stubble weeds (glyphosate
  + dicamba or 2,4-D; paraquat where glyphosate-resistant kochia/Palmer present), then
  atrazine + S-metolachlor (Bicep II Magnum-type premix; K-State research baseline).
  S-metolachlor can be applied up to 45 days before planting Concep III-treated seed
  (eUpdate 1102, 2026). Atrazine alone is weak on grasses — "depending on it for grass
  control is generally not wise" (K-State). Other PRE options: mesotrione + S-metolachlor
  + atrazine premixes (Lumax/Lexar-type), dimethenamid (Outlook), acetochlor (Warrant,
  Degree Xtra) — check the K-State Chemical Weed Control Guide (SRP, annual).
- **POST**: atrazine (3-leaf to 12 in, with COC/surfactant on emerged weeds); Huskie
  (pyrasulfotole + bromoxynil) best tank-mixed with atrazine, but large Palmer escaped
  Huskie + atrazine in K-State trials (eUpdate 1107, 2026) — spray Palmer under 3–4 in;
  dicamba and 2,4-D (height/growth-stage limits, drift risk to soybeans/cotton); Aim can
  be added but burns sorghum leaves. Grass POST only via HT systems (Zest, FirstAct,
  ImiFlex). Delayed planting (after mid-June) shrinks the POST window and favors a strong
  PRE (eUpdate 1110, 2026).
- **Atrazine rotation restrictions (critical for this farm's rotation)**:
  - Soybeans: label historically allowed soybeans only the year after application and,
    where atrazine was applied after June 10, only corn or sorghum the following season
    (K-State summary of Aatrex label; older — **verify current Kansas label**). Practical
    rule: do not plan full-season soybeans the spring after a sorghum crop that received
    atrazine after ~June 10, and never double-crop soybeans into atrazine-treated
    sorghum ground.
  - Wheat: a 12-month interval appears on general rotation tables (Mississippi State
    table) and wheat planted the fall after a spring/June atrazine application on sorghum
    carries injury risk, especially in a dry summer at higher rates; Kansas labels have
    specific rate caps and language for wheat-sorghum-fallow systems — **verify Kansas
    24(c)/label**. Field experience: ≤1 lb ai applied by early June on medium-textured
    soils with normal summer rain is usually safe for October wheat; dry summers and
    high-pH soils raise carryover risk.
  - 2026 label change: the only non-crop (fallow) uses of atrazine now permitted are on
    wheat stubble in wheat-fallow-wheat, wheat-corn-fallow and wheat-sorghum-fallow
    rotations (eUpdate 1106, 2026-06-04).
  - Group 2 (imazamox) and Group 27 (mesotrione) products have their own plant-backs;
    read labels for wheat/soybean/sunflower.

### Diseases (MF3046 2022; L741; K-State disease summaries)
- **Fusarium stalk rot**: the predominant Kansas sorghum disease (2017 survey); red/purple
  internal pith; worse with late-season stress, high populations, high N:K imbalance.
  Manage with stalk-strength / stay-green hybrids, moderate population, balanced
  fertility, timely harvest (lodging risk).
- **Charcoal rot** (*Macrophomina*): hot, droughty late season; gray stalk interior with
  black microsclerotia; lodging. Lower seeding rates, stay-green hybrids, harvest
  promptly after black layer.
- **Anthracnose** (leaf, stalk, head): low levels in Kansas; rotation and residue
  management; some hybrid differences; more common in wetter SE Kansas.
- **Head molds / grain mold** (Fusarium, Alternaria, Curvularia): wet weather at
  flowering–maturity; open-headed hybrids, timely harvest; test for mycotoxins if damaged.
- **Sorghum downy mildew**: infects soon after emergence in saturated soils; found in
  chronic wet spots; resistant hybrids, drainage, metalaxyl seed treatment.
- **Ergot**: cool, wet weather at bloom; rare in Kansas grain fields.
- Fungicides are rarely economic in dryland Kansas sorghum (MF3046).

Timeliness penalties: planting after June 25 loses insurance coverage after the late
period; late-planted sorghum flowers in the September midge/aphid window and may not
reach black layer before frost. Harvest delay past dry-down risks lodging in stalk-rot
years and bird/weather losses.

## Harvest, drying and storage
- **Physiological maturity**: black layer at the kernel base; grain ~30–35 % moisture.
  Harvest aids do not dry grain; they dry leaves/stalks for faster, cleaner combining
  (Sorghum Checkoff).
- **Glyphosate desiccation**: apply at black layer / ≤30 % grain moisture; 7-day PHI;
  K-State statewide trials show no yield loss when applied at black layer (eUpdates 2014,
  2016; MF3046 2020). Kills the plant and stops water use — the main tool for getting
  wheat planted on time after sorghum.
- **Sodium chlorate**: leaf desiccant only, does not kill the plant, regrowth possible;
  apply at <30 % (OSU) to <20 % grain moisture about 5 days before harvest (Arkansas).
- **Harvest moisture**: combine at 18–20 % down to 14 %; sell/store at ≤14 %; dry to
  13 % for long storage (**verify** K-State storage guidance). Drying is rarely needed in
  Kansas; field dry-down is slow in cool Octobers, and a desiccant plus an early-maturing
  hybrid is the usual substitute for a dryer.
- **Combine settings (general; verify against operator's manual)**: cylinder/rotor
  speed lower than wheat (roughly 500–800 rpm), concave fairly open, high fan speed with
  care (small kernels blow easily), sieves tighter than corn; use a row-crop header or
  platform with reel set to minimize head shatter; check for kernels left in the heads.
- **Lodging**: stalk rots after late-season drought; harvest promptly, run the header
  low, and prioritize fields with weak stalks.
- **Storage**: aerate quickly (sorghum has more fines and foreign material than corn);
  monitor temperature; 13–14 % moisture long term; dockage/foreign material discounts at
  elevators are common.

## Marketing
- **Buyers**: Kansas ethanol plants (Kansas Ethanol LLC, Lyons, 80 MMgy corn + sorghum;
  Western Plains Energy, Oakley, historically ~8.8 M bu sorghum/yr; others in the state
  grind sorghum seasonally), Kansas/Oklahoma/Texas feedlots, and the export channel via
  the Texas Gulf (Houston/Corpus Christi) and Pacific Northwest. Roughly one-third of
  U.S. sorghum has gone to ethanol in recent years; grain sorghum ethanol qualifies for
  advanced D5 RINs under facility-specific EPA pathways (2021). Local Sedgwick/Harvey/
  Sumner county cooperatives and Wichita-area terminals post milo bids — **list specific
  elevators and their milo policy (some refused deliveries in late 2025) in
  `knowledge/markets/` once confirmed**.
- **Basis vs corn**: milo normally trades at a discount to corn in Kansas (typically
  $0.20–0.60/bu under corn; **verify** with AMS Kansas Daily Grain Bids, report ams_2886),
  narrowing or inverting when China is buying aggressively (as in 2020–2021) and widening
  sharply when China is absent (2018, 2025). A sorghum–corn spread futures contract was
  announced in Aug 2026 (Oklahoma Farm Report), which may improve hedging.
- **Cash bids observed**: Kansas City sorghum $3.98/bu (2026-10-30); Midland Marketing
  (central KS) $3.75–3.81/bu Oct 2026 delivery; Garden City Coop $3.83–3.88 (Sep–Dec
  2026). USDA projects a 2026/27 U.S. season-average sorghum farm price of $4.60/bu vs
  $3.70 for 2025/26.
- **China dependence and tariff history**: China has taken ~80–90 % of U.S. sorghum
  exports in most years (farmer/industry quotes). 2018: Chinese anti-dumping probe and
  retaliatory tariffs collapsed demand. March 2025: China imposed an additional 10 %
  tariff on U.S. sorghum (15 % on corn/wheat); by July 2025 U.S. sorghum exports were down
  ~80 % y/y and sales to China down ~97 %; Kansas bids fell below $3.00/bu by Dec 2025 as
  a record 252 M bu Kansas crop arrived. China removed the extra agricultural tariffs
  effective 2025-11-10 (USDA FAS CH2026-0085) and bought >3 MMT of U.S. sorghum for
  2025/26 by early 2026 (Reuters, Feb 2026), though volumes lagged expectations as Brazil
  supplied corn and sorghum (Ukragroconsult, 2026). Australia and Argentina backfilled
  during the 2025 gap. Treat Chinese demand as the single largest price driver and a
  binary political risk; see `knowledge/geopolitics/`.
- **Contracts**: forward cash contracts and HTAs are priced off corn futures (CBOT) with
  a milo basis; ethanol plants sometimes offer direct contracts. Quality: U.S. No. 2
  sorghum; discounts for test weight <55–56 lb/bu, moisture >14 %, foreign material, and
  sprout/mold damage (**verify** local discount schedules).
- **Farm programs**: sorghum has its own PLC/ARC base; the 2025 farm-bill changes raised
  statutory reference prices (sorghum reference price cited around $4.51/bu — **verify**
  with FSA). Low 2025/26 prices increased the probability of PLC payments for 2025 crop
  (RFD-TV / K-State).

## Risk profile
- **Weather**: lowest drought/heat sensitivity of the summer grains; still yields 39–90
  bu/ac statewide across 2022–2025. Hail at heading and early frost on late plantings are
  the other main losses. Late-season drought → stalk rot → lodging.
- **Pest**: sugarcane aphid (episodic, 1–2 sprays at $12–18/ac when it hits), headworms,
  chinch bugs next to wheat; herbicide-resistant Palmer and kochia raise program cost.
- **Price**: thin, export-driven market with the highest political exposure of any crop
  in this rotation (China). Basis volatility exceeds corn's.
- **Input geopolitics**: N fertilizer price exposure similar to corn per acre but lower
  per-acre N use; herbicide supply (atrazine regulatory review in the EPA, S-metolachlor
  label changes) is a standing watch item.
- **Regulatory**: EPA atrazine mitigation (label changes 2024–2026) and ESA-driven
  herbicide labels can change the core program with little notice.

## Rotation effects
- **Wheat after sorghum (the timing problem)**: sorghum harvest (mid-Oct to Nov) overlaps
  or follows the optimal wheat-planting window for Sedgwick County (~Oct 1–20, verify
  with wheat file). Options: (a) desiccate sorghum with glyphosate at black layer to
  harvest earlier and plant wheat 1–3 weeks sooner; (b) plant wheat late at a higher
  seeding rate; (c) insert fallow or a double-crop/soybean year (wheat–sorghum–soybean
  or wheat–sorghum–fallow–wheat). K-State: apply an **additional 30 lb N/ac** to wheat
  after sorghum (or sunflower) because of residue immobilization; allelopathy from
  sorghum residue is real but inconsistent (some trials show reduced double-crop wheat
  yield, others none) and should not by itself stop no-till wheat after sorghum;
  terminating sorghum early lets compounds dissipate. Long-term Tribune data: recrop
  wheat yields ~75 % of wheat following sorghum (rotation benefit of the summer crop).
- **Soybeans after sorghum**: agronomically good (grass→legume, breaks weed cycles) but
  governed by atrazine plant-back (see Weeds). Soybeans after sorghum are the natural
  home for a Group 2/15-based sorghum program when a soybean year is planned. No Kansas
  double-crop soybean-after-sorghum data were found (gap).
- **Sorghum after wheat**: the best slot — stored stubble moisture, residue, broadleaf
  weed break; Tribune 2008–2023: sorghum after wheat averaged 85.6 bu/ac vs 59.6 for corn
  after wheat (sponsored source citing K-State; verify).
- **Sorghum after corn or sorghum**: avoid continuous sorghum (stalk rot, shattercane,
  johnsongrass, Palmer build-up; Double Team cannot be planted consecutively). Volunteer
  corn is controllable only with Inzen/Double Team or igrowth systems.
- **N credit**: none to the following crop; sorghum residue ties up N (hence +30 lb N for
  wheat). High-residue, high-C:N stubble is good for erosion control and moisture.
- **Herbicide carryover summary**: atrazine → soybeans/sunflower/alfalfa next spring at
  risk (date- and rate-dependent); wheat in fall usually OK at moderate rates with normal
  rainfall; mesotrione → soybeans and sunflower restrictions; imazamox (igrowth) →
  18-month-type restrictions to some crops (verify).

## Drought tolerance and water use vs corn
- Seasonal ET ranges overlap (sorghum 16.0–30.6 in; corn 15.6–31.6 in, MF3208 2025), but
  sorghum's peak daily demand is lower, its critical window (boot–bloom) is shorter and
  later, and it can delay development under stress and still set grain; corn's pollination
  window is unforgiving. Sorghum's yield response per inch above its lower threshold is
  the reason it wins in the 20–28 in effective-rainfall band and loses to corn only when
  water is ample (K-State MF3066 gives the production-function numbers — not retrieved,
  **verify**).
- K-State long-term dryland data (Tribune, west KS) show sorghum ~40 % higher yield than
  corn in the same rotations; in south-central Kansas with ~30 in rainfall the gap is
  smaller and corn can win on deep soils in wet years, which is why the decision is made
  field by field and year by year (see Decision rules).

## Decision rules for this farm (orchestrator inputs)

1. **Default dryland summer crop after wheat is grain sorghum** on fields without
   irrigation and on any field with a productivity index or soil depth below the farm
   median.
2. **Switch the summer crop to dryland corn** on a field only when all hold: deep silt
   loam with ≥4 in plant-available water in the top 4 ft at planting (soil probe), a
   neutral-to-wet spring outlook (ENSO/CPC) in `knowledge/weather/`, the corn:sorghum
   expected-revenue ratio (APH yield × forward price, less direct cost) exceeds 1.10, and
   corn APH on that field ≥ 90 bu/ac. Otherwise sorghum.
3. **Switch from sorghum to full-season soybeans** on a field when the soybean expected
   margin exceeds sorghum's by >$25/ac at planning prices, the field is on the north/
   east (wetter) side of the operation or has better water holding, and either the field
   received no atrazine after June 10 the prior season or the prior crop was wheat
   (plant-back clear). Also favor soybeans whenever the China/sorghum export picture is
   in an "absent buyer" state (see geopolitics scenario flags) and milo basis is wider
   than $0.60 under corn.
4. **Sorghum beats corn by default when** the 90-day outlook is dry/hot, nitrogen is
   expensive relative to grain (N cost >$0.60/lb), operating credit is tight (lower cash
   outlay per acre), or planting is pushed past May 25 (corn's dryland yield penalty is
   steeper than sorghum's).
5. **Hybrid/trait rule**: use an HT system only on fields with a documented grass problem
   (shattercane excluded, since none controls it); otherwise conventional hybrid plus the
   atrazine + S-metolachlor PRE. Choose SCA-tolerant, stay-green, medium maturity by
   default; early maturity if planting after June 15 or if wheat follows immediately.
6. **Wheat-after-sorghum rule**: if wheat follows, select an early/medium-early hybrid,
   plan a glyphosate harvest aid at black layer, budget +30 lb N for the wheat, and set
   a hard wheat-planting cutoff from the wheat file; if that cutoff cannot be met, the
   plan moves that field to wheat–sorghum–soybean (or fallow) instead.
7. **Marketing rule**: price at least a third of expected production at or before planting
   whenever the forward milo bid exceeds full-cost breakeven at the field's adverse-case
   yield; do not store unpriced milo into a year when China purchases are suspended.
8. **Insurance rule**: RP at 70–75 % with the sorghum projected price (~$4.55 for 2026);
   check the Margin Coverage Option for grain sorghum (RMA 26-MCO-PP-0051) when N prices
   are high; keep planting before June 25 to hold full coverage.
