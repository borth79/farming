# Soybeans — full-season

> Scope: soybeans planted as the primary summer crop (May–mid June) on dryland in
> Sedgwick County, Kansas, with irrigated differences noted. Double-crop soybeans
> after wheat are in `soybeans-double-crop.md`, which cross-references this file for
> shared agronomy.

> Sourcing note (2026-10-31): the environment could not open PDFs on agmanager.info,
> nass.usda.gov, rma.usda.gov or k-state.edu; figures come from search excerpts of
> those documents. Any figure marked "verify" must be checked against the primary
> document before it is used in a plan.

## Fit for this operation
- Climate: south-central Kansas receives roughly 30–33 in of precipitation a year
  (farm-profile.md), concentrated in May–June, with July–August heat and frequent
  flash drought during flowering and pod fill. This is the single biggest yield risk
  and the reason Sedgwick County dryland soybean yields swing 2–3x between years.
- Soil: best fit on deeper silt loams and silty clay loams with good water-holding
  capacity (Arkansas River alluvium, upland silt loams). Poor fit on shallow, sandy
  or high-pH (>7.3) calcareous soils, where iron deficiency chlorosis (IDC) and
  drought stress limit yield. Field-by-field soil series must be entered in
  farm-profile.md before soybeans are assigned.
- Rotation: the standard rotation partner for grain sorghum and corn; breaks grass
  diseases and leaves an N credit of about 40 lb N/acre (K-State L778, MF2586;
  verify current table). Also the main double-crop option after wheat.
- Equipment: no-till planter (30-in or 15-in) or no-till drill; sprayer with
  dicamba/2,4-D stewardship capability if those trait platforms are used; combine
  with a flex/draper header. Soybeans need no N applicator and no dryer in most
  years, which lowers the fixed-cost burden compared with corn.
- Storage: soybeans store well at 13% moisture or below; existing wheat/sorghum bins
  are suitable. Harvest overlaps late sorghum and precedes wheat seeding, so labor
  timing is generally workable.
- Economics: the no-nitrogen crop. When N is expensive (urea above about $550/ton)
  and the soybean/sorghum price ratio is wide, soybeans displace sorghum on the
  better soils. See "Comparison vs grain sorghum" below.
- Disqualifiers: fields with confirmed high SCN populations and no resistant
  variety plan; fields with pH >7.5 and a history of severe IDC; fields with
  Palmer amaranth resistant to the herbicide platform available.
- Irrigated branch: if water rights are confirmed, irrigated soybeans in SC Kansas
  commonly yield 55–70 bu/acre (K-State irrigated budgets; verify) and become a
  strong rotation partner with irrigated corn. Irrigation timing R3–R6 is the
  highest-value water use.

### Planting window
- K-State Soybean Production Handbook: planting in south-central Kansas runs from late
  May into early July; K-State's 2016 eUpdate reported yields at Hutchinson and
  Wellington peaked with late-April planting, and K-State recommends early planting
  for south-central Kansas when soil conditions allow. For drier areas and shallow
  soils, yields have been most consistent with late-May to early-June planting.
- Statewide, K-State reports maximum yield falls about 0.3 bu/acre per day as planting
  is delayed, with potential near 80–90 bu/acre for mid-April planting versus about
  50 bu/acre for mid-July (eUpdate, c. 2021–2022; applies to yield potential, not
  dryland averages).
- Farm rule: target 5 May to 5 June for full-season. Plant when soil at 2 in is at or
  above 55–60°F with a warming forecast. Do not plant before the crop-insurance
  earliest planting date for Sedgwick County (verify; typically late April in Kansas).
- Final planting date for insurance: Kansas counties carry 15, 20 or 25 June final
  planting dates for non-irrigated soybeans, with a 25-day late planting period at a
  1%/day coverage reduction (Harris Crop Insurance chart, 2025-03; verify the
  Sedgwick County date in the 2027 actuarial documents, believed to be June 25; verify).

### Seed treatment and inoculant
- Base fungicide + insecticide seed treatment (for example metalaxyl/mefenoxam +
  fludioxonil + strobilurin + a neonicotinoid) is standard on treated commercial seed
  and is justified for early planting into cool no-till soils, for Phytophthora/
  Pythium stand protection and for early bean leaf beetle suppression.
- SDS/SCN-protectant seed treatment (fluopyram, ILeVO; or pydiflumetofen, Saltro):
  K-State's 2026 eUpdate lists these as the second line of defense after variety
  choice in fields with SDS history; K-State 2019–2020 trials found ILeVO added to a
  base treatment performed most consistently across environments and that ILeVO and
  Saltro gave comparable SDS control. Expect roughly $10–15/acre added (verify with
  dealer). Use only on fields with a documented SDS or SCN problem.
- Inoculant (Bradyrhizobium japonicum): required on any field without soybeans in the
  previous 3–4 years, after flooding, or where pH is below 6.0 or above 7.5.
  Low cost (about $2.50/acre in the Nebraska 2026 budget; Kansas figure verify).
  Cheap insurance; apply on all first-time soybean fields.
- Iron chelate seed treatment (o,o-EDDHA): on fields with pH above 7.4 and IDC
  history, K-State found seed-applied iron chelate gave significant yield increases
  under IDC-favorable conditions; foliar iron greened plants but gave inconsistent
  yield response (eUpdate 745, 2019).

### No-till system
- Soybeans are well suited to no-till into sorghum, corn or wheat residue; residue
  conserves the moisture that limits SC Kansas yields. Plant into standing stubble
  with row cleaners set to move residue without moving soil.
- Fall or early-spring burndown is needed for marestail and kochia (both have
  glyphosate-resistant populations in Kansas); include a residual.
- Avoid planting into a thick, wet residue mat on cold soils; delay until soil
  temperature is adequate rather than tilling.

### Water and temperature needs
- Seasonal water use roughly 18–24 in for a 40–50 bu crop (verify; K-State irrigation
  guides); peak use about 0.3 in/day at R3–R5.
- Most yield-sensitive period: K-State handbook states stress from R4 to R6.5 reduces
  yield more than at any other stage. Secondary sources citing K-State put the heat
  threshold at sustained daytime temperatures above about 95°F during flowering
  (R1–R2), and 90–95°F during pod set (R3–R4), worsened by dry soil (verify against
  primary K-State text).
- K-State 2026 yield-estimation guidance: average 2.5 seeds per pod (range 1–4); under
  severe drought and heat a pessimistic expectation is 1–1.5 seeds per pod. Soybeans
  compensate for stress better than corn when the stress is before seed fill.
- Frost: seedlings tolerate light frost poorly after emergence; a killing frost
  before R7 cuts seed size. MG IV planted by early June matures well ahead of the
  average first fall freeze (late October in Wichita; verify).

### Irrigated differences
- Population 130,000–160,000 seeds/acre; narrow rows preferred; MG 4.0–4.5.
- Irrigation scheduling priority R3–R6; avoid heavy early irrigation that encourages
  rank growth and Phytophthora/SDS.
- SDS and Phytophthora pressure rise under irrigation (K-State 2026: SDS is a
  persistent problem in irrigated Kansas River Valley fields).
- Target yield 55–70 bu/acre (verify against K-State irrigated budget for SC Kansas).

## Fertility
- Nitrogen: none. Soybeans fix their N; applied N suppresses nodulation and increases
  IDC risk (high soil nitrate magnifies iron chlorosis — K-State eUpdate 745).
- Phosphorus: K-State recommendations (MF2586) use Bray P1; sufficiency equations are
  keyed to yield goal and soil-test P. Working critical level about 20 ppm Bray P1
  (verify against MF2586). Below critical, apply build-plus-removal; above, apply
  removal or skip when P is expensive (fall 2026 DAP $936/ton is expensive relative
  to the 5-year range — see fertilizer-phosphorus-potassium.md). Apply P ahead of
  the wheat or sorghum in the rotation rather than directly to soybeans where
  logistics favor it; soybeans respond to residual P.
- Potassium: K-State uses Mehlich-3 or ammonium acetate K; working critical level about
  130 ppm (verify against MF2586). Many SC Kansas soils are adequate; test first.
- pH and lime: K-State 2026 liming eUpdate: if soybeans or alfalfa will be grown and
  pH is below 6.0, apply the full lime rate; SE Kansas guidance considers lime below
  6.1 and some research supports liming at 6.4 and below. Target pH 6.0–6.8. Lime
  ahead of soybeans in the rotation (6–12 months before planting). Do not over-lime
  on soils already near 7.0; it worsens IDC and reduces P and Mn availability.
- Iron deficiency chlorosis (high-pH soils): triggered by pH >7.0, free carbonates,
  high salinity, low DTPA-Fe, and high nitrate; severity varies year to year with
  plant stress (K-State eUpdate 1110, 2026-07-02). Management order: avoid the
  worst fields; tolerant variety; o,o-EDDHA iron chelate seed treatment; higher
  seeding rate in affected zones (more roots per acre); companion oat strip is not
  recommended here; foliar Fe is a rescue with inconsistent yield response.
- Sulfur, molybdenum, chloride: generally not limiting for soybeans in SC Kansas;
  test tissue before investing. Mo deficiency is tied to low pH and is corrected by
  liming.
- Sampling: soil test every 2–3 years, 0–6 in, by zone; add an SCN egg count on any
  field going to soybeans for the first time or showing unexplained yield drag.

## Weeds
### Key species and resistance status in Kansas
| Weed | Resistance in Kansas | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Palmer amaranth | ALS (Group 2) assumed statewide; glyphosate (9) widespread; PPO (14) confirmed; populations with resistance to six groups documented (2021); dicamba/2,4-D tolerance emerging regionally | The driver of the whole program. Emerges May–August; a PRE-only program fails. |
| Waterhemp | ALS; glyphosate (first Kansas case 2006); PPO confirmed | More in eastern Kansas and bottom ground. |
| Kochia | Glyphosate resistant common; ALS resistant; dicamba-resistant populations reported in the western Plains | Early emerging (March–April); control at burndown; dicamba platforms have an edge. |
| Marestail (horseweed) | Glyphosate and ALS resistant common | Fall or early spring burndown with 2,4-D/dicamba + residual; hard to kill once bolted. |
| Grasses (foxtails, crabgrass, shattercane, johnsongrass) | Generally glyphosate susceptible | Group 15 residuals and glyphosate/glufosinate handle them. |

Kansas list; K-State pigweed studies. Confidence medium.

### Program principles (K-State)
- Start clean: burndown plus a PRE residual applied before pigweed emergence.
- Layer residuals: PRE (Group 14 flumioxazin or sulfentrazone, plus Group 5
  metribuzin and/or Group 15 S-metolachlor/acetochlor/pyroxasulfone) followed by a
  POST with another Group 15 to extend control through canopy. K-State: Group 14
  residuals give excellent pigweed and morningglory control but little grass control
  and can injure seedlings via poor furrow closure or rain splash. K-State
  double-crop work found PRE-followed-by-POST programs gave greater Palmer control
  than either alone, and that delayed POST timing leaves pigweeds too large.
- POST timing: Palmer amaranth under 3–4 in tall. In a K-State trial dicamba gave
  better Palmer and waterhemp control than 2,4-D; 2,4-D gave highly variable control
  in a double-crop study, so glufosinate should be in the Enlist E3 tank mix on
  Palmer fields.
- Rotate sites of action across the rotation (sorghum and wheat years carry different
  chemistry).
- Narrow rows and early canopy closure are part of the program (MF3154).

### Label restrictions, 2026 season (check for 2027)
- Dicamba over-the-top (XtendiMax, Engenia, Tavium and successors): EPA registered
  OTT dicamba on 2026-02-06 for the 2026 and 2027 seasons. The new label drops the
  fixed calendar cutoff and imposes temperature-based requirements (volatility
  reducing agents on hotter days), plus the existing time-of-day, wind and buffer
  rules. K-State's 2026 cutoff table lists dicamba through R1 for soybeans and notes
  OTT dicamba is subject to additional restrictions on temperature, time of day and
  expected rainfall; K-State weather analysis found an average of only 9 days in July
  had 2 or more hours when OTT dicamba could legally be applied. Litigation in the
  Ninth Circuit is ongoing; a stay would remove the product mid-season. Kansas
  Department of Agriculture state-specific restrictions and required training:
  verify before each season.
- 2,4-D choline (Enlist One, Enlist Duo): apply through R1 (K-State 2026 cutoff
  table); wind, buffer and nozzle requirements per label; no temperature-based
  volatility rule equivalent to dicamba. Kansas has no additional county
  prohibitions known (verify with KDA).
- Glufosinate: through R1 (bloom) per label; needs warm, humid, sunny conditions and
  high water volume (15–20 gal/acre).
- Planning rule: every planned OTT dicamba application needs a glufosinate fallback
  in the plan because the legal window may not occur.

## Heat and drought stress
- Critical period: R3–R6 (pod set through seed fill), typically late July through
  early September for mid-May planting of MG IV in Sedgwick County. Stress R4–R6.5
  reduces yield more than at any other stage (K-State handbook).
- Mechanisms: flower and pod abortion above about 95°F during flowering; reduced
  seeds per pod and seed size with drought during fill; leaf flipping and curling
  are the first visible signs (K-State 2022–2023 eUpdates).
- Mitigation on dryland: plant in the first half of the window so flowering starts
  before the hottest weeks; maintain residue cover; moderate populations; MG choice
  spreading flowering across July–August; avoid fields with low water-holding
  capacity. Soybeans' indeterminate flowering gives recovery potential if rain
  returns by mid-August, an advantage over corn.
- Irrigated: schedule to avoid any deficit R3–R6.

## Harvest, moisture and storage
- Maturity: R8 (95% of pods brown); harvest can begin 5–10 days later.
- Target harvest moisture 13%; K-State suggests starting at 14–15% so that little of
  the crop is harvested below 11%, where shatter losses rise sharply, and to avoid
  repeated wetting-drying cycles that split pods (K-State dry-down eUpdate; NDSU).
- Field loss check: 4 seeds per sq ft on the ground equals about 1 bu/acre (K-State).
  Target under 1 bu/acre total loss; most loss occurs at the header.
- Storage: 13% for winter storage, 11–12% for storage into summer; aerate to cool;
  soybeans do not need drying in most Kansas years. If bins have aeration and fans,
  harvest up to 15–16% and air-dry down.
- Dectes-infested fields and shatter-prone varieties are harvested first.
- Green stem and uneven maturity in late-planted or drought-stressed fields slow
  harvest; do not wait for stems to dry (Farm Progress/K-State).

## Risk profile
- Weather: drought/heat during R3–R6 (highest), hail, wet harvest (shatter, seed
  quality), spring cold/wet delaying planting. Yield coefficient of variation at the
  county level is high (roughly 35–45%; verify from QuickStats).
- Pests: Palmer amaranth resistance escalation; SCN; charcoal rot in dry years;
  podworm in late fields; Dectes lodging.
- Price: sensitive to China trade policy, biofuel policy (RFS, 45Z), South American
  crop size, soybean oil/meal spread. Historical 1-year price volatility about 20–25%.
- Regulatory: dicamba registration litigation (Ninth Circuit) could remove the
  product mid-season; seed-trait and herbicide label changes each year.
- Input geopolitics: low exposure to nitrogen; exposure to P (Morocco, China, Russia)
  and herbicide active ingredients (China supply of glyphosate, glufosinate, 2,4-D
  intermediates) — see `knowledge/geopolitics/`.
- Operational: soybean harvest coincides with wheat seeding; a wet October delays
  both.

## Rotation effects
- N credit to following corn or sorghum: about 40 lb N/acre (K-State L778; MF2586
  previous-crop adjustment -40 lb N/acre; verify current table). At a nitrogen cost of
  $0.60–0.90/lb N (urea $616/ton equals about $0.67/lb N; anhydrous $832/ton about
  $0.51/lb N, fall 2026 Kansas co-op prices), the credit is worth roughly $20–36/acre.
- Residue: light, decomposes quickly; good seedbed for wheat (soybeans harvested
  early enough for timely wheat planting in Sedgwick County with MG IV planted by
  early June). Low residue raises erosion and moisture-loss risk compared with
  sorghum stubble.
- Disease breaks: non-host for wheat and sorghum diseases; host for SCN and charcoal
  rot (charcoal rot also infects sorghum and corn, so soybean-sorghum rotations do
  not break it).
- Herbicide carryover into soybeans: atrazine from sorghum/corn (do not plant
  soybeans the next spring after a late or high-rate atrazine application; follow
  label rotation intervals), sulfonylurea wheat herbicides (chlorsulfuron, metsulfuron
  — long intervals on high-pH soils), and fomesafen/Group 14 carryover into wheat
  after soybeans (check label).
- Weed-control window: a soybean year allows Groups 10, 14, 15 and the platform
  herbicide to be used, which are unavailable or limited in sorghum.

## Comparison vs grain sorghum when nitrogen is expensive
- K-State 2026 SC budgets: sorghum priced at $4.56/bu with N at $0.89/lb (Nov 2025);
  soybeans at $10.53/bu. At those budget prices the soybean/sorghum price ratio was
  2.3; at fall-2026 cash (soy about $11.50, sorghum cash not retrieved — verify) it is
  likely wider.
- Sorghum on this farm needs roughly 1.1–1.2 lb N per bushel of yield goal less
  credits (K-State; verify), so an 80 bu goal takes about 90 lb N: $60/acre at
  $0.67/lb (urea, fall 2026) or $80/acre at the K-State budget $0.89/lb, plus
  application cost. Soybeans need zero.
- Decision rule: on class A and B soils, choose full-season soybeans over sorghum
  when (soybean price x soybean trend yield) minus (sorghum price x sorghum trend
  yield) exceeds the sorghum N and drying cost savings by less than $0, that is:
  prefer soybeans if soy_price x soy_yield + N_cost_sorghum > sorghum_price x
  sorghum_yield + $30 (the $30 reflects sorghum's lower drought risk and higher
  residue value). Worked example, class B: $11.50 x 36 = $414 + $60 N = $474 versus
  $4.50 x 80 = $360 + $30 = $390 → soybeans favored by about $84/acre. On class C
  soils sorghum's drought tolerance usually wins unless the ratio exceeds about 2.8.
- Keep sorghum where Palmer amaranth has escaped the soybean platform, on shallow or
  high-pH soils, and where wheat follows (sorghum harvest is later, so soybeans are
  the better wheat predecessor).

## Decision rules for this farm
1. Field eligibility: deep or moderately deep soils, pH 5.8–7.2, no severe IDC
   history, SCN tested within 3 years. Class C fields go to sorghum or wheat.
2. Variety: MG 4.2–4.8, SCN resistant, IDC score in the best third for any field
   with pH above 7.0, Enlist E3 platform by default; XtendFlex where kochia
   dominates; buy two MGs to spread flowering risk.
3. Planting: 5 May–5 June at 110,000–140,000 seeds/acre (30-in) or 120,000–150,000
   (15-in/drilled), 1–1.5 in deep, no-till, treated seed, inoculant on first-time
   fields. Stop full-season planting on 15 June and switch remaining acres to the
