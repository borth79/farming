# Wheat grazing: dual-purpose and graze-out wheat

> Scope: using hard red winter wheat as fall–winter (dual-purpose) or fall–spring (graze-out)
> pasture for stocker cattle on this Sedgwick County farm, either with owned cattle or by leasing
> the pasture to a cattle owner. The farm profile lists no cattle, fencing or water
> infrastructure; those are open items that decide whether this enterprise is feasible at all.
> Grain agronomy, costs and markets are in `wheat-hard-red-winter.md`; this file covers only
> what changes when cattle are added. Numbers tagged **verify** must be confirmed before use.

## Summary and decision rules for this farm
1. Dual-purpose wheat (graze Nov–early Mar, remove cattle at first hollow stem, harvest grain) is the default form if the farm grazes wheat at all. Full graze-out (no grain) is a spring decision, taken field by field at first hollow stem when the expected grazing value exceeds the expected grain margin.
2. Unless the operator already owns stockers or has cattle experience, lease the pasture out on a per-pound-of-gain or per-head-per-month basis rather than buying cattle. At October 2026 prices a 500–600 lb steer costs $2,200–2,600 per head; 300 head on 400 acres ties up roughly $700,000–780,000 with full cattle price risk. The lease captures most of the forage value with none of that exposure.
3. Only graze fields that (a) can be fenced and watered at reasonable cost, (b) are planted by about Sep 15–25 with 1.5–2x grain seeding rate and 60–90 lb/acre extra N, and (c) are not adjacent to green volunteer wheat (earlier planting raises wheat streak mosaic, Hessian fly and army cutworm risk).
4. Do not turn cattle in until wheat is well anchored (6–8 inches, crown roots developed, usually 6–8 weeks after emergence), and keep stocking at 250–600 lb of animal per acre in fall–winter; pull cattle when forage is grazed below 3–4 inches or soils are saturated.
5. Pull cattle at first hollow stem (FHS, 5/8 inch of hollow stem below the developing head, measured on ungrazed plants). Every day past FHS costs 1–5% of grain yield (K-State). Monitor from mid-February using the Kansas Mesonet FHS tool and field checks of the variety's known FHS date.
6. Expect a grain yield penalty of 0–15% with well-managed dual-purpose wheat and 25% or more if removal is late or grazing is heavy in a dry winter (K-State trials: 53.5 bu grain-only vs 28.1 bu dual-purpose under simulated heavy grazing, 2024). Budget 5–8 bu/acre as the base-case penalty **verify** against farm experience.
7. Graze-out decision rule at FHS: graze out when (expected additional lb of gain per acre × value of gain) − (extra cattle costs) exceeds (expected grain yield × expected harvest price) − (remaining grain costs: topdress N not yet applied, fungicide, harvest, hauling) − (grain insurance consequences). In an $8 wheat year the grain almost always wins; in a $5 wheat year with $1.00+/lb value of gain the graze-out often wins.
8. Any grazing decision must be reported to the crop insurance agent before cattle are turned in and again before any graze-out; wheat destroyed by grazing without a release is uninsured.

## Fit for this operation
- Soils and fields: wheat pasture works best on well-drained upland silt loams; bottomland fields that stay wet in winter suffer trampling, compaction and stand loss. Fields near the farmstead or with existing fence and water are the only practical candidates; temporary electric fence and hauled or piped water are feasible but add cost and labor.
- Climate: Sedgwick County's fall rainfall (Sep–Nov ~7–9 inches **verify**) normally supports 1,000–2,000 lb/acre of fall dry matter; December–February growth is minimal, so most fall-winter forage is grown by Thanksgiving. Spring growth from late February to early May is rapid and is the basis of graze-out.
- Labor and equipment: daily checking of cattle, water and fence; a stock trailer and working pens or a lease partner who supplies them; mineral/bloat feeders. None are in the farm's current inventory (stub). This is the main feasibility question.
- Rotation: graze-out wheat terminated by early May is an excellent entry for full-season grain sorghum or soybeans planted in May–June (moisture use is the cost), and a workable entry for sunflowers; dual-purpose wheat harvested for grain fits the normal double-crop sequence.
- Disqualifiers: fields with known Hessian fly or wheat streak mosaic history that would need late planting; fields that cannot be fenced; fields with pH below 5.5 (poor fall growth); if no willing cattle partner and no owned cattle.

### Forage production and quality
- Fall dry-matter yield in K-State dual-purpose variety trials ranged 871–1,969 lb/acre; 11 of 16 varieties were in the high group at 1,370–1,969 lb/acre, including KS Ahearn and KS Providence (K-State, Kansas Wheat "Wheat Scoop" summary of MF3312). Year-to-year environmental differences in forage are larger than differences between varieties (K-State; OSU).
- Rule of thumb: about 100 lb of beef per acre per 1,000 lb of wheat forage (K-State). Fall-winter dual-purpose therefore supports roughly 100–200 lb of gain per acre; graze-out through early May typically adds another 100–200 lb/acre for 250–400 lb/acre total **verify** (OSU graze-out studies).
- Wheat forage is 20–30% crude protein and 70–80% digestible, high in potassium and low in magnesium and calcium; it supports 1.5–2.5 lb/day gains (K-State FORA23) and 2.5–3.0 lb/day in good spring grazing (OSU example uses 3 lb/day).

### Stocking rates and animal management
- Fall–winter: 250–600 lb of animal per acre, i.e., 1–2 acres per 450–550 lb stocker, depending on forage (K-State FORA23; MF3375). Spring (graze-out): 500–1,000 lb of animal per acre (K-State ASI). Rotational grazing adds 10–15% gain per acre by reducing spot grazing (K-State).
- Bloat: frothy bloat is the main death-loss risk on lush wheat; provide poloxalene (Bloat Guard) blocks or in mineral, an ionophore where allowed, dry roughage access, and avoid turning hungry cattle onto wet, lush wheat. Budget 1–2% death loss **verify**.
- Mineral: high-Mg, high-Ca mineral free-choice; grass tetany risk is highest with lactating cows, which should not be the primary class on wheat here.
- Health: wheat pasture cattle are usually purchased or received in October–November, the peak respiratory-disease window; a 2–4 week receiving period off the wheat (or a lease partner who handles it) is standard.
- Soil: avoid grazing saturated fields; pull cattle for 3–5 days after heavy rain or snowmelt. Compaction and hoof damage are the usual causes of unexpectedly large grain penalties.

## First hollow stem (FHS) monitoring
- Definition: 5/8 inch (1.5 cm) of hollow stem below the developing head on the largest tillers of ungrazed plants (K-State; OSU). Check plants from an ungrazed strip or outside the fence, split the stem lengthwise, measure.
- Timing in south-central Kansas: typically late February to mid-March; early varieties may reach FHS in mid-February in a warm winter; the Kansas Mesonet FHS tool (Wichita, Hutchinson, Hesston stations) models it from soil temperature and growing degree days; K-State MF3312 lists FHS dates by variety at Hutchinson.
- Yield cost of late removal: 1–5% per day past FHS (K-State MF3312, 2025); older K-State guidance says up to 5% or more per day (MF3375). OSU's extended-grazing study found cattle gains rarely offset the grain lost after FHS at normal price relationships.
- Farm rule: begin checking Feb 10 (Feb 1 in warm winters), check twice weekly, pull cattle within 48 hours of FHS or when the Mesonet model indicates FHS for the variety.

## Economics
### Value of forage, four ways (Oct 2026 prices)
1. Per pound of gain lease: reported wheat pasture rates of $0.60–1.00 per lb of gain (Texas, 2022) and $0.75 per lb of gain in a more recent report of unstated region **verify** for Kansas 2026. At 150 lb/acre of fall–winter gain and $0.75/lb, gross pasture revenue is about $112/acre; at 300 lb/acre with graze-out, about $225/acre.
2. Per head per month lease: K-State worksheet example $2.25 per cwt of animal per month, i.e., $11.25–13.50 per head per month on 500–600 lb cattle, or $45–54 per head for four months; at 1 head per 1.5 acres that is about $30–36/acre **verify** current Kansas rates (the K-State 2023 pasture lease survey and NASS 2026 Kansas pasture rent of $24/acre are for grass, not wheat).
3. Owned cattle, value of gain: value of gain per lb = (sale weight × sale price − purchase weight × purchase price) ÷ lb gained. October 2026 markets: 500–600 lb M&L 1 steers $437.50–475.00/cwt nationally (USDA AMS, 2026-10-10) and $419–455/cwt at Pratt, KS; 600–700 lb steer calves $305–345/cwt at El Dorado, KS (2026-10-15); K-State's outlook priced a 600 lb weaned steer at about $2,226/head (2026-09-24). Example: buy 525 lb at $4.40/cwt = $2,310; sell 725 lb at $3.30/cwt = $2,392; value of gain = $82 ÷ 200 lb = $0.41/lb. The steep price slide on light cattle in Oct 2026 makes the value of gain on owned cattle poor relative to the lease rates above; this is the quantitative reason rule 2 prefers leasing. Re-run with the current slide every fall: when heavy feeders are priced within $0.60/cwt of light calves the value of gain rises above $1.00/lb and ownership becomes attractive **verify**.
4. Substitution value: wheat pasture replacing hay and supplement for a cow herd, valued at the hay it displaces (roughly $1.00–1.50 per head per day at 2026 hay prices **verify**); applicable only if the operator has cows.

### OSU worked example (for calibration)
At 0.64 head/acre, 3 lb/day gain and $7.50/bu wheat, one additional day of grazing past FHS adds about $2.32/acre in calf net returns, and the breakeven grain loss is less than one-third of a bushel per acre per day (OSU Extension). Because K-State measures losses of 1–5% per day (0.4–2.3 bu/day on a 45 bu crop), grazing past FHS fails that test at normal prices.

### Cattle ownership budget skeleton (if the operator chooses to own)
Per head, 500 lb purchase to 700 lb sale, 120 days on wheat: purchase $2,200–2,400 (Oct 2026) + freight, commission, processing and receiving $60–90 + interest at 8–9% for 4 months $60–70 + mineral/bloat/health $25–40 + death loss 1.5% $35 + labor and fence (see above) ≈ $2,400–2,650 total cost vs sale $2,300–2,500 (700 lb at $3.30–3.60/cwt) **verify**; negative to breakeven at the Oct 2026 slide. Price risk tools: LRP (Livestock Risk Protection) insurance, feeder cattle futures/options; forward contracts with a feedyard. The planner must treat an owned-cattle wheat-pasture enterprise as a separate budget with its own adverse/base/favorable cases.

## Crop insurance and program interactions
- Multi-peril (RP/YP) wheat remains insurable as grain when grazed, provided the cattle are removed in time for the crop to make grain; in Kansas the policy does not set a fixed removal date but the insured must not cause damage through grazing (grazing beyond FHS can be treated as an uninsured cause of loss) **verify** with agent and the RMA Small Grains Crop Provisions.
- Wheat intended from planting for graze-out only is not insurable for grain; wheat insured for grain and later grazed out must be released by the adjuster before termination or the acreage is counted as production-to-count/uninsured **verify**. Report intent at acreage reporting (Dec 15 **verify**).
- Hail and private forage insurance: pasture, rangeland, forage (PRF) rainfall index insurance does not cover wheat pasture; private forage policies are uncommon **verify**.
- Cost-share: none specific; NRCS EQIP may share fencing and water for grazing systems **verify**.

## Machinery and labor requirements
- Earlier drilling (Sep 10–25) overlaps sorghum and soybean harvest and raises the peak-labor conflict already noted in the main file; dual-purpose wheat is therefore limited by the drill and crew, realistically 200–400 acres on this farm **verify** against the equipment inventory.
- Cattle: daily checks (10–20 min per group), weekly mineral/bloat block runs, fence repair, water in freezing weather (tank heaters or daily ice breaking). A lease arrangement should assign these to the cattle owner in writing.
- Termination of graze-out wheat: one herbicide pass (glyphosate + dicamba or 2,4-D, or paraquat) in early May, followed by planting sorghum or soybeans no-till.

## Marketing (cattle and pasture)
- Pasture lease forms: per lb of gain (aligns incentives, requires weighing in and out; typical $0.60–1.00/lb in the southern Plains, Kansas 2026 rate **verify**); per head per month ($2–2.50 per cwt per month in K-State examples **verify** current); per acre flat (rare for wheat pasture). Include in the written lease: stocking limits, turn-in/removal dates tied to FHS, who pays mineral/bloat/health, fence and water responsibility, death-loss responsibility, wet-weather removal clause, access and liability insurance.
- Owned cattle: Kansas sale barns (El Dorado, Pratt, Winter Livestock, Wichita-area auctions) and direct-to-feedyard sales; price reporting via USDA AMS Kansas weekly summary; price risk via LRP, CME feeder futures/options.

## Rotation effects
- Graze-out wheat terminated in early May behaves like an early cover crop: 300–600 lb/acre of residue remain, soil water in the top 3 feet is partly depleted (expect a 5–15% yield drag on the following dryland sorghum or soybeans in a dry spring **verify**), N is partly cycled through manure. It is a strong rotational break for wheat diseases and grassy weeds if seed set is prevented.
- Dual-purpose wheat harvested for grain has the same rotation effects as grain wheat (main file), with more compaction risk on wet soils and a slightly later harvest because grazing delays maturity by a few days.
- Manure distribution is uneven (around water and feeders); soil sampling should avoid those zones.

## Breakeven
Landowner, leasing pasture, dual-purpose (per acre, Oct 2026): extra costs $115–200 (table above) require 150–270 lb of gain per acre at $0.75/lb, or 115–200 lb at $1.00/lb, to break even against grain-only wheat. Graze-out: total pasture revenue needed ≈ foregone grain margin (45 bu × $6.40 cash − $45–60 saved harvest/fungicide/hauling ≈ $230–245/acre) plus extra inputs ($70–100) ≈ $300–345/acre, i.e., 300–460 lb of gain per acre at $0.75/lb, which is at or above the realistic range; graze-out therefore pays mainly when wheat is below about $5.50/bu, when the grain crop is already damaged (freeze, thin stand), or when the value of gain exceeds $1.00/lb **verify** all inputs with current quotes.
