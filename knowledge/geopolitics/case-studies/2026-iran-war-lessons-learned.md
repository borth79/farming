# 2026 Iran war: impact on farmers and lessons learned

## Summary
The war that began on 28 February 2026 closed the Strait of Hormuz, which carries
roughly a quarter of world urea exports and a large share of Gulf diesel, LNG, sulfur
and ammonia. For US farmers the shock arrived through inputs, not grain prices: urea
roughly doubled within weeks, diesel rose 40 percent or more, while wheat, corn and
soybean futures rallied briefly and fell back. Farmers who had bought nitrogen and fuel
before March were largely protected for the 2026 crop; those who had not faced the
highest input bills since 2022 at planting time. The event is the clearest recent test
of this project's procurement rules and is the template for the scenario playbook.

## How the shock reached a Kansas farm
| Channel | Mechanism | Lag | Size observed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Urea / UAN | Gulf exporters cut off; NOLA import parity reprices; 30–45 days ship time plus 3–4 weeks inland | days for price, weeks for physical shortage | +45 to +80 percent at peak |
| Anhydrous | US domestic production (incl. Koch, Wichita) kept supply; price followed urea up with a smaller move | weeks | about +20 percent (ammonia) |
| Diesel | Crude plus a wider distillate crack as Gulf and Russian diesel exports fell | days | +40 to +65 percent, persistent |
| Phosphate / sulfur | Gulf sulfur exports feed phosphate production; slower to spike, slower to recover | months | elevated into Q4 2026 |
| Potash | Not Hormuz-dependent (Canada) | — | minimal direct effect |
| Grain prices | Short war-premium rally; no exporter bans; prices stayed near cost of production | days | small and temporary |
| Freight and insurance | War-risk premiums up 600–800 percent; bulk urea voyage insurance from $5–7k to $35–50k | immediate | embedded in delivered cost |
| Credit | Higher operating-line draws at planting; lenders tightened | weeks | reported stress, not quantified here |

## What separated protected from exposed operations
- **Purchase timing.** Growers who prepaid or physically took nitrogen in summer and fall
  2025 were insulated for the 2026 crop. Those buying spring-applied urea or UAN at
  market paid the full spike. This is the single biggest lesson.
- **Product mix.** Anhydrous users fared better than urea or UAN users because domestic
  ammonia supply held and ammonia rose less.
- **Fuel storage and contracts.** Operations with full tanks or fixed-price contracts
  avoided the spring and harvest diesel peaks; those buying at the pump paid 40 percent
  or more extra all season.
- **Crop flexibility.** Analysts projected corn acres shifting to soybeans. Farms with
  rotation flexibility and uncommitted inputs could cut nitrogen demand; farms with
  prepaid corn programs could not and did not need to.
- **Rate discipline.** Growers who recomputed the economic optimum nitrogen rate at the
  new price trimmed cost without proportional yield loss; those who simply skipped
  topdress on wheat took a yield and protein hit.

## Lessons learned for this operation
1. Hormuz is a fertilizer chokepoint first and an oil chokepoint second. Our exposure is
   nitrogen (urea and UAN), diesel, and later phosphate; potash and anhydrous are
   partially insulated.
2. The price response is faster than the physical shortage. Prices moved in days; the
   last pre-war cargo took six to ten weeks to reach farms. Procurement must act on the
   trigger, not on the local quote.
3. Shocks arrive in waves tied to diplomacy. Ceasefires and memoranda produced sharp
   dips (urea from $850 to $453) that were buying windows, followed by renewed spikes
   when agreements failed. Do not treat the first de-escalation as the end.
4. Diesel is stickier than nitrogen. Nitrogen normalized within months; diesel stayed
   65 percent elevated eight months in because global distillate supply was structurally
   reduced. Fuel contracts and storage deserve as much attention as fertilizer.
5. Grain did not bail anyone out. Input shocks do not reliably raise grain prices;
   margins compress. Plans must survive high inputs with flat grain.
6. Layered buying works. The rules in `knowledge/inputs/` (30–50 percent of nitrogen at
   the summer low, 40–60 percent of fuel forward-contracted) would have covered most of
   the 2026 exposure.
7. Anhydrous capability is a hedge. Having the equipment and storage to apply anhydrous
   instead of urea or UAN widens options when import-dependent products spike.
8. Secondary effects matter: sulfur and phosphate lagged by months; war-risk insurance
   and Gulf freight raised delivered costs beyond the headline commodity price.
9. Government response was slow and uncertain. Do not plan on aid; plan on cash.

## Pre-committed responses (feed `scenario-playbook.md`)
- On a credible Hormuz closure signal: within 48 hours fill all fuel storage, lock a
  forward diesel contract for harvest, and buy the remaining nitrogen for the coming
  crop up to the 70 percent prepaid cap.
- On a ceasefire-driven price dip during an unresolved conflict: buy the next layer of
  nitrogen and fuel; do not wait for further declines.
- On diesel sustained above 40 percent over pre-shock levels: re-run the tillage and
  hauling plan to cut passes; reprice custom-hire options.
- On urea above a set threshold relative to wheat price: recompute the wheat topdress
  economic optimum rather than skipping; favor anhydrous where equipment allows.
